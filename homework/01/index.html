§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Homework 1: Practical: Create a toy example of hashing using ecc in particular SECP256K1. And study its statistical properties desirable for cryptography. Research: Modern application of cryptography and statistics to the most famous blockchains

"Hashing with elliptic-curve cryptography" is taken here to mean building a function that maps a message of any length to a digest of fixed length by means of the arithmetic of an elliptic-curve group, in place of the bit-mixing rounds of a dedicated design such as SHA-256. The curve is secp256k1, the curve of Bitcoin, whose domain parameters are fixed by the SEC 2 standard [34]. The one-way map at the heart of elliptic-curve cryptography, from a scalar k to the point k \cdot G, is already a kind of hash: every Bitcoin public key is derived from its private key by it, and an address is then a conventional hash of that point [4]. The toy of §3 makes this observation literal and asks whether a digest built this way behaves as a cryptographic hash should.

A satisfactory answer must establish four things. First, a working construction, implemented from the definitions and checked against published test vectors, so that every number on this page can be regenerated. Second, the properties desirable in a cryptographic hash, translated into hypotheses that can be tested on samples of digests: uniformity of the output, independence of its bits, the avalanche of changes caused by a single input bit, and collision rates that match the birthday bound. Third, experiments with printed seeds and sample sizes, run side by side on the toy hash and on SHA-256, the baseline of a hash designed for the purpose. Fourth, a critical reading of the results: which properties a sample can certify and which it cannot. The research question of the practical part is therefore twofold: does a hash built from secp256k1 arithmetic pass the statistical tests that an ideal hash would pass, and, if it does, is that sufficient for it to be secure? The survey of §6 asks the complementary question of how the most famous blockchains use cryptography and statistics, and where statistics has been turned against them.

§2 Background

2.1 The curve secp256k1 and its group law

Elliptic-curve cryptography was proposed independently by Miller [69] and Koblitz [65] as a way to run discrete-logarithm protocols in a group for which no sub-exponential attack is known. The curve used by Bitcoin is one of those recommended by the Standards for Efficient Cryptography Group, secp256k1, defined over the prime field \mathbb{F}_p by the equation

y^2 = x^3 + 7, \qquad p = 2^{256} - 2^{32} - 977,
(1)

with the base point G and the order n of the group it generates printed in the standard as [34, §2.4.1]:

The cofactor is 1, so G generates the whole group, of prime order n \approx 2^{256}. The letter k in the name marks a Koblitz curve: SEC 2 explains that its parameters were "chosen by repeatedly selecting parameters admitting an efficiently computable endomorphism until a prime order curve was found", as opposed to the verifiably random curves derived from a hashed seed [34, §2.1]. The Bitcoin community reads this as a virtue, arguing that constants chosen in a predictable way leave little room for a hidden weakness and that optimised implementations run faster than on the random curves [18]. The SafeCurves project, which rates curves on the safety of their implementation as well as on the cost of the discrete logarithm, puts the cost of the best attack on secp256k1 at 2^{127.8} operations and yet rates the curve "Safe? False", because it has a small complex-multiplication discriminant and lacks a Montgomery ladder, complete addition formulas and point encodings indistinguishable from random strings [14]; the last three are properties of implementations, not of the group, and none of them affects the use made of the curve here.

The points of the curve, together with a point at infinity O that serves as the identity, form an abelian group under the chord-and-tangent rule of Figure 1. The line through P = (x_1, y_1) and Q = (x_2, y_2) meets the curve in a third point, and P + Q is its reflection in the horizontal axis; writing \lambda for the slope of the line,

\lambda = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, \qquad x_3 = \lambda^2 - x_1 - x_2, \qquad y_3 = \lambda\,(x_1 - x_3) - y_1,
(2)

and the tangent at P, with \lambda = 3x_1^2 / (2y_1), gives 2P by the same two formulas. The negative of (x, y) is (x, -y), and P + (-P) = O. Over the finite field the division in \lambda becomes multiplication by an inverse modulo p, and the geometry disappears, but the formulas, and the group they define, remain the same [33].

Over a finite field the picture loses all geometry: Figure 2 plots every point of the same equation over the field of {{fig.smallCurve.p}} elements. The {{fig.smallCurve.points}} affine points show no structure beyond the mirror symmetry -(x, y) = (x, p - y), and their number, {{fig.smallCurve.order}} with the point at infinity, lies within the interval |\,\#E - (p + 1)\,| \le 2\sqrt{p} given by Hasse's theorem, here between {{fig.smallCurve.hasseLow}} and {{fig.smallCurve.hasseHigh}}. Nothing in the picture betrays the group structure, and that is the point: the order and the position of a point reveal nothing about the scalar that produced it.

2.2 Scalar multiplication and the discrete logarithm

Scalar multiplication k \cdot G is computed by double-and-add: the 256 bits of k are read in turn, the accumulator is doubled at every bit and G is added at every bit equal to one, so that a multiplication costs at most 256 doublings and 256 additions, each a handful of multiplications in \mathbb{F}_p. The implementation behind this page keeps points in Jacobian coordinates, so that one inversion modulo p suffices per multiplication, and for the hash it precomputes the 256 multiples of each generator, so that a digest costs one addition per non-zero byte (§3).

The inverse problem, recovering k from k \cdot G, is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP). No algorithm is known that exploits the structure of a general prime-order curve, so the best attacks are the generic ones: Pollard's rho method [86] finds the logarithm in about \sqrt{n} group operations with negligible memory, van Oorschot and Wiener showed how to distribute it over many machines [96], and Shoup proved that no algorithm that treats the group as a black box can do better than \Omega(\sqrt{n}) operations when n is prime [94]. For secp256k1 this is about 2^{128} operations, the 128-bit security level that the standard assigns to the curve [34, Table 1] and the 2^{127.8} of SafeCurves [14]. The largest instances solved in public give the scale: a 112-bit prime-field curve, secp112r1, was broken in 2009 on a cluster of about 215 PlayStation 3 consoles running from 13 January to 8 July [22], and a 113-bit binary-field curve on a cluster of FPGAs in under two months in 2016 [13]. Each pair of additional bits doubles the cost, so secp256k1 lies about 2^{72} times beyond the prime-field record.

Bitcoin turns this one-way map into its identity system. A private key is an integer k in [1, n - 1]; the public key is the point K = k \cdot G, and recovering k from K is exactly the ECDLP [4]. A legacy address is a hash of the public key, \mathrm{RIPEMD160}(\mathrm{SHA256}(K)), prefixed by a version byte, extended by the first four bytes of a double SHA-256 checksum and written in Base58 [4]; Bitcoin Core names the two compositions "double SHA-256" and "SHA-256 + RIPEMD-160" and uses the first for block headers and transaction identifiers as well [15]. Everything after the point multiplication is a conventional hash; the point multiplication itself is the one-way step. This is the sense in which secp256k1 arithmetic is already a hash in production, and the scalar variant of §3 is nothing more than this step applied to a message instead of a key.

2.3 What a cryptographic hash must provide, and how statistics can test it

A cryptographic hash function h maps inputs of arbitrary length to outputs of a fixed length \ell, here 256 bits, and is expected to resist three kinds of attack, in the classical formulation of Menezes, van Oorschot and Vanstone [68, §9.2]: preimage resistance, so that for essentially any output y it is infeasible to find an input with h(x) = y; second-preimage resistance, so that given x it is infeasible to find x' \ne x with h(x') = h(x); and collision resistance, so that it is infeasible to find any two distinct inputs with the same digest. Rogaway and Shrimpton refined these into seven notions and settled which implies which: collision resistance implies second-preimage resistance, but implies preimage resistance only provisionally, to a degree that depends on how much the function compresses [89]. Against a generic adversary an ideal \ell-bit hash resists preimage and second-preimage search up to about 2^{\ell} evaluations and collision search up to about 2^{\ell/2}, the birthday bound [68, Fact 9.33]; for 256 bits these are 2^{256} and 2^{128}, the latter equal to the cost of the discrete logarithm on the curve.

The strongest idealisation is the random oracle of Bellare and Rogaway: a public function that answers every new query with a uniformly random string and repeats its answer on a repeated query [9]. Protocols are proved secure with the oracle and then instantiated with a concrete hash, on the understanding that the hash should be indistinguishable from the oracle by any feasible test. This is the bridge to statistics. If a hash behaved as a random oracle, the digest of a fresh random message would be a uniformly random 256-bit string, independent of the message and of every other digest; equivalently, its bits would be independent and identically distributed Bernoulli variables with parameter one half. That statement is the null hypothesis H_0 of §4, and each desirable property becomes a consequence of it with a known sampling distribution.

  • Uniformity. The count of ones at a fixed bit position over n digests is \mathrm{Bin}(n, \tfrac12), and the pooled count over all bits is the subject of the frequency, or monobit, test of NIST Special Publication 800-22 [91, §2.1]; the 256 byte values are equally likely, which Pearson's chi-square statistic tests on 255 degrees of freedom; and the number of ones in a digest is \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12).
  • Independence. The number of runs of identical bits in a digest follows the law tested by the runs test [91, §2.3], and adjacent bits are uncorrelated.
  • Avalanche. Complementing one input bit should change "an average of one half of the output bits"; Webster and Tavares, who attribute the idea to Feistel, sharpened it into the strict avalanche criterion, that every output bit changes with probability exactly one half whenever any single input bit is complemented, and the bit independence criterion, that these changes are pairwise independent [99]. Under H_0 the digests of two messages that differ in one bit are independent, so their Hamming distance is \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12) and every cell of the input-bit by output-bit matrix of flip probabilities equals one half.
  • Collisions. Among m digests truncated to t bits the number of colliding pairs has mean \binom{m}{2} / 2^{t}, and the probability of at least one repeat is 1 - \prod_{i < m} (1 - i / 2^{t}), which passes one half when m is about 1.18 \sqrt{2^{t}} [68, Fact 2.27].

These are the tests of §4. Larger batteries exist, TestU01 among them [66], but the NIST tests have the merit that each comes with an explicit null distribution, so that a p-value can be read as the probability, under H_0, of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. What such tests can establish is bounded by the authors of SP 800-22 themselves: "no set of statistical tests can absolutely certify a generator as appropriate for usage in a particular application, i.e., statistical testing cannot serve as a substitute for cryptanalysis", and "no specific finite set of tests is deemed 'complete'" [91]. The toy hash of §3 is built to make this caveat concrete.

§3 Construction of the toy hash

The toy hash reads a message as a sequence of bytes b_0, b_1, \dots, b_{L-1}, assigns a curve point G_i to every byte position, and returns the x-coordinate of the point

H(m) = x\Big( \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} b_i \cdot G_i \Big) \in \{0, 1\}^{256},
(3)

written as 32 big-endian bytes; the point at infinity, reached only by the all-zero message, is mapped to the all-zero digest. Two families of generators share this single definition and are compared throughout.

  • Scalar variant. G_i = 2^{8i} \cdot G, so that the sum collapses to k \cdot G with k the message read as a little-endian integer. The digest is the x-coordinate of the public key of private key k: exactly the map Bitcoin applies to derive keys, used here as a hash.
  • Pedersen variant. G_i are independent "nothing-up-my-sleeve" points obtained by hashing the index i to the curve with SHA-256 and try-and-increment, so that no discrete-logarithm relation between them is known. This is the hash of Pedersen with 8-bit chunks; a collision would reveal a linear relation among the generators and hence a discrete logarithm [85] [61, §4.1.8].

Every experiment also runs on SHA-256, implemented from the standard in the same code base, as the baseline of a hash designed for the purpose. The implementation keeps, for each byte position, a table of the 256 multiples 0 \cdot G_i, \dots, 255 \cdot G_i, so that a digest costs one point addition per non-zero byte and one field inversion. All arithmetic is written from the definitions in the project's own modules, linked in the appendix.

§4 Statistical study

Every test below is a test of the same null hypothesis: the digest of a random message is a uniformly random 256-bit string, independent of the message and of the other digests. Messages are 32 random bytes from a seeded generator, so that every figure is reproducible from the seed it prints. A pool of {{fig.params.n}} messages (seed {{fig.params.seedPool}}) feeds the frequency, weight, runs and collision tests; the avalanche test uses {{fig.params.avalanchePairs}} message pairs (seed {{fig.params.seedAvalanche}}); the strict-avalanche matrix uses {{fig.params.sacTrials}} messages per input bit, that is {{fig.params.sacHashes}} digests (seed {{fig.params.seedSac}}). Each figure can be switched between the two variants of the toy hash and SHA-256, and redrawn in the browser on a fresh sample of reduced size.

4.1 Uniformity of the output bits

For each output bit position j, the number of ones c_j over n digests is binomial \mathrm{Bin}(n, \tfrac12) under the null hypothesis. The standardised counts z_j = (c_j - n/2) / \sqrt{n/4} are then approximately standard normal, and their sum of squares is approximately \chi^2 with 256 degrees of freedom. Figure 4 plots the 256 values of z_j on the normal probability paper of this site, where a sample of standard normal variates lies on the line of slope one through the origin, and below them as a strip in bit order.

4.2 Uniformity of the output bytes

The {{fig.byteFrequency.scalar.bytes}} bytes of the pooled digests should be spread evenly over the 256 byte values, {{fig.byteFrequency.scalar.expected}} each. Pearson's statistic \sum_v (O_v - E)^2 / E is approximately \chi^2 with 255 degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis.

4.3 Hamming weight of a digest

The number of ones in a digest of 256 independent fair bits is \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12), with mean 128 and standard deviation 8. The histogram of the weights is compared with that law by a chi-square test after merging the tails into bins of expected count at least five.

4.4 Avalanche effect and the strict avalanche criterion

A hash should change about half of its output bits when a single input bit changes, and no output bit should be predictable from the flipped input bit. Figure 7 records, for {{fig.avalanche.scalar.n}} random messages, the Hamming distance between the digests of the message and of the message with one random bit flipped; under the null hypothesis the distance is again \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12). Figure 8 refines the question to every pair of input and output bits: the strict avalanche criterion requires that output bit j flip with probability one half whenever input bit i is flipped, for all i and j [99].

4.5 Independence within a digest

Uniform bits may still be dependent. The runs test of NIST SP 800-22 counts the maximal blocks of identical bits in each 256-bit digest: too few runs betray clustering, too many an oscillation. Each digest yields a p-value. For long sequences these p-values are uniform on [0, 1] under the null hypothesis, but for a 256-bit string the number of runs takes few distinct values, so the p-value is a discrete variable whose exact law can be computed by counting the strings with k ones and r runs, 2\binom{k-1}{m-1}\binom{n-k-1}{m-1} for r = 2m and the analogous sum for odd r, each with probability 2^{-n}. Figure 9 compares the histogram of p-values over ten bins with that exact law by a chi-square test; a flat reference would wrongly reject even SHA-256. The proportion of digests with p \ge 0.01 is compared with the interval 0.99 \pm 3\sqrt{0.0099 / n} used by the NIST suite. The same section tests adjacent bits directly: the 2 × 2 table of pairs (b_j, b_{j+1}) pooled over all digests, with its chi-square statistic on one degree of freedom and the lag-one correlation [91, §2.3].

4.6 Collisions and the birthday bound

Collisions of a 256-bit digest cannot be observed directly, so the digests are truncated to their leading t bits. Among n uniform values on t bits the expected number of colliding pairs is \binom{n}{2} / 2^{t}, and the probability that a sample of m values contains a repeat is 1 - \prod_{i < m} (1 - i / 2^{t}), which passes one half near m \approx 1.18 \sqrt{2^{t}} [68, Fact 2.27]. 4.7 Preimage resistance and the cost of the discrete logarithm

Inverting the toy hash means recovering the scalar from the point, the discrete logarithm problem. It cannot be observed on secp256k1, so it is measured where it can be solved: on the curves y^2 = x^3 + 7 over the {{fig.ecdlp.curves}} primes nearest to 2^7, \dots, 2^{14}, with a random base point and {{fig.ecdlp.challenges}} random challenges each. Exhaustive search costs about half the group order; Shanks's baby-step giant-step costs about 2\sqrt{n} operations and \sqrt{n} memory, and Pollard's rho the same time with no memory [86], which no generic algorithm can beat [94]. §5 What statistics cannot see

The scalar variant passes every test of §4 as convincingly as SHA-256, and yet it is broken as a hash function. Its digest is the x-coordinate of k \cdot G, and two facts about the group give second preimages for free. First, (n - k) \cdot G = -(k \cdot G) has the same x-coordinate, so the message encoding n - k collides with the message encoding k. Second, n \cdot G = O, so the message encoding k + n collides as well. For the worked example of §3.1 the two colliding messages are

Negation message, 32 bytes, digest {{fig.demo.negationEqual}}{{fig.demo.negationHex}}
Wrap message, 33 bytes, digest {{fig.demo.wrapEqual}}{{fig.demo.wrapHex}}

Both variants are also additively homomorphic: the point of the byte-wise sum of two messages is the sum of their points, so a digest leaks relations between messages that a random oracle would hide. None of these properties leaves a statistical trace in random samples, because the colliding messages and the related messages are vanishingly rare among random inputs; they are found by algebra, not by sampling. This is the central lesson of the exercise: statistical tests are necessary, since a hash that fails them is certainly unfit, but they are never sufficient, since security is a statement about every adversary and not about the average input; the NIST test suite says as much of itself [91].

The Pedersen variant repairs collision resistance, and the argument is short. A collision \sum_i b_i G_i = \sum_i b_i' G_i between two distinct messages of the same length gives \sum_i (b_i - b_i') G_i = O, a non-trivial linear relation among generators whose mutual discrete logarithms are unknown; an adversary who finds collisions can therefore compute discrete logarithms, which is the argument of Pedersen for the binding of his commitment [85], of Chaum, van Heijst and Pfitzmann for the first collision-free hash based on the discrete logarithm [35], and of Bellare and Micciancio for their incremental hash MuHASH, with a loss of a factor two [10]; the Zcash specification states it in one line, a collision "trivially gives a discrete logarithm relation" [61, §4.1.8]. The reduction holds for inputs of a fixed length and requires generators derived without a known relation, hence the hash-to-curve step; RFC 9380 declines to specify the try-and-increment method used here and marks it "NOT RECOMMENDED", because its running time depends on the input and such methods "have been a perennial cause of side-channel vulnerabilities" [52, §1]; it is used knowingly, on public inputs, for a toy. What the reduction does not give is pseudorandomness. The construction remains additively homomorphic, a property Bellare and Micciancio present as the feature of incrementality; they prove collision-freeness alone, in the random-oracle model for the inner function, and warn that this is not standard provable security [10]. The halo2 book makes the same point about Zcash's Sinsemilla, which "is not designed to be used where a random oracle, PRF, or preimage-resistant hash is required" [104], and Bottinelli catalogues the practical pitfalls of Pedersen hashes, among them keeping only the x-coordinate, which identifies P with -P [23]: here that cannot be exploited, because negative byte values are not in the domain, but it halves the output space to (n - 1)/2 values and so lowers the birthday bound to about 2^{127.5}. ECOH, a SHA-3 candidate built entirely on elliptic-curve arithmetic [27], fell to a second-preimage attack costing 2^{143} operations for its 256-bit version, far below the 2^{256} of an ideal hash [60]: algebraic structure is a liability for a general-purpose hash. The production descendants of the toy are the Pedersen hash of Zcash Sapling, on the Jubjub curve with 3-bit windows, and Sinsemilla, on the Pallas curve with 10-bit chunks [61, §5.4.1.7, §5.4.1.9], both specified as collision-resistant for fixed-length inputs and for nothing else (§6.3).

The cost is the other difference. In the same environment the toy hash runs at about {{fig.speed.scalar}} digests per second for the scalar variant and {{fig.speed.pedersen}} for the Pedersen variant, against {{fig.speed.sha256}} for SHA-256, a factor of about {{fig.speed.ratio}}. A digest of a 32-byte message costs up to 32 point additions and one inversion on 256-bit integers, each addition about a dozen modular multiplications of 256-bit numbers, where SHA-256 processes the same message with 64 rounds of additions, rotations and logical operations on 32-bit words. The gap is intrinsic to the arithmetic, not to the implementation, and it is the reason the algebraic hashes of §6.3 are used only where their homomorphic structure buys something, inside zero-knowledge circuits.

§6 Cryptography and statistics in the most famous blockchains

Cryptography gives a blockchain its objects, hashes, signatures, commitments and proofs, and statistics gives it its security arguments: proof of work is a race between Poisson processes, proof of stake is a lottery, the privacy of a coin is judged by how well its decoys imitate real spending, and the failures that have cost real money have often been failures of randomness. This section surveys the systems most often described as the most famous, Bitcoin and Ethereum at length and then Zcash, Monero, Algorand, Cardano and Solana, and closes with three themes that cut across them. Facts are taken from the specifications, papers and announcements listed in the references; dates are those of mainnet activations as recorded there, and estimates that differ between sources are reported with both values.

6.1 Bitcoin

Bitcoin's cryptography is a small set of primitives used with discipline. Transactions are authorised by ECDSA signatures on secp256k1 [4]; since the Taproot upgrade, which activated at block height 709,632 on 14 November 2021 after a signalling period with a 90% miner threshold [101], outputs can also be spent with the Schnorr signatures of BIP 340, 64-byte signatures over 32-byte x-only public keys whose linearity makes them easy to combine [100]. On that linearity rest MuSig2, a two-round multi-signature whose output is a single ordinary BIP 340 signature [77], and FROST, a two-round threshold Schnorr scheme standardised by the IRTF in 2024 with a secp256k1 ciphersuite [38]. Hashing is SHA-256 applied twice, "double SHA-256", for block headers, transaction identifiers and the Merkle tree that commits to the transactions of a block, and SHA-256 followed by RIPEMD-160 for addresses [15, 17].

The statistical heart of the protocol is proof of work. A header is valid when its double SHA-256 hash, read as a 256-bit integer, is at or below a target encoded in the header [17]. Each hash attempt succeeds independently with the same small probability, so block discovery is, to a first approximation, a Poisson process whose rate is the network hash rate divided by the difficulty, and the times between blocks are exponential. Every 2016 blocks the target is rescaled by the ratio of the time the last 2016 blocks actually took to the two weeks they should have taken at one block per 600 seconds, the ratio being clamped to the interval from one quarter to four [15, 16]. Bowden and co-authors examined the arrival data and showed that a homogeneous Poisson process does not fit them, and proposed a refined model that accounts for the periods of constant difficulty and for the evolution of the difficulty over time [24].

The race analysed in the original paper is the clearest example of statistics as a security argument [76, §11]: an attacker holding a fraction q of the hash rate, trying to replace a transaction buried under z blocks, progresses as a Poisson variable with mean \lambda = z\,q/p while the honest chain grows, and catches up from a deficit of d blocks with probability (q/p)^d, as in the gambler's ruin problem, giving

P = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{z} \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \Big( 1 - (q/p)^{z-k} \Big), \qquad p = 1 - q.

Rosenfeld replaced the Poisson approximation by the exact negative-binomial law of the attacker's progress and found the original table slightly optimistic: for q = 10% the probability of success drops below 0.1% at six confirmations rather than five [90]; Grunspan and Pérez-Marco obtained a closed form through the regularised incomplete beta function and proved that the probability decays exponentially in the number of confirmations [59]. Honest majority is not the only assumption at stake. Eyal and Sirer showed that a miner who withholds blocks strategically earns more than its fair share as soon as its share \alpha of the hash rate exceeds (1 - \gamma)/(3 - 2\gamma), where \gamma is the fraction of honest miners that build on the attacker's block in a tie; the threshold is one third for \gamma = 0, vanishes for \gamma = 1, and random tie-breaking restores it to one quarter [51].

Statistics has also been turned against Bitcoin's signatures. ECDSA needs a fresh, uniformly random nonce for every signature: a repeated nonce reveals the private key outright, and a merely biased one reveals it given enough signatures. In August 2013 Bitcoin.org warned that the Android component responsible for secure random numbers "contains critical weaknesses" that rendered all Android wallets generated to date vulnerable to theft, and asked users to rotate their keys [19]; Google traced the flaw to improper initialisation of the platform's pseudorandom generator [64]. Brengel and Rossow later scanned the blockchain for repeated nonces and found that attackers had exploited them to take 412.80 BTC [26], and Breitner and Heninger, with lattice algorithms for the hidden number problem, computed hundreds of Bitcoin private keys from signatures whose nonces were merely non-uniform [25]. The standard remedy is to derive the nonce deterministically from the key and the message, as RFC 6979 specifies, since "even slight biases in that process may be turned into attacks on the signature schemes" [87]; BIP 340 goes further with synthetic nonces that combine the deterministic derivation with auxiliary randomness [100].

Statistics is, finally, the instrument of forensic analysis. Meiklejohn and co-authors clustered 12,056,684 public keys into 3,384,179 clusters with two heuristics, that the inputs of a transaction are controlled by one user and that change addresses can be recognised, and attached names to 2,197 clusters by transacting with services [67]. Vičič and Tošić tested the first digits of transaction values against Benford's law and found that the aggregated values of the well-known projects conform to it, while most of the non-conforming coins had documented anomalous incidents [98].

6.2 Ethereum

Ethereum shares Bitcoin's curve but not its hash: an account is identified by the last 20 bytes of the Keccak-256 hash of the secp256k1 public key [48]. Keccak-256 is the function submitted to the SHA-3 competition; the standardised SHA3-256 of FIPS 202 differs from it only by a two-bit domain-separation suffix appended to the message before padding [78, §6.1], which is why the two give different digests for the same input. Since the Merge, executed on 15 September 2022, Ethereum has been secured by proof of stake [49]: validators who have deposited stake are chosen to propose and attest blocks under Gasper, which combines the finality gadget Casper FFG with the fork-choice rule LMD GHOST [31]. Attestations are BLS signatures [20] on the pairing-friendly curve BLS12-381 [43]; because BLS signatures can be aggregated, the thousands of attestations of a slot are verified as one.

Proof of stake replaces a race by a lottery, and a lottery needs randomness that no participant can steer. Ethereum's source is RANDAO: each proposer reveals a BLS signature over the epoch number, which is deterministic and therefore unpredictable only to others, and the beacon state mixes its hash into a running value by exclusive or [43]. A proposer in the last slot of an epoch can withhold its block and thereby choose between two mixes, "one bit of influence"; k consecutive end-of-epoch proposers choose among 2^k [41]. Alpturer and Weinberg computed the optimal strategy and found the gain small: a strategic participant with 5% of the stake can propose 5.048% of the blocks, and one with 20% can propose 20.68% [3]. The randomness drives the committee shuffle: validators are assigned to committees of target size 128 by a swap-or-not shuffle, a size chosen so that the probability of an adversary holding a third of the stake capturing two thirds of a committee, a binomial tail, falls below 2^{-40} [41, 43].

Data availability is the other place where Ethereum reasons probabilistically. EIP-4844, activated in the Dencun upgrade on 13 March 2024 [45], introduced blob transactions: 4096 field elements of 32 bytes each, committed with the polynomial commitments of Kate, Zaverucha and Goldberg [30, 62], whose trusted setup was produced by a ceremony of 141,416 contributions over 208 days and is secure as long as a single participant was honest [44]. Blobs are meant to be sampled, not downloaded. Al-Bassam, Sonnino and Buterin showed that if a block of k \times k shares is extended by a Reed–Solomon code to 2k \times 2k, withholding it requires hiding at least (k + 1)^2 shares, so that a client sampling s shares at random misses the withholding with probability at most about (3/4)^s [1]. PeerDAS, specified in EIP-7594 and activated in the Fusaka upgrade on 3 December 2025, implements this with nodes that each custody a few of 128 columns and sample eight per slot, so that with ten thousand nodes the probability that a withheld blob is accepted as available by even 2% of them is bounded by about 10^{-20} [46, 92].

Much of Ethereum's computation now happens in rollups that post succinct proofs: Groth16, a pairing-based SNARK with three-element proofs [58]; PLONK, a universal SNARK with one setup for all circuits [53]; and STARKs, which need no trusted setup and rest on the FRI proximity test for Reed–Solomon codes [11, 12]. The same concern now shapes the state tree. Verkle trees, vector-commitment trees with very short proofs, were the planned replacement for the hexary Merkle–Patricia trie, but EIP-7864 proposes instead a binary tree over a hash function, because the Verkle design "relies on elliptic curves that aren't post-quantum secure" [29]; the Foundation's post-quantum roadmap plans to replace BLS validator signatures by the hash-based leanXMSS and states that the layer-1 upgrades could be completed by 2029 [47, 50].

6.3 Zcash

Zcash is the closest relative of the toy of §3. Its shielded transactions prove, in zero knowledge, that a spend is valid without revealing sender, receiver or amount. Sapling, activated at block 419,200 on 29 October 2018, used Groth16 proofs over BLS12-381 [61, 103, 105]; NU5, activated at height 1,687,104 on 31 May 2022, introduced the Orchard protocol with the Halo 2 proving system, which needs no trusted setup [42, 106]. Inside those circuits ordinary hash functions are expensive, so Zcash uses algebraic ones. The Sapling Pedersen hash is a sum of points of the Jubjub curve indexed by 3-bit windows of the message, "an algebraic hash function with collision resistance (for fixed input length) derived from assumed hardness of the Discrete Logarithm Problem on the Jubjub curve", built on the work of Chaum, van Heijst and Pfitzmann among others [61, §5.4.1.7]; Orchard's Sinsemilla does the same on the Pallas curve with 10-bit chunks drawn from a table of 1024 precomputed generators, so as to exploit the lookup arguments of Halo 2 [61, §5.4.1.9]. The specification names the only property claimed, collision resistance between inputs of fixed length, and adds that "no other security properties commonly associated with hash functions are needed"; the halo2 book is blunter: Sinsemilla "is not designed to be used where a random oracle, PRF, or preimage-resistant hash is required" [104]. The Pedersen variant of §3 is this construction with 8-bit chunks on secp256k1, and §5 is the reason for the caveat.

6.4 Monero

Monero hides the sender with ring signatures: an input is signed by one of a ring of possible outputs, the real one and a number of decoys, and a key image prevents double spending without revealing which member signed. The scheme descends from the one-time ring signatures of the CryptoNote protocol [97]; amounts were hidden from January 2017 by Ring Confidential Transactions, which embed Pedersen commitments in the ring signature [83], with range proofs given since October 2018 by Bulletproofs, whose size grows only logarithmically in the number of bits proved [28, 70]. The upgrade of August 2022, at block 2,688,888, adopted the shorter Bulletproofs+ and raised the ring size from 11 to 16 [37, 71], and the ring signature in use is CLSAG, a concise linkable ring signature with smaller signatures than its predecessor [57].

Whether the decoys hide anything is a statistical question, and it was answered empirically. Möser and co-authors found that about 62% of the inputs with one or more decoys before RingCT could be traced by "chain-reaction" elimination, because decoys provably spent elsewhere could be excluded, and that the real input could be guessed with 80% accuracy as simply the newest member of the ring, since decoys were drawn from a distribution unlike that of real spending [75]. They fitted the age of spent outputs, on a logarithmic time scale, with a gamma distribution of shape 19.28 and rate 1.61 and proposed sampling decoys from it; Monero adopted the gamma sampler in version 0.13.0 in October 2018 [70], and a post-mortem published in 2023 documented an off-by-one error that had prevented the sampler from ever choosing an output exactly ten blocks old, a statistical fingerprint in its own right [72]. The next step, Full-Chain Membership Proofs, replaces the ring of 16 by a proof that the spent output is any output on the chain [84]; as of October 2026 it is being exercised on a beta stress-test network, with no mainnet date announced [73].

6.5 Algorand and Cardano

Proof-of-stake protocols that elect leaders privately rely on verifiable random functions, "the public key version of a keyed cryptographic hash" in the words of RFC 9381: the holder of a secret key evaluates a pseudorandom function on an input and produces a proof that anyone can check against the public key [56]. Algorand selects its committees by cryptographic sortition. Every unit of stake is a sub-user selected with probability p = \tau / W, where \tau is the expected committee size and W the total stake, and a user who owns w units learns from the VRF output how many of them were selected, a number distributed as B(k; w, p), the binomial law; because the binomial law is additive in its weight, splitting stake among Sybil identities does not change the expected number of seats [55]. Cardano's Ouroboros, the first proof-of-stake protocol with a rigorous security proof [63], became Ouroboros Praos, in which each slot's leader is decided privately by comparing a VRF output with a threshold: a stakeholder with relative stake \alpha leads a slot with probability \varphi_f(\alpha) = 1 - (1 - f)^{\alpha}, where f is the active slots coefficient, and this function has the "independent aggregation" property 1 - \varphi(\sum_i \alpha_i) = \prod_i (1 - \varphi(\alpha_i)), so that the chance of election does not depend on whether stake is held as one party or as many [39]. On the Cardano mainnet f = 0.05 with one-second slots [32]: a slot is active with probability one twentieth, one block every twenty seconds on average, and a pool with stake \alpha is elected with probability 1 - 0.95^{\alpha} per slot.

6.6 Solana

Solana's distinctive primitive is Proof of History, a clock made of hashing: a single core runs SHA-256 in sequence, each output the next input, recording the output and the count at intervals, so that the length of the chain is a verifiable lower bound on the time elapsed and the verification can be run in parallel [102]. It is a delay function in the informal sense, built from a sequential hash rather than from the algebraic constructions of §6.7, and the order it imposes on events is used before consensus is reached. Votes are cast under Tower BFT, in which a validator's successive votes carry exponentially growing lockouts, so that reverting an old vote becomes prohibitively expensive [5]; transactions are signed with Ed25519, 64 bytes per signer [95].

6.7 Cross-cutting themes

Randomness as a shared resource. Leader election, committee sampling and the lotteries of §6.5 are fair only if their seed cannot be biased. Commit-and-reveal schemes such as RANDAO leave the last revealer a choice, quantified above; two constructions remove it. In a threshold beacon such as drand, n nodes jointly hold a BLS key on BLS12-381, with threshold t = f + 1 for at most f faulty nodes, and sign each round number; because a BLS signature is unique, the output of a round is fixed once t shares exist and no smaller group can predict it [40]. A verifiable delay function, as defined by Boneh, Bonneau, Bünz and Fisch, needs a prescribed number of sequential steps to evaluate and yet yields a unique output that is quickly verifiable, so that a seed committed before the delay cannot be adjusted to its outcome [21]. Hashing to a curve, which the toy of §3 did by trial and error, is standardised for these systems: RFC 9380 specifies constant-time maps for BLS12-381 and secp256k1 and marks try-and-increment as not recommended [52].

The post-quantum transition. Every signature in this survey, ECDSA and Schnorr on secp256k1, Ed25519, BLS and the VRFs, rests on a discrete logarithm, and Shor's algorithm computes discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a quantum computer [93]. NIST published the first post-quantum standards on 13 August 2024, ML-KEM for key establishment and ML-DSA and SLH-DSA for signatures [79, 80, 81], selected the code-based HQC for a further encryption standard in March 2025 [82], and in a draft transition plan proposed that ECDSA, EdDSA and RSA be deprecated after 2030 at 112 bits of security and disallowed after 2035 at every strength [74]. The resource estimates have fallen quickly. Roetteler and co-authors put Shor's algorithm for a 256-bit curve at 2,330 logical qubits in 2017 [88]; Chevignard, Fouque and Schrottenloher reduced the count to 1,193 in 2026 [36]; and Babbush and co-authors, in a paper by Google Quantum AI, the Ethereum Foundation and Stanford, estimated in March 2026 that the 256-bit ECDLP runs with fewer than 1,200 logical qubits and 90 million Toffoli gates, on fewer than half a million physical qubits at an error rate of one in a thousand, in about nine minutes [6]. By comparison, Gidney's 2025 estimate for RSA-2048 is under a week on fewer than a million noisy qubits [54]. The exposure is measurable on chain: about 1.7 million bitcoin lie in outputs that publish their public key directly, and about 6.9 million in all once reused addresses are counted [6], where an earlier Deloitte analysis had counted over 4 million, about a quarter of the supply [7]. Bitcoin's response is BIP 360, a draft soft fork for a new output type, Pay-to-Merkle-Root, with the post-quantum signature algorithms themselves deferred to a later proposal [8]; Ethereum's is the roadmap of §6.2 [47]; Algorand already signs its state proofs with the lattice-based Falcon scheme [2].

Statistics as a weapon. The thread that runs through this survey is that the quality of randomness is at once a security assumption and an attack surface. The Android generator of 2013 and the biased nonces of 2019 broke signatures that were mathematically sound; Monero's decoys were defeated by an age distribution; RANDAO's bias is a few hundredths of a percentage point that an adversary can nevertheless compute and collect; and the toy hash of §3 passes every statistical test while admitting collisions by algebra. The tests of §4 are therefore necessary and never sufficient, exactly as the NIST suite says of itself [91], and the designers of the systems above have increasingly replaced "random enough" by "unique and verifiable": deterministic nonces, threshold signatures, verifiable random functions and delay functions.

§7 Conclusion

The question of §1 has two answers. Statistically, a hash built from secp256k1 arithmetic is indistinguishable, in these experiments, from an ideal hash. Over {{fig.params.n}} digests its bit frequencies, byte frequencies, Hamming weights, runs and adjacent-bit correlations agree with the hypothesis of independent fair bits as closely as those of SHA-256; one flipped input bit changes {{fig.avalanche.scalar.mean}} output bits on average, against 128 expected; every cell of the strict-avalanche matrix is consistent with one half; and truncated digests collide at the birthday rate. The 21 p-values of Table 2 contain a single value below 0.05, which Table 3 shows not to replicate. Cryptographically, the scalar variant is nonetheless broken by construction: the negation and wrap-around symmetries of the group give a second preimage for every message at no cost, and the additive homomorphism leaks relations between messages. The Pedersen variant repairs collision resistance by a reduction to the discrete logarithm but remains homomorphic, which is why its production descendants in Zcash are specified for collision resistance alone.

Statistical tests are thus necessary, since a function that fails them is unfit, but never sufficient, since they sample the average input and security is a statement about the input an adversary chooses. The same lesson organises the survey: the blockchains use statistics to argue security, from Nakamoto's Poisson race to Algorand's binomial sortition and Ethereum's sampled availability, and they have been attacked through statistics, from biased nonces to distinguishable decoys. The direction of the last decade is to replace randomness that must be trusted by randomness that can be verified, and signatures whose security rests on the discrete logarithm by signatures that will survive a quantum computer.
