§1 Problem statement

The assignment asks for two things. The practical part is to build a toy example of hashing with elliptic-curve cryptography on the curve secp256k1 and to study the statistical properties that a cryptographic hash function is expected to have. The research part is a survey of how cryptography and statistics are applied in the most famous blockchains. [ref]

§3 Construction of the toy hash

The toy hash reads a message as a sequence of bytes b_0, b_1, \dots, b_{L-1}, assigns a curve point G_i to every byte position, and returns the x-coordinate of the point

H(m) = x\Big( \sum_{i=0}^{L-1} b_i \cdot G_i \Big) \in \{0, 1\}^{256},
(2)

written as 32 big-endian bytes; the point at infinity, reached only by the all-zero message, is mapped to the all-zero digest. Two families of generators share this single definition and are compared throughout.

  • Scalar variant. G_i = 2^{8i} \cdot G, so that the sum collapses to k \cdot G with k the message read as a little-endian integer. The digest is the x-coordinate of the public key of private key k: exactly the map Bitcoin applies to derive keys, used here as a hash.
  • Pedersen variant. G_i are independent "nothing-up-my-sleeve" points obtained by hashing the index i to the curve with SHA-256 and try-and-increment, so that no discrete-logarithm relation between them is known. This is the hash of Pedersen with 8-bit chunks; a collision would reveal a linear relation among the generators and hence a discrete logarithm. [ref]

Every experiment also runs on SHA-256, implemented from the standard in the same code base, as the baseline of a hash designed for the purpose. The implementation keeps, for each byte position, a table of the 256 multiples 0 \cdot G_i, \dots, 255 \cdot G_i, so that a digest costs one point addition per non-zero byte and one field inversion. All arithmetic is written from the definitions in the project's own modules, linked in the appendix.

§4 Statistical study

Every test below is a test of the same null hypothesis: the digest of a random message is a uniformly random 256-bit string, independent of the message and of the other digests. Messages are 32 random bytes from a seeded generator, so that every figure is reproducible from the seed it prints. A pool of {{fig.params.n}} messages (seed {{fig.params.seedPool}}) feeds the frequency, weight, runs and collision tests; the avalanche test uses {{fig.params.avalanchePairs}} message pairs (seed {{fig.params.seedAvalanche}}); the strict-avalanche matrix uses {{fig.params.sacTrials}} messages per input bit, that is {{fig.params.sacHashes}} digests (seed {{fig.params.seedSac}}). Each figure can be switched between the two variants of the toy hash and SHA-256, and redrawn in the browser on a fresh sample of reduced size.

4.1 Uniformity of the output bits

For each output bit position j, the number of ones c_j over n digests is binomial \mathrm{Bin}(n, \tfrac12) under the null hypothesis. The standardised counts z_j = (c_j - n/2) / \sqrt{n/4} are then approximately standard normal, and their sum of squares is approximately \chi^2 with 256 degrees of freedom. Figure 4 plots the 256 values of z_j on the normal probability paper of this site, where a sample of standard normal variates lies on the line of slope one through the origin, and below them as a strip in bit order.

4.2 Uniformity of the output bytes

The {{fig.byteFrequency.scalar.bytes}} bytes of the pooled digests should be spread evenly over the 256 byte values, {{fig.byteFrequency.scalar.expected}} each. Pearson's statistic \sum_v (O_v - E)^2 / E is approximately \chi^2 with 255 degrees of freedom under the null hypothesis.

4.3 Hamming weight of a digest

The number of ones in a digest of 256 independent fair bits is \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12), with mean 128 and standard deviation 8. The histogram of the weights is compared with that law by a chi-square test after merging the tails into bins of expected count at least five.

4.4 Avalanche effect and the strict avalanche criterion

A hash should change about half of its output bits when a single input bit changes, and no output bit should be predictable from the flipped input bit. Figure 7 records, for {{fig.avalanche.scalar.n}} random messages, the Hamming distance between the digests of the message and of the message with one random bit flipped; under the null hypothesis the distance is again \mathrm{Bin}(256, \tfrac12). Figure 8 refines the question to every pair of input and output bits: the strict avalanche criterion requires that output bit j flip with probability one half whenever input bit i is flipped, for all i and j. [ref]

4.5 Independence within a digest

Uniform bits may still be dependent. The runs test of NIST SP 800-22 counts the maximal blocks of identical bits in each 256-bit digest: too few runs betray clustering, too many an oscillation. Each digest yields a p-value. For long sequences these p-values are uniform on [0, 1] under the null hypothesis, but for a 256-bit string the number of runs takes few distinct values, so the p-value is a discrete variable whose exact law can be computed by counting the strings with k ones and r runs, 2\binom{k-1}{m-1}\binom{n-k-1}{m-1} for r = 2m and the analogous sum for odd r, each with probability 2^{-n}. Figure 9 compares the histogram of p-values over ten bins with that exact law by a chi-square test; a flat reference would wrongly reject even SHA-256. The proportion of digests with p \ge 0.01 is compared with the interval 0.99 \pm 3\sqrt{0.0099 / n} used by the NIST suite. The same section tests adjacent bits directly: the 2 × 2 table of pairs (b_j, b_{j+1}) pooled over all digests, with its chi-square statistic on one degree of freedom and the lag-one correlation. [ref]

4.6 Collisions and the birthday bound

Collisions of a 256-bit digest cannot be observed directly, so the digests are truncated to their leading t bits. Among n uniform values on t bits the expected number of colliding pairs is \binom{n}{2} / 2^{t}, and the probability that a sample of m values contains a repeat is 1 - \prod_{i < m} (1 - i / 2^{t}), which passes one half near m \approx 1.18 \sqrt{2^{t}}. [ref] Table 1 compares expected and observed pairs for four truncations; Figure 10 draws the probability curve for t = {{fig.collisions.scalar.bits}}, estimated from {{fig.collisions.scalar.subsets}} random subsets of each size taken from the pool.

4.7 Preimage resistance and the cost of the discrete logarithm

Inverting the toy hash means recovering the scalar from the point, the discrete logarithm problem. It cannot be observed on secp256k1, so it is measured where it can be solved: on the curves y^2 = x^3 + 7 over the {{fig.ecdlp.curves}} primes nearest to 2^7, \dots, 2^{14}, with a random base point and {{fig.ecdlp.challenges}} random challenges each. Exhaustive search costs about half the group order; Shanks's baby-step giant-step costs about 2\sqrt{n} operations and \sqrt{n} memory, and Pollard's rho the same time with no memory, which no generic algorithm can beat. [ref] Figure 11 shows the measured costs growing as n and \sqrt{n} (on the largest curve, of order {{fig.ecdlp.largestOrder}}, exhaustive search averaged {{fig.ecdlp.largestBrute}} operations and baby-step giant-step {{fig.ecdlp.largestBsgs}}), and extends the lines to the order of secp256k1, where \sqrt{n} \approx 2^{128}.

§5 What statistics cannot see

The scalar variant passes every test of §4 as convincingly as SHA-256, and yet it is broken as a hash function. Its digest is the x-coordinate of k \cdot G, and two facts about the group give second preimages for free. First, (n - k) \cdot G = -(k \cdot G) has the same x-coordinate, so the message encoding n - k collides with the message encoding k. Second, n \cdot G = O, so the message encoding k + n collides as well. For the worked example of §3.1 the two colliding messages are

Negation message, 32 bytes, digest {{fig.demo.negationEqual}}{{fig.demo.negationHex}}
Wrap message, 33 bytes, digest {{fig.demo.wrapEqual}}{{fig.demo.wrapHex}}

Both variants are also additively homomorphic: the point of the byte-wise sum of two messages is the sum of their points, so a digest leaks relations between messages that a random oracle would hide. None of these properties leaves a statistical trace in random samples, because the colliding messages and the related messages are vanishingly rare among random inputs; they are found by algebra, not by sampling. This is the central lesson of the exercise: statistical tests are necessary, since a hash that fails them is certainly unfit, but they are never sufficient, since security is a statement about every adversary and not about the average input. [ref]

6.1 Bitcoin

Section placeholder: secp256k1 signatures, SHA-256 and RIPEMD-160, Merkle trees, Schnorr signatures and Taproot, and proof of work as a Poisson process with its difficulty adjustment. [ref] The statistical heart of the protocol is the race analysed in the original paper: an attacker holding a fraction q of the hash rate, trying to replace a transaction buried under z blocks, progresses as a Poisson variable with mean \lambda = z\,q/p while the honest chain grows, and catches up from a deficit of d blocks with probability (q/p)^d, giving

P = 1 - \sum_{k=0}^{z} \frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!} \Big( 1 - (q/p)^{z-k} \Big), \qquad p = 1 - q.
(3)

Figure 12 evaluates equation (3). With a tenth of the hash rate the attacker's chance falls below {{fig.nakamoto.q10z6}} after {{fig.nakamoto.markZ}} confirmations, the depth conventionally treated as final; with three tenths it is still {{fig.nakamoto.q30z6}} at the same depth and {{fig.nakamoto.q30z20}} after twenty. App. Reproducibility

Every figure is computed at build time from the seeds printed in its caption and can be recomputed by anyone from the repository: npm test runs the test suite, including the published test vectors of secp256k1 and SHA-256 and the NIST worked examples, and npm run build rebuilds the page, recomputing the experiments in about {{fig.params.computeSeconds}} seconds (last computed {{fig.params.computedAt}}). The browser controls redraw figures on fresh seeds at reduced sample sizes and report the seed used.

  • src/lib/secp256k1.js: field and curve arithmetic from the definitions.
  • src/lib/ec-hash.js: the toy hash, its two generator families and the structural collisions.
  • src/lib/sha256.js: SHA-256 from FIPS 180-4.
  • src/lib/stats.js: chi-square, binomial, the NIST frequency and runs tests, the birthday bound and equation (3).
  • src/lib/ec-hash-study.js: the experiments of §4.
  • homework/01/figures.js: the parameters, seeds and sizes of every figure on this page.
