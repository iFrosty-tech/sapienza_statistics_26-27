§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Ex. 2 – Sampling Mean and Variance

Extend the previous homework by assigning each penetration attempt a score between 0 and 10, drawn from a fixed probability distribution of your choice. Let the theoretical mean and variance of this distribution be μ and σ², respectively.

Assume there are m hackers, each attempting to penetrate n systems. Each hacker receives one score for each system, resulting in a trajectory of n scores.

For each hacker, use Welford’s recurrence formulas to compute the sampling mean and sampling variance of their n scores.

Across the m hackers, compute the average of the sampling means and the average of the sampling variances. Compare these two averages with the theoretical mean μ and variance σ², respectively.

What conclusions can you draw from these comparisons? Why are these results useful in inferential statistics? Repeat the experiment for different values of n. How does increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker affect the variability of the sampling means and sampling variances?

Throughout this page, the sampling mean and the sampling variance of the assignment are the sample mean \bar{x}_n and the sample variance s_n^2 = \mathrm{SS}_n/(n - 1), where \mathrm{SS}_n is the corrected sum of squares of the n scores of one hacker; the variance therefore needs n \ge 2.

The law of the scores is the free choice of the exercise, and §2 makes it so that nothing on this page can owe its result to normality. §3 runs the experiment, with every estimate obtained through Welford's recurrences, and §4 compares the two averages with \mu and \sigma^2. §5 proves why they agree, §6 repeats the experiment for {{fig.params.nCount}} values of n from 2 to {{fig.params.nMax}}, §7 turns the results into inference about a single hacker, and §8 gathers the answers. In short: the average of the sampling means stays on \mu and the average of the sampling variances on \sigma^2 at every n, even n = 2, because both estimators are unbiased; the spread of both across hackers shrinks as 1/\sqrt{n}; and the 95% interval for \mu reaches its nominal level as n grows, whereas the usual interval for \sigma^2 never does, stalling near {{fig.cover.chiLimit}} however many systems are attempted.

§2 The score law

2.1 From a penetration attempt to a score

Homework 3 modelled one penetration attempt by a Bernoulli increment B, equal to 1 if the attempt succeeds, with p = 0.3. The extension asked for here replaces the bare outcome by a score between 0 and 10. A failed attempt scores 0; a successful one scores the depth Y it reached, which is uniform on the interval from 0 to 10 and independent of the outcome:

\begin{gathered} X = B\,Y, \\ B \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.3), \quad Y \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 10], \quad B \perp Y . \end{gathered}
(1)

This law is chosen for three reasons. It extends the previous homework literally: X > 0 exactly when the attempt of homework 3 succeeds, with the same p. All its moments are available in closed form, so every comparison below is made against an exact value and not against another simulation. And it is far from normal, with a spike at 0 and a long flat tail, so that nothing below can be an artefact of normality. The range from 0 to 10 echoes the base score of the Common Vulnerability Scoring System [5], which rates the severity of a vulnerability from 0.0 to 10.0; the score of (1) is not a CVSS score, and it borrows only the scale.

2.2 The distribution

The law of (1) is neither discrete nor continuous. It has an atom of 1 - p at 0 and a constant density p/10 on (0, 10], so that

F(x) = \Pr\{X \le x\} = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0, \\ 0.7 + 0.03\,x, & 0 \le x < 10, \\ 1, & x \ge 10. \end{cases}
(2)

Scores are simulated by inversion, with exactly one uniform U per score: X = 0 when U \le 0.7, and X = (U - 0.7)/0.03 otherwise. Figure 1 sets (2) against a sample of {{fig.sample.size}} scores, of which {{fig.sample.zeros}} are 0.

2.3 Exact moments

Since B and Y are independent and B^k = B for k \ge 1, the raw moments of (1) are

\mathbb{E}[X^k] = \mathbb{E}[B]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^k] = \frac{p\,10^k}{k + 1}, \qquad k \ge 1 ,
(3)

which gives 3/2, 10, 75 and 600 for k = 1, \dots, 4. Expanding the central moments \mu_k = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^k] in these,

\begin{aligned} \mu &= \tfrac{3}{2}, \\ \sigma^2 &= 10 - \mu^2 = \tfrac{31}{4}, \\ \mu_3 &= 75 - 3\mu \cdot 10 + 2\mu^3 = \tfrac{147}{4}, \\ \mu_4 &= 600 - 4\mu \cdot 75 + 6\mu^2 \cdot 10 - 3\mu^4 = \tfrac{4317}{16} . \end{aligned}
(4)

Thus \mu = {{fig.law.mu}}, \sigma^2 = {{fig.law.sigma2}}, \mu_3 = {{fig.law.mu3}} and \mu_4 = {{fig.law.mu4}}. The skewness \mu_3/\sigma^3 is {{fig.law.skewness}} and the kurtosis \kappa = \mu_4/\sigma^4 is {{fig.law.kurtosis}}, against 0 and 3 for a normal law: the score is markedly right-skewed and heavy in the fourth moment, and both facts matter in §7.

§3 The experiment

3.1 m hackers, n systems

Hacker j attempts n systems in turn and receives the score X_i^{(j)} on system i; the scores are independent across systems and hackers and follow (1). Each hacker's trajectory of n scores goes through the recurrences derived in Exercise 1 [9], which from \bar{x}_0 = 0 and \mathrm{SS}_0 = 0 read

\begin{aligned} \bar{x}_k &= \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}, \\ \mathrm{SS}_k &= \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) , \end{aligned}
(5)

and leave \bar{x}_n^{(j)} and s_n^{2\,(j)} = \mathrm{SS}_n^{(j)}/(n - 1). No hacker's scores are stored: three numbers of state are enough, and the same function performs every update on this page, in the study as in the live panel. For each n of §6 this is an independent experiment of {{fig.params.m}} hackers.

3.2 The whole path of an estimate

Because (5) holds at every step, a trajectory of n scores yields not one estimate but a path of them: what the hacker's sample would report after k systems, for every k \le n. Figure 2 draws these paths for {{fig.params.trajM}} hackers over {{fig.params.trajN}} systems, against the exact mean and standard deviation at each k given in §5.

Panel 1 repeats the experiment live for any n and m. The running paths are those of a few hackers, while the histograms beside them and the table underneath hold all m of them. Both vertical axes are fixed, so that moving n changes the spread and not its scale, and Play advances every hacker through the recurrences one system at a time.

Panel 1 (interactive, not available in print or without scripting) simulates m hackers over n systems in the browser and shows their running estimates, the histograms of the sampling means and variances, and the two averages. In the study, at n = 100 with {{fig.params.m}} hackers, the average of the sampling means was {{fig.avg.n100.mean}} against {{fig.law.mu}} and the average of the sampling variances {{fig.avg.n100.variance}} against {{fig.law.sigma2}} (Table 1), with spreads across hackers of {{fig.spread.n100.sdMean}} and {{fig.spread.n100.sdVariance}} (Table 2).

§4 The averages against the theory

4.1 The comparison

Across the m hackers the assignment asks for the two averages

A_n = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \bar{x}_n^{(j)}, \qquad B_n = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} s_n^{2\,(j)} .
(6)

An average of m independent estimates has a standard error of \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)/m}, and both variances are known exactly from §5, so each comparison can be read as a z-score, (A_n - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2/(mn)} and (B_n - \sigma^2)/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2)/m}. Table 1 lists them for every n, and Figure 3 plots them.

The averages stay on their theoretical values at every n, from a hacker with two scores to one with {{fig.params.nMax}}. Of the {{fig.avg.zCount}} z-scores, {{fig.avg.zAboveTwo}} exceed 2 in absolute value: {{fig.avg.zList}}. If the averages were exactly unbiased, and the z-scores close to normal, about {{fig.avg.zExpected}} would be expected to do so, so this count is what an honest experiment yields and not a discrepancy. The seed was fixed before the first run and has not been changed since. The two exceedances are of opposite sign and sit at unrelated values of n; the z-score of B_n at the same n moves with that of A_n because the two estimators are correlated (§7.4).

4.2 Why the divisor is n − 1

The recurrences give \mathrm{SS}_n, and the obvious estimator would divide it by n. The last two columns of Table 1 show what that estimator does: its average does not settle on \sigma^2 but on \sigma^2(n - 1)/n. At n = 2 it reports {{fig.avg.n2.ssOverN}} where the variance is {{fig.law.sigma2}}, {{fig.avg.n2.shortfall}} too low; at n = 10 the shortfall is still {{fig.avg.n10.shortfall}}. Figure 4 shows this against n: averaging more hackers only pins the wrong value more precisely, because the error is a bias, not noise.

§5 Why the averages agree

The results of §4 hold for any law with enough moments, and the proofs below use nothing about (1) beyond independence and identical distribution. They are standard [2, Ch. 5; 3], and they are given here because they explain every column of Table 1.

5.1 The sampling mean

By linearity of the expectation, and by independence for the variance,

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_n] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu, \\ \mathrm{Var}(\bar{x}_n) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} . \end{aligned}
(7)

5.2 The sampling variance

Adding and subtracting \mu inside the sum of squares, and using \sum_i (X_i - \bar{x}_n) = 0,

\mathrm{SS}_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{x}_n - \mu)^2 .
(8)

The first term has expectation n\sigma^2 and the second, by (7), n \cdot \sigma^2/n = \sigma^2. Hence

\begin{gathered} \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SS}_n] = (n - 1)\,\sigma^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}[s_n^2] = \sigma^2, \\ \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SS}_n/n] = \frac{n - 1}{n}\,\sigma^2 . \end{gathered}
(9)

Deviations are measured from \bar{x}_n and not from the unknown \mu, and \bar{x}_n is the point that makes them smallest. That is the whole of the bias in §4.2, and it costs exactly one \sigma^2 out of n\sigma^2.

5.3 The variance of the sampling variance

The spreads of §6 need the second moment of s_n^2. The shortest route writes the sample variance as an average over pairs,

s_n^2 = \binom{n}{2}^{\!-1} \sum_{i < j} \frac{(X_i - X_j)^2}{2} ,
(10)

which is a U-statistic with kernel h(x, y) = (x - y)^2/2. Hoeffding's formula for such a statistic [6] gives

\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2) = \frac{4(n - 2)\,\zeta_1 + 2\,\zeta_2}{n(n - 1)} ,

with \zeta_1 = \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[h(X_1, X_2) \mid X_1]) and \zeta_2 = \mathrm{Var}(h(X_1, X_2)). Here \mathbb{E}[h \mid X_1] = ((X_1 - \mu)^2 + \sigma^2)/2, so \zeta_1 = (\mu_4 - \sigma^4)/4, and expanding (X_1 - X_2)^4 gives \zeta_2 = (\mu_4 + \sigma^4)/2. Substituting and simplifying,

\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2) = \frac{1}{n} \left( \mu_4 - \frac{n - 3}{n - 1}\,\sigma^4 \right) ,
(11)

the classical result [3]. It depends on the law only through \sigma^2 and \mu_4. For normal scores \mu_4 = 3\sigma^4 and (11) reduces to the familiar 2\sigma^4/(n - 1).

5.4 The two estimators together

Taking \mu = 0 without loss of generality, (10) expresses s_n^2 through pairs, and only a pair that contains system i can be correlated with X_i. For such a pair \mathrm{Cov}(X_1, (X_1 - X_2)^2) = \mu_3, and counting the n - 1 pairs that contain each index gives

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Cov}(\bar{x}_n, s_n^2) &= \frac{\mu_3}{n}, \\ \mathrm{Corr}(\bar{x}_n, s_n^2) &= \frac{\mu_3}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 \bigl(\mu_4 - \frac{n - 3}{n - 1}\sigma^4\bigr)}} . \end{aligned}
(12)

The covariance vanishes for a symmetric law. For the law (1) it does not, and the correlation tends to \mu_3/\sqrt{\sigma^2(\mu_4 - \sigma^4)} = {{fig.scatter.limit}} as n grows.

5.5 What the comparisons show

The first conclusion is that \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are unbiased, and this is what the averages of Table 1 display: an estimator is unbiased when the mean of its sampling distribution equals the quantity it estimates, and an average over many hackers is a direct estimate of that mean. The second is that unbiasedness holds at every n, not only in the limit, and for this strongly non-normal law as much as for any other: neither the central limit theorem nor normality is used in (7) or (9). The third is that it is a property of the divisor: \mathrm{SS}_n/n, computed from the same recurrences, is biased, and no number of hackers repairs it. The fourth is a caution: unbiasedness says nothing about a single hacker. At n = 2 the averages are right while the individual estimates are almost useless; how far one hacker's estimate can stray is the subject of §6.

§6 Repeating for different n

6.1 The spreads across hackers

Each n of {{fig.params.nGrid}} is a fresh experiment of {{fig.params.m}} hackers with its own random stream, so no estimate at one n is a continuation of another. The averages did not move with n (§4); what moves is the spread of the estimates across hackers, measured here by their standard deviation.

Both spreads fall as 1/\sqrt{n}. For the mean, (7) gives \mathrm{SD}(\bar{x}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} exactly, a slope of precisely -1/2 on the log–log plot; the fitted slope is {{fig.spread.slopeMean}}. For the variance, (11) gives \mathrm{SD}(s_n^2) \approx \sqrt{(\mu_4 - \sigma^4)/n} for large n, the same rate with a constant of {{fig.law.sdConstant}}. The fitted slope over the whole grid is {{fig.spread.slopeVariance}}, and the exact curve of (11) has the same {{fig.spread.slopeExactVariance}}: the small excess over -1/2 comes from the factor (n - 3)/(n - 1) at the smallest values of n, not from a different rate. Multiplying the number of systems by 100 therefore divides both spreads by about 10: from n = 10 to n = 1,000, the spread of the means falls from {{fig.spread.n10.sdMean}} to {{fig.spread.n1000.sdMean}} and that of the variances from {{fig.spread.n10.sdVariance}} to {{fig.spread.n1000.sdVariance}}.

The two estimators obey the same rate but not the same constant, and they answer to different properties of the law. The spread of \bar{x}_n is governed by the variance \sigma^2, the spread of s_n^2 by the fourth moment, that is, by the kurtosis. Writing (11) in terms of \kappa, the right panel of Figure 5 shows what this costs: the true variance of s_n^2 exceeds its normal-theory value at every n, and the ratio climbs to (\kappa - 1)/2 = {{fig.spread.ratioLimit}}. It does not fall back to 1 however many systems are attempted, and §7.3 draws the consequence.

6.2 The sampling distributions

Figure 6 shows the whole law of the two estimators at n = {{fig.params.distributionN}}, against the normal approximations N(\mu, \sigma^2/n) and N(\sigma^2, \mathrm{Var}(s_n^2)).

At n = 5 neither law resembles a normal one. A share {{fig.dist.n5.atomShare}} of the hackers scored 0 on every system (the exact value is 0.7^5, or {{fig.dist.n5.atomExact}}) and they pile up at \bar{x}_n = 0 and s_n^2 = 0, the atom of the score law seen through the estimators. As n grows the central limit theorem takes over, and by n = 500 both histograms sit close to their normal curves, the sampling mean sooner than the sampling variance: the skewness of \bar{x}_n is the skewness of the score divided by \sqrt{n}, {{fig.dist.n5.skewMean}} at n = 5 and {{fig.dist.n500.skewMean}} at n = 500.

6.3 Hackers against systems

The two indices of the experiment are easily confused, and they do different things. The number of systems n sets how much each hacker knows. The number of hackers m sets how well the experiment measures what a hacker would know. Figure 7 fixes n = {{fig.precision.n}} and cuts a long run of {{fig.params.precisionHackers}} hackers into groups of m to show the difference.

The error of the two averages falls as 1/\sqrt{m}, from {{fig.precision.m10.rmsMean}} at m = 10 to {{fig.precision.m1000.rmsMean}} at m = 1,000 for the sampling mean, with fitted slopes of {{fig.precision.slopeMean}} and {{fig.precision.slopeVariance}}. The fit runs a little steeper than -1/2 because the largest groups are few ({{fig.precision.m10000.groups}} groups at m = 10,000), and the last points are themselves noisy. The spread of one hacker's estimate does not change at all: {{fig.precision.m10.spreadMean}} at m = 10, {{fig.precision.m1000.spreadMean}} at m = 1,000, on its exact value of {{fig.precision.exactSdMean}}. More hackers tell us more exactly what a hacker with {{fig.precision.n}} scores can expect; they do not make that hacker any better informed. Only n does that.

The direct answer to the last question of the assignment follows. Increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker reduces the variability of both the sampling means and the sampling variances, each in proportion to 1/\sqrt{n}, leaves their centres where they are, and brings their laws closer to the normal law. The spread of the sampling means depends on the variance of the scores and the spread of the sampling variances on their kurtosis. In absolute terms the sampling variance is much the noisier estimate; relative to what each one estimates, the two happen to be about equally precise for this law, since at n = 1,000 the spreads are {{fig.spread.n1000.sdVariance}} out of {{fig.law.sigma2}} and {{fig.spread.n1000.sdMean}} out of {{fig.law.mu}}, or {{fig.spread.n1000.relVariance}} and {{fig.spread.n1000.relMean}}. Both are slow: a tenfold reduction takes a hundredfold effort.

§7 Inference

7.1 One hacker, and the thought experiment behind it

The experiment above has a privileged position: it can repeat the sample {{fig.params.m}} times. Nobody who studies real attackers can. In practice there is one sample, and inferential statistics is the discipline of saying something about \mu and \sigma^2 from it. The m hackers are the frequentist thought experiment that gives such statements their meaning: the sampling distribution of an estimator is what we would see across many repetitions, and a claim about one sample is a claim about the procedure that produced it. The results of §4–§6 are useful because they describe that sampling distribution, and because they can be used from one sample alone.

A single hacker's trajectory supplies a point estimate \bar{x}_n of \mu, unbiased by (7), and also an estimate of its own precision. The standard error \sigma/\sqrt{n} involves the unknown \sigma, but s_n^2, unbiased by (9) and consistent by (11), can stand in for \sigma^2. The sample therefore reports both a value and how far to trust it, \bar{x}_n \pm s_n/\sqrt{n}, and the two numbers come out of the same three-line recurrence. This is why unbiasedness of s_n^2 matters beyond itself: it is what makes the standard error of a sample computable from that sample.

7.2 The t-interval for the mean

The central limit theorem gives \sqrt{n}(\bar{x}_n - \mu)/\sigma \to N(0, 1) in distribution, and s_n \to \sigma in probability by (11), so Slutsky's theorem [4] gives the same limit with the estimated standard deviation:

T_n = \frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{x}_n - \mu)}{s_n} \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N(0, 1) .
(13)

Any finite-variance law qualifies, and normality of the scores is not needed. The 95% interval \bar{x}_n \pm t_{n-1;\,0.975}\, s_n/\sqrt{n}, with the quantile of Student's distribution [8], is exact for normal scores and, by (13), asymptotically correct for these. Figure 8 and Table 3 measure how soon: the share of hackers whose interval contains \mu is {{fig.cover.n5.tRate}} at n = 5, {{fig.cover.n20.tRate}} at n = 20, {{fig.cover.n100.tRate}} at n = 100 and {{fig.cover.n5000.tRate}} at n = 5,000.

At the smallest n the hackers with no success dominate: at n = 2 they make up {{fig.cover.n2.degenerate}} of the {{fig.params.m}} hackers, and the coverage of {{fig.cover.n2.tRate}} sits just under the ceiling of {{fig.cover.n2.ceiling}} they impose. Their probability falls fast, to {{fig.cover.n20.zeroProbability}} at n = 20, and from then on what remains of the shortfall has a subtler cause (§7.4).

7.3 The χ² interval for the variance

For normal scores (n - 1)s_n^2/\sigma^2 has a \chi^2_{n-1} law, which gives the textbook interval

\left[ \frac{(n - 1)\,s_n^2}{\chi^2_{n-1;\,0.975}}, \; \frac{(n - 1)\,s_n^2}{\chi^2_{n-1;\,0.025}} \right] .
(14)

No analogue of (13) comes to its rescue here. By the central limit theorem s_n^2 is asymptotically normal, but with the variance of (11), of order \sigma^4(\kappa - 1)/n, while the half-width of (14) is calibrated for the normal value \sigma^4 \cdot 2/n. The interval therefore stays too short by the fixed factor \sqrt{2/(\kappa - 1)}, and its coverage tends not to 95% but to

2\,\Phi\!\left(1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{\kappa - 1}}\right) - 1 .
(15)

For \kappa = {{fig.law.kurtosis}} this limit is {{fig.cover.chiLimit}}. The simulation confirms it: at n = {{fig.cover.largestN}} the coverage is {{fig.cover.n5000.chiRate}}, with a Wilson interval from {{fig.cover.n5000.chiLow}} to {{fig.cover.n5000.chiHigh}}, which differs from (15) by {{fig.cover.chiZ}} standard errors, and the average over all n from {{fig.cover.tailFrom}} upwards is {{fig.cover.chiLargeMean}}. More data do not help: this is the sensitivity to non-normality that Box [1] set out, and the reason why the two intervals of this section behave so differently. A central limit theorem protects the mean because the interval uses it directly; the interval for the variance leans on the exact \chi^2 law, which is a property of normal samples and which no amount of averaging supplies.

7.4 Why the t-interval is slow: the two estimators are tied

For normal scores \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are independent, and independence characterises the normal law [7]. For the law (1) they are far from independent: by (12) their correlation tends to {{fig.scatter.limit}}. A hacker with few successes has both a low mean and a low variance, since nearly all of the spread of the scores comes from the successes themselves; Figure 9 shows the resulting cloud, and at n = 10 the hackers with few successes draw visible arcs in it.

This dependence explains how the t-interval fails at moderate n. When \bar{x}_n happens to be low, s_n tends to be low with it, so the interval is short exactly when it needs to be long, and it stops short of \mu from below. When \bar{x}_n is high the interval is long and easily reaches back. The misses are therefore lopsided: at n = 20, {{fig.cover.n20.tBelow}} intervals lie wholly below \mu against {{fig.cover.n20.tAbove}} above it, so that {{fig.cover.n20.tBelowShare}} of the misses are on one side, although the nominal 5% is meant to be shared equally. At n = 5,000 the split is {{fig.cover.n5000.tBelow}} to {{fig.cover.n5000.tAbove}}, and the coverage has reached its nominal level. The practical lesson is that (13) is a statement about large n, and that for a skewed score, "large" is a matter of how skewed: the same 95% label covers {{fig.cover.n10.tRate}} of the hackers at n = 10.

§8 Discussion

What the comparisons show. Across the hackers, the average of the sampling means agrees with \mu and the average of the sampling variances with \sigma^2, within the sampling error of the experiment, at every n and for a law that is nowhere near normal: \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are unbiased (§4, §5). The divisor n - 1 is what makes the second true, and \mathrm{SS}_n/n would fall short by the factor (n - 1)/n.

Why it matters for inference. Unbiasedness and the exact variances describe the sampling distribution of an estimator, which is the object that gives a statement drawn from one sample its meaning. They let one hacker report an estimate and its own standard error, and through the central limit theorem and Slutsky's theorem they yield an interval for \mu with the stated coverage for large n, whatever the law. They also mark the limits of that argument: the coverage of the usual interval for \sigma^2 settles near {{fig.cover.chiLimit}}, not 95%, because it rests on normality itself and not on a limit theorem (§7).

What n does. Increasing the number of systems shrinks the variability of both estimators as 1/\sqrt{n}, keeps their centres fixed and makes their laws more nearly normal. The number of hackers does none of this: m sharpens our picture of the sampling distribution, not the estimates of any hacker (§6).

What the model leaves out. The conclusions rest on the assumptions of the assignment. The scores are independent across systems, whereas a hacker who breaches one system of a network learns something that helps on the next, and a vulnerability is often shared by many systems; positive dependence inflates the variance of \bar{x}_n beyond \sigma^2/n, while s_n^2/n goes on reporting the smaller value. All hackers also share one p, so the study has no skilled and unskilled attackers: with hackers of different ability the spread of \bar{x}_n across hackers would not shrink to 0 with n but to the spread of their abilities, and the average over hackers would estimate the mean ability, not the ability of any one of them. The depth of a success is uniform by choice, and a heavier tail would slow every convergence measured here.

App. Reproducibility

Every figure, table and statistic on this page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds of Table A1, and can be recomputed by anyone from the repository: npm test runs the test suite, which covers the score law, the two quantile functions, the study, the figures and the interactive panel, and npm run build rebuilds the page. The study draws {{fig.params.draws}} pseudo-random numbers and takes about {{fig.params.computeSeconds}} seconds (last computed {{fig.params.computedAt}}); its results are cached under node_modules/.cache/hw06/ with the key {{fig.params.cacheKey}}, a hash of the parameters, of the source of every module involved and of the figure provider itself, so that a change to any of them recomputes everything.

Each run has its own stream of a seeded Mulberry32 generator, whose seed is the master seed {{fig.params.seed}} combined by exclusive or with the 32-bit FNV-1a hash of a label: hackers:n100 gives {{fig.params.seeds.n100}} for the experiment at n = 100, and every other n has its own label, so that no experiment reuses another's scores. Within an experiment hackers read the stream one after another. One uniform makes one score, by inversion of (2). Student's quantile is computed by bisection on the regularised incomplete beta function and the \chi^2 quantile by bisection on the regularised incomplete gamma function; both reproduce printed tables. The generator is statistical, not cryptographic. The interactive panel starts from the master seed and reads one stream system by system, so that it can advance all hackers together.
