§1 Problem statement

The assignment has two parts. The first asks for the differences between the main modes of convergence of probability theory (in probability, almost sure, in mean square and in distribution), for the role each plays in the law of large numbers (LLN) and in the central limit theorem (CLT), and for an explanation of why the factor \sqrt{n} appears so often, in connection with the additivity of variance and with independence. The second asks for conditional probability and Bayes' theorem, for the relation between the prior, the likelihood and the posterior, for the reason why updating plays a central part in Bayesian inference, and for examples of priors and likelihoods with their advantages, limitations and influence on the conclusions.

Every statement of theory is accompanied by a computation. Where a probability has a closed form it is computed exactly (a sum of independent exponential variables is a gamma variable, and the posterior of a proportion under a beta prior is a beta law); where it has none it is simulated. Every table, figure and number is produced when the site is built, by src/lib/convergence.js and src/lib/bayes.js, and nothing is typed in by hand.

§2 Four modes of convergence

Let X_1, X_2, \dots and X be random variables on one probability space. A sequence of numbers has one meaning of convergence; a sequence of random variables has several, because a variable is a function of the outcome \omega and the closeness of two functions can be measured in more than one way.

  • In probability, X_n \xrightarrow{P} X: for every \varepsilon \gt 0,
    P\bigl(|X_n - X| \gt \varepsilon\bigr) \to 0 \quad (n \to \infty).
    (1)
    The probability of a deviation of any fixed size vanishes, but at each n a different set of outcomes may deviate.
  • Almost surely, X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X:
    P\bigl(\omega : X_n(\omega) \to X(\omega)\bigr) = 1.
    (2)
    Individual trajectories converge, as sequences of numbers, for all outcomes except a set of probability zero. It is the strongest of the four in the sense of (5) below, and equivalent to P(\sup_{k \ge n} |X_k - X| \gt \varepsilon) \to 0 for every \varepsilon: the whole tail of the sequence, and not one term, stays close.
  • In mean square (in L^2), X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X:
    E\bigl[(X_n - X)^2\bigr] \to 0.
    (3)
    The mean squared error vanishes. It is a statement about averages over outcomes and it is sensitive to rare, large deviations.
  • In distribution, X_n \xrightarrow{d} X:
    F_{X_n}(x) \to F_X(x) \quad \text{at every } x \text{ where } F_X \text{ is continuous.}
    (4)
    Only the laws converge: the variables need not even be defined on the same space, and no closeness of X_n to X as functions of \omega is implied.

The implications between them are

X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{P} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{d} X, \qquad X_n \xrightarrow{L^2} X \;\Longrightarrow\; X_n \xrightarrow{P} X,
(5)

and no arrow reverses in general. Two partial converses matter: if the limit is a constant c, convergence in distribution to c is the same as convergence in probability to c; and a sequence that converges in probability has a subsequence that converges almost surely. Table 1 collects the four modes with the results in which each is used, which §4 to §6 explain.

§3 Telling the modes apart

Three sequences show that the arrows of (5) do not reverse. In the first two, U_1, U_2, \dots are independent and uniform on (0, 1).

  • The indicator sequence X_n = \mathbf{1}\{U_n \lt 1/n\} takes the value 1 with probability 1/n and 0 otherwise. Then P(|X_n| \gt \varepsilon) = 1/n \to 0 and E[X_n^2] = 1/n \to 0, so it converges to 0 in probability and in mean square. But \sum 1/n diverges and the terms are independent, so by the second Borel–Cantelli lemma X_n = 1 for infinitely many n, with probability one: it does not converge almost surely.
  • The spike sequence X_n = n\,\mathbf{1}\{U_n \lt 1/n^2\} takes the value n with probability 1/n^2. Now \sum 1/n^2 converges, so by the first Borel–Cantelli lemma only finitely many spikes occur, and X_n \to 0 almost surely. Yet E[X_n^2] = n^2 \cdot 1/n^2 = 1 for every n: it does not converge in mean square. A rare, huge value is invisible to probability and decisive for a second moment.
  • The alternating sequence X_n = (-1)^n Z, with Z standard normal, has the standard normal law at every n, so it converges in distribution to Z. For odd n, however, |X_n - Z| = 2|Z|, and P(|X_n - Z| \gt \varepsilon) = 2\bigl(1 - \Phi(\varepsilon/2)\bigr) does not depend on n; at \varepsilon = 1 it is {{fig.seq.alternating.gap}}. There is no convergence in probability.

The simulation makes the distinction visible. Table 3 gives, for the two independent sequences, the probability that some term among X_{n+1}, \dots, X_N is nonzero, with N = {{fig.params.N}}. Because the terms are independent it is a telescoping product, 1 - n/N for the indicator and 1 - n(N+1)/\bigl((n+1)N\bigr) for the spike, and the table compares it with the fraction of {{fig.params.paths}} simulated paths.

At n = 100 the indicator sequence still has a further 1 ahead in {{fig.seq.indicator.exact100}} of the paths (simulated {{fig.seq.indicator.sim100}}), the spike sequence in {{fig.seq.spike.exact100}} (simulated {{fig.seq.spike.sim100}}); by n = 1{,}000 the latter is {{fig.seq.spike.exact1000}}. §4 The laws of large numbers

Let X_1, X_2, \dots be independent and identically distributed with mean \mu and let \bar X_n be their average. The weak law says that \bar X_n \xrightarrow{P} \mu; the strong law (Kolmogorov) says that \bar X_n \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu, and needs only E|X_1| \lt \infty. They differ exactly as in §2: the weak law bounds the chance of a miss at each n; the strong law says that a single sequence of experiments, run indefinitely, settles at \mu, which is the statement that justifies reading a relative frequency as a probability.

When the variance \sigma^2 is finite, the weak law has a one-line proof, and it also gives convergence in mean square, because E[(\bar X_n - \mu)^2] = \sigma^2/n \to 0 and Chebyshev's inequality then gives

P\bigl(|\bar X_n - \mu| \gt \varepsilon\bigr) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n\,\varepsilon^2}.
(6)

This is the route by which L^2 convergence implies convergence in probability in (5). The bound is crude. Table 4 compares it, for \varepsilon = {{fig.params.epsilon}}, with the exact probability for exponential variables (\mu = \sigma = 1) and with the normal approximation of §5: at n = 1{,}000 the bound is {{fig.lln.chebyshev1000}} and the exact value {{fig.lln.exact1000}}.

The last column shows what happens when the mean does not exist. The average of n independent standard Cauchy variables is itself standard Cauchy, for every n, so P(|\bar X_n| \gt \varepsilon) = {{fig.lln.cauchy}} never decreases and the law of large numbers fails. Figures 2 and 3 show five running means of each kind: the exponential paths settle at 1, the Cauchy paths keep making jumps that no further averaging absorbs.

The role of the laws in statistics is consistency: an estimator that is an average (a sample proportion, a sample moment, the empirical distribution function, by the Glivenko–Cantelli theorem) converges to the quantity it estimates, in probability or almost surely. They also license Monte Carlo integration, where an average of simulated values stands for an expectation. They say nothing about the size of the error at a given n, and that is the task of the next theorem.

§5 The central limit theorem

If X_1, X_2, \dots are independent and identically distributed with mean \mu and finite variance \sigma^2 \gt 0, then

Z_n = \frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar X_n - \mu)}{\sigma} \xrightarrow{d} N(0, 1).
(7)

The mode is distribution, and it cannot be strengthened. The sequence Z_n does not converge in probability to anything: Z_n and Z_{2n} are strongly correlated, with correlation 1/\sqrt{2}, but they are not close, so the laws settle while the variables keep moving. The CLT refines the LLN: \bar X_n - \mu goes to 0, and (7) says at what scale, \sigma/\sqrt{n}, and with what shape, the normal one.

Figures 4 and 5 show the standardised mean of exponential variables, a law with skewness 2, for n = {{fig.params.cltSmall}} and n = {{fig.params.cltLarge}}. The skewness of Z_n is 2/\sqrt{n}, which is {{fig.clt.skewSmall}} and {{fig.clt.skewLarge}}: the asymmetry fades, but slowly.

The speed of the approximation can be stated exactly. The Berry–Esseen theorem bounds the Kolmogorov distance D_n = \sup_z |F_n(z) - \Phi(z)| by C\,\rho/(\sigma^3\sqrt{n}), where \rho = E|X - \mu|^3 and C \le 0.4748 (Shevtsova, 2011). For exponential variables \rho = 12/e - 2, and the distance can be computed exactly, since n\bar X_n is a gamma variable. Table 5 shows that \sqrt{n}\,D_n tends to a constant, {{fig.clt.scaled}} at n = 1{,}000: the error falls exactly at the rate 1/\sqrt{n}, from {{fig.clt.d1}} at n = 1 to {{fig.clt.d30}} at n = 30 and {{fig.clt.d1000}} at n = 1{,}000, and the theoretical bound, although it holds, is more than eight times too large.

The CLT underlies the usual tools of inference. Combined with Slutsky's lemma, which allows \sigma to be replaced by a consistent estimate, it gives the interval \bar X_n \pm 1.96\,s/\sqrt{n}, the z and t tests for a mean, and, through the delta method, the normal limit of smooth functions of means. In the same way the maximum likelihood estimator satisfies \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} N(0, I(\theta)^{-1}), where I(\theta) is the Fisher information. Each of these results is a statement in distribution, assembled with convergence in probability.

§6 Why √n appears

Let S_n = X_1 + \dots + X_n with every X_i of variance \sigma^2. Expanding the variance of a sum,

\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) + 2\sum_{i \lt j} \operatorname{Cov}(X_i, X_j) = n\sigma^2 \quad \text{when the } X_i \text{ are uncorrelated,}
(8)

and independence is the usual way of guaranteeing that the covariances vanish. Variances add; standard deviations do not. The spread of the sum is therefore \sigma\sqrt{n}, not \sigma n, and the spread of the average is \sigma/\sqrt{n}. The square root is the signature of the addition of independent variances. It also fixes the only scaling that works. For n^{-a}(S_n - n\mu), an exponent a \lt 1/2 gives a spread that grows without bound, a \gt 1/2 one that collapses to 0 (this is the LLN, already at a = 1), and a = 1/2 alone gives a nondegenerate limit, the normal law of (7).

The role of independence shows when it is removed. If every pair has correlation \rho \gt 0,

\operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \sigma^2\,n\bigl(1 + (n-1)\rho\bigr) \sim \sigma^2 \rho\, n^2,
(9)

and the spread is of order n. Table 6 estimates the standard deviation of the centred sum from {{fig.params.spreadReplicates}} simulated paths in two cases: independent exponential terms and equicorrelated normal terms with \rho = {{fig.params.rho}}. In the first, \mathrm{sd}(S_n)/\sqrt{n} stays at 1 (at n = 1{,}000 the standard deviation is {{fig.spread.n1000.independent}} against \sqrt{1000} = {{fig.spread.n1000.theory}}). In the second it grows with n, and \mathrm{sd}(S_n)/n does not vanish: it tends to \sqrt{\rho} = {{fig.spread.limit}} (simulated {{fig.spread.n1000.dependent}} against {{fig.spread.n1000.dependentTheory}} at n = 1{,}000). The average then does not converge to \mu at all, but to a random limit: the common component of the terms is never averaged out. Without independence, neither the LLN nor the CLT holds in its usual form.

The consequences for inference are practical. The standard error of a mean is \sigma/\sqrt{n}, so halving it takes four times the data, and an interval ten times shorter takes a hundred times as many. The statistics \sqrt{n}(\bar X_n - \mu_0)/s and \sqrt{n}(\hat\theta_n - \theta) carry the factor for the same reason. When observations are positively correlated (repeated measures on the same unit, time series, clusters), the effective sample size shrinks to about n/(1 + (n-1)\rho), and a standard error computed as if the data were independent is too small. The rate \sqrt{n} is also not universal: for variables with infinite variance the right scale is n^{1/\alpha} with \alpha \lt 2, and the limit is a stable law, not the normal.

§7 Conditional probability and Bayes' theorem

The conditional probability of A given B, with P(B) \gt 0, is the probability of A in the world restricted to B:

P(A \mid B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}, \qquad P(A \cap B) = P(A \mid B)\,P(B) = P(B \mid A)\,P(A).
(10)

If B_1, \dots, B_k partition the sample space, the law of total probability gives P(A) = \sum_i P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i), and substituting it into (10) gives Bayes' theorem:

P(B_i \mid A) = \frac{P(A \mid B_i)\,P(B_i)}{\sum_{j} P(A \mid B_j)\,P(B_j)}.
(11)

The theorem reverses a conditioning, and the two directions are different quantities: the probability that a person with the disease tests positive is not the probability that a person who tests positive has the disease. Confusing them is the transposed conditional fallacy, and §8 shows its size. In statistics, conditioning is everywhere: the conditional distribution of a response given a covariate, the conditional expectation E[Y \mid X] that a regression estimates, and the likelihood L(\theta) = P(\text{data} \mid \theta), a conditional probability read as a function of the unknown \theta.

§8 Prior, likelihood and posterior

In Bayesian inference the unknown \theta is itself described by a probability law. Theorem (11), written for a parameter and data y, reads

\underbrace{p(\theta \mid y)}_{\text{posterior}} = \frac{\overbrace{p(y \mid \theta)}^{\text{likelihood}}\;\overbrace{p(\theta)}^{\text{prior}}}{\underbrace{\textstyle\int p(y \mid t)\,p(t)\,dt}_{\text{evidence } p(y)}} \;\propto\; p(y \mid \theta)\,p(\theta).
(12)

The prior p(\theta) is what is believed before the data, from earlier studies, from theory or from a deliberate attempt to be uninformative. The likelihood is the model of how the data arise given \theta, and it is the only place where the data enter. The posterior is the prior reweighted by the likelihood: values of \theta that made the data likely gain weight and the others lose it. The evidence p(y) does not depend on \theta; it only normalises. Updating is central because it is the rule for combining information coherently: the posterior after one batch of data is the prior for the next, and for data that are conditionally independent the final posterior does not depend on the order or the grouping of the batches. It also yields direct probability statements, such as P(\theta \gt 0.5 \mid y), which a frequentist procedure does not provide.

Example 1: a diagnostic test

A disease has prevalence \pi, the prior probability that a person selected at random has it. A test has sensitivity P(+ \mid D) = 0.95 and specificity P(- \mid \bar D) = 0.95. By (11) the probability of disease after a positive result is \pi\,0.95 / \bigl(\pi\,0.95 + (1 - \pi)\,0.05\bigr). At a prevalence of 1% it is {{fig.bayes.d1.afterOne}}: in 100,000 people {{fig.bayes.d1.tp}} are true positives and {{fig.bayes.d1.fp}} are false positives, so most positives are healthy. A second, independent positive test, with the first posterior as the new prior, raises the probability to {{fig.bayes.d1.afterTwo}}. Table 7 shows how strongly the answer depends on the prior: {{fig.bayes.d0.afterOne}} at a prevalence of 0.1%, {{fig.bayes.d50.afterOne}} at 50%.

Example 2: a proportion with a beta prior

Let \theta be an unknown proportion and let k successes be observed in n independent trials, so that the likelihood is proportional to \theta^k(1-\theta)^{n-k}. With the prior \theta \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a, b), whose density is proportional to \theta^{a-1}(1-\theta)^{b-1}, the product (12) has the same form, and

\theta \mid k \sim \mathrm{Beta}(a + k,\; b + n - k), \qquad E[\theta \mid k] = \frac{a + b}{a + b + n}\cdot\frac{a}{a + b} + \frac{n}{a + b + n}\cdot\frac{k}{n}.
(13)

A conjugate prior keeps the posterior in the same family, and (13) displays the mechanism: the posterior mean is a weighted average of the prior mean and the sample proportion, with the prior worth a + b pseudo-observations. We take three priors, the uniform \mathrm{Beta}(1, 1), a weakly informative \mathrm{Beta}(2, 2) and a misleading \mathrm{Beta}(2, 18) that places the proportion near 0.1, and observe 70% successes. Figure 6 shows the posteriors for 7 successes in 10 trials, Figure 7 for 70 in 100.

At n = 1{,}000 the misleading prior has a weight of {{fig.bayes.n1000.misleadingWeight}} and the means, {{fig.bayes.n1000.uniform}} and {{fig.bayes.n1000.misleading}}, are almost equal: the data dominate the prior, which is the Bernstein–von Mises phenomenon, and a Bayesian and a frequentist then agree to first order. §9 Advantages and limitations

The two examples show where the Bayesian approach helps and where it needs care.

  • Information is used, and stated. Prior knowledge, such as a known prevalence, enters the calculation and not just the interpretation. With a sample of 3 successes in 3 trials the maximum likelihood estimate is 1, a certainty that no one would assert, whereas the uniform prior gives {{fig.bayes.laplace}} (Laplace's rule of succession), a value that grows with the evidence.
  • Small samples and sequential data. The posterior is exact at every n, with no asymptotic argument, and it is updated one observation at a time, which suits studies that stop or continue according to what has been seen.
  • Direct statements. A credible interval says what a reader wants to know, where the parameter probably lies; a confidence interval says how the procedure behaves in repetition.
  • The prior is a choice. Figure 6 and Table 8 show that with little data the conclusion depends on it, and a misleading prior needs hundreds of observations to be overcome. The remedy is a sensitivity analysis: report the posterior under several reasonable priors, as above.
  • No prior is neutral. The uniform prior on \theta is not uniform on \theta^2 or on the odds, and improper priors can give improper posteriors.
  • Computation and model dependence. The beta–binomial case is closed-form; most models are not, and need simulation of the posterior. As in any approach, a wrong likelihood gives wrong conclusions however good the prior.

§10 Conclusion

The four modes are ordered by strength as in (5), and each has its place: the strong law holds almost surely, the weak law in probability (and in mean square when the variance is finite), and the central limit theorem in distribution, the only mode in which a standardised mean can converge. The sequences of §3 show that these are not interchangeable, and the Cauchy law shows that the laws of large numbers need a mean. The factor \sqrt{n} is the consequence of the additivity of variances of independent terms; with correlation it is replaced by n and the limit theorems change. Bayes' theorem turns conditional probability into a rule of learning, in which the posterior is the prior reweighted by the likelihood: the answer depends on the prior when data are scarce (Example 1 and Figure 6) and ceases to depend on it as they accumulate (Table 8).
