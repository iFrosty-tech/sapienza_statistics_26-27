§1 Problem statement

The assignment asks, for a bivariate data set (X, F) of two categorical variables, for four things. First, to construct the contingency table by program and to compute its marginal and conditional frequencies. Second, to compute the frequencies that the table would show if the two variables were independent, and from them Pearson's \chi^2 statistic, with its p-value as an option. Third, to recall what independence means in probability and in statistics. Fourth, to interpret the result, deciding whether the variables are statistically independent or dependent, and to distinguish statistical association from causality.

The data set is the passenger list of the Titanic: X is the travel class of each of the {{fig.data.n}} people aboard (first, second, third, or crew) and F is the outcome (died or survived). The question is whether the chance of surviving the sinking was the same in every class. It is a natural data set for the exercise, because the answer is not obvious from the margins and because the story behind it invites a causal reading that the statistics cannot support alone.

Every table, figure and number on this page is computed when the site is built, by src/lib/independence.js from a sample of {{fig.data.n}} records, one per person; nothing is typed in by hand.

§2 Independence

In probability

Two events A and B are independent when knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other:

P(A \cap B) = P(A)\,P(B), \qquad \text{equivalently} \qquad P(A \mid B) = P(A) \quad (P(B) \gt 0).
(1)

Two discrete random variables X and F are independent when every pair of events \{X = x_i\} and \{F = f_j\} is independent, that is, when the joint distribution is the product of the marginal distributions at every point:

P(X = x_i,\, F = f_j) = P(X = x_i)\,P(F = f_j) \qquad \text{for all } i, j.
(2)

In statistics

A sample of n pairs gives the counts n_{ij} of the pairs equal to (x_i, f_j), with row totals n_{i\cdot} and column totals n_{\cdot j}. The joint relative frequency is f_{ij} = n_{ij}/n, the marginal frequencies are f_{i\cdot} = n_{i\cdot}/n and f_{\cdot j} = n_{\cdot j}/n, and the conditional frequency of F = f_j within X = x_i is f_{j \mid i} = n_{ij}/n_{i\cdot}. Independence in the sample would mean the empirical version of (2), f_{ij} = f_{i\cdot} f_{\cdot j}, or, equivalently, that the conditional distribution of F is the same in every row and equal to the marginal one:

f_{j \mid i} = \frac{n_{ij}}{n_{i\cdot}} = f_{\cdot j} \quad \text{for all } i, j \qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad n_{ij} = E_{ij} := \frac{n_{i\cdot}\, n_{\cdot j}}{n}.
(3)

The numbers E_{ij} are the expected frequencies under independence. Sample counts never match them exactly, even when the variables are independent, because of sampling variation; the question is whether the departures are larger than that variation would produce. Pearson's statistic measures them, scaling each squared departure by the count it is expected to be:

\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sum_{j=1}^{c} \frac{(n_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}.
(4)

Under the null hypothesis H_0 that X and F are independent, and when the expected counts are not small (a customary rule asks for E_{ij} \ge 5), the statistic is approximately \chi^2-distributed with (r-1)(c-1) degrees of freedom, because the margins fix r + c - 1 of the rc cells. The p-value is the probability, under H_0, of a statistic at least as large as the one observed; a small p-value is evidence against (2). Since \chi^2 grows with n for a fixed pattern of association, its size alone does not measure how strong the association is. Cramér's coefficient does,

V = \sqrt{\frac{\chi^2}{n\,(\min(r, c) - 1)}} \in [0, 1],
(5)

which is 0 for a table that is exactly independent and 1 when each level of one variable determines the other.

§3 The data and the contingency table

The data are those of Dawson (1995), as distributed with the R package datasets: {{fig.data.n}} people, of whom {{fig.data.survived}} survived and {{fig.data.died}} died. They are published as counts; the program expands them into one record (x, f) per person and builds the contingency table from those records, so that the table is constructed from a bivariate sample and not copied from the counts. The order of the records carries no information. Table 1 is the result.

The margins already say something about the sample. The crew ({{fig.class.share.Crew}} of those aboard) and the third class ({{fig.class.share.Third}}) are the two largest groups, and {{fig.data.survivedShare}} of all those aboard survived. That last figure is the benchmark for what follows: if class played no part, each class would survive at about that rate.

§4 Joint, marginal and conditional frequencies

Table 2 divides every count by n, which gives the joint frequencies f_{ij} in the cells and the marginal frequencies f_{i\cdot} and f_{\cdot j} in the last column and row.

The joint frequencies mix two things, how large each class is and how it fared, so they are not the best table for asking whether class matters. The conditional frequencies separate them. Table 3 gives the distribution of the outcome within each class, which is the one that answers the question of §1; its last row is the marginal distribution of the outcome, the reference against which each class is compared, as in (3).

The rows differ widely. {{fig.class.rate.First}} of the first class survived, {{fig.class.rate.Second}} of the second, {{fig.class.rate.Third}} of the third and {{fig.class.rate.Crew}} of the crew, against {{fig.data.survivedShare}} overall. Under independence the four rows of Table 3 would be equal to its last row, up to sampling variation. Figure 1 draws the same comparison.

The conditional frequencies can also be taken the other way. Table 4 gives the composition by class within each outcome. It describes who the survivors and the dead were, not how likely each class was to survive, and the two readings should not be confused: the crew make up {{fig.class.share.Crew}} of those aboard and, by Table 4, a larger share of the dead than of the survivors.

§5 Expected frequencies and the χ² statistic

Table 5 gives the frequencies expected under independence, computed from (3) with the margins of Table 1. By construction it has the same row and column totals as Table 1; it differs from it only in how those totals are spread over the cells.

Table 6 sets each observed count against its expected count and gives the contribution of the cell to (4) and its standardised residual (n_{ij} - E_{ij})/\sqrt{E_{ij}}. The residuals show where the departure from independence lies; a value beyond about \pm 2 is unusual under H_0, and these are far beyond it.

The sum of the contributions is \chi^2 = {{fig.class.chi}} on (4-1)(2-1) = {{fig.class.df}} degrees of freedom. The p-value is {{fig.class.p}}. The condition for using the \chi^2 law is met, since the smallest expected count is {{fig.class.minExpected}}, far above 5. Cramér's coefficient (5) is V = {{fig.class.v}}.

The largest departures are in the first class, with {{fig.class.top.firstObserved}} survivors against {{fig.class.top.firstExpected}} expected (residual {{fig.class.top.firstResidual}}), and {{fig.class.top.secondObserved}} deaths against {{fig.class.top.secondExpected}} expected (residual {{fig.class.top.secondResidual}}). The first class survived far more, and the third class and the crew somewhat less, than independence would predict.

§6 A permutation check

The p-value of §5 rests on an approximation, and on the idea of a random sample from a larger population, which does not quite describe a ship's passenger list. A permutation test needs neither. If class had nothing to do with the outcome, then which person carried which outcome would be arbitrary, so the {{fig.data.survived}} survivors could be reassigned at random among the {{fig.data.n}} people without changing anything that matters. The program does this {{fig.params.replicates}} times, which keeps the row totals and the column totals fixed and removes any link between class and outcome, and recomputes \chi^2 each time. The p-value is the fraction of these statistics, counting the observed one, that reach the observed value.

The permuted statistics follow the \chi^2 law closely, which supports the approximation, and none of them comes near the observed value: {{fig.class.permAtLeast}} of the {{fig.params.replicates}} reach {{fig.class.chi}}, so the permutation p-value is {{fig.class.permP}}, the smallest that this many permutations can resolve. The two tests agree.

§7 Interpretation

At any usual level of significance the null hypothesis of independence is rejected: class and outcome are statistically dependent. In terms of (3), the conditional distribution of the outcome is not the same in every class, and the differences in Table 3 are too large to be the work of chance alone.

Two cautions apply to how far this goes. The p-value says that the variables are not independent; it does not say that the association is strong, and for a large sample it would reject even a faint one. That is the role of V, and a comparison helps to read it. Table 7 repeats the whole calculation for the sex of the person aboard, taken from the same list, in a second table with two rows.

Both pairs are dependent, but not equally. Sex is the stronger association, with V = {{fig.sex.v}} against {{fig.class.v}} for class: {{fig.sex.rate.Female}} of the {{fig.sex.n.Female}} women survived against {{fig.sex.rate.Male}} of the {{fig.sex.n.Male}} men. A V near 0.3 is a moderate association: knowing the class helps to predict the outcome, but leaves most of it unexplained.

The second caution concerns the sampling. The {{fig.data.n}} people are the whole population of the ship, not a random sample of it, so the p-value does not describe repeated sampling from a larger population. What the permutation argument of §6 does support is the statement that a separation this large would almost never arise if the outcomes were assigned to people at random.

§8 Association and causality

A test of independence establishes an association: knowing X changes what we should expect of F. It does not establish that X causes F, for three reasons that this very table illustrates.

  • Confounding. A third variable may drive both. The classes differ in sex and age as well as in price: the crew were almost all men (Dawson, 1995), and Table 7 shows that sex is strongly associated with the outcome, so part of what Table 3 attributes to class may be an effect of sex. A causal statement about class would require comparing people of the same sex and age in different classes, that is, a table for each stratum, which the margins used here cannot provide.
  • Mechanisms. Even an association that survives such adjustment does not say how it arises. The location of the cabins, the order in which the lifeboats were filled, the instructions given to passengers and the roles of the crew are all plausible routes, and a contingency table of two variables cannot tell them apart.
  • Direction and aggregation. The statistic is symmetric in X and F: swapping the roles of the variables changes nothing, so the test cannot say which way an effect runs. Moreover an association seen in the aggregate table can weaken, vanish or reverse within the strata of a third variable (Simpson's paradox), so the aggregate association is not a safe guide to what would happen to an individual.

In this case the physical order of events makes the direction clear, since a person's class was fixed before the sinking; but that is knowledge about the setting, not a result of the test.

§9 Conclusion

From the records of the {{fig.data.n}} people aboard, the contingency table of class by outcome was constructed, together with its joint, marginal and conditional frequencies and the frequencies expected under independence. The statistic is \chi^2 = {{fig.class.chi}} on {{fig.class.df}} degrees of freedom, with p-value {{fig.class.p}}, and a permutation test over {{fig.params.replicates}} shuffles agrees. Class and outcome are therefore statistically dependent: the first class survived at {{fig.class.rate.First}}, the third class and the crew at about a quarter. The association is moderate (V = {{fig.class.v}}) and weaker than that of sex with the outcome (V = {{fig.sex.v}}). It is an association, not a demonstration that class caused survival, because sex, age and the circumstances of the sinking are not controlled for.
