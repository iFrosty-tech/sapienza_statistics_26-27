§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Consider n systems and m attackers. Model the penetration process using a stochastic process with independent and identically distributed increments X₁, X₂, …, representing the penetration score at each step. Consider two possible models for the score:

  • Bernoulli model: Xᵢ ∈ {0,1}, with P(Xᵢ = 1) = p.
  • Rademacher model: Xᵢ ∈ {−1,+1}, with P(Xᵢ = +1) = P(Xᵢ = −1) = 1/2. This second model is introduced to study the symmetric, zero-mean case.

For increasing values of n and suitable choices of m, simulate multiple sample trajectories and study the distributions of the following three quantities:

  • Total score: Sₙ = ∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ
  • Average score: Sₙ/n = (1/n)∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ
  • Normalized total score: Sₙ/√n = (1/√n)∑ᵢ₌₁ⁿ Xᵢ

Compare the behavior of these quantities as n increases. In particular, investigate the convergence in probability of Sₙ/n according to the Law of Large Numbers, the behavior of the variance under the different normalizations, and the convergence in distribution of Sₙ/√n to a standard normal distribution in the Rademacher case, according to the Central Limit Theorem. Interpret the simulated trajectories in the context of the n systems and m attackers, discussing how the system size and number of attackers affect the aggregate penetration score.

The assignment fixes the law of one step and leaves open how the n systems and the m attackers enter the process. This page reads it as follows. Each attacker is one sample trajectory: attacker j attacks the n systems in sequence and X_i^{(j)} is its score on system i, so that

S_n^{(j)} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i^{(j)}, \qquad j = 1, \dots, m ,
(1)

is its penetration score after n systems, and the X_i^{(j)} are independent across both steps and attackers. The number of systems n is then the length of a trajectory, the horizon of the process, and the number of attackers m the number of independent trajectories: the Monte Carlo sample from which the law of S_n is estimated, and the population over which aggregates such as the total score of all attackers or the score of the best of them are taken. Quantitative models of the intrusion process treat the successive breaches achieved by attackers as a stochastic process of this kind [6]; the increments of the assignment are its simplest instance, one independent trial per system. The study follows {{fig.params.m}} attackers over up to {{fig.params.nMax}} systems in each model.

The page is organised as follows. §2 derives the exact law of S_n and the moments of the three quantities; §3 shows sample trajectories; §4 the laws of the three quantities as n grows; §5 the variance under the three normalisations; §6 the law of large numbers and §7 the central limit theorem, each checked against the exact law and against the classical bounds; §8 turns to the roles of n and m, and §9 discusses the results. In both models the average score converges in probability to its mean, with a deviation probability that falls exponentially in n; the variance of S_n, S_n/n and S_n/\sqrt{n} grows, decays and stays constant as n, 1/n and 1; and in the Rademacher model the law of S_n/\sqrt{n} approaches the standard normal law at the rate n^{-1/2}, the Kolmogorov distance falling from {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10.exact}} at n = 10 to {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.exact}} at n = 10,000. Every number on the page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds listed in the appendix.

§2 The model

2.1 Increments

Both increments take one of two values and can be written as an affine image of a Bernoulli variable:

\begin{gathered} X_i = a + (b - a)\,B_i, \quad B_i \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(q), \\ \mu = \mathbb{E}[X_i] = a + (b - a)\,q, \quad \sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = (b - a)^2\,q\,(1 - q), \end{gathered}
(2)

with a = 0, b = 1, q = p in the Bernoulli model, so that \mu = p and \sigma^2 = p(1 - p), and a = -1, b = 1, q = 1/2 in the Rademacher model, so that \mu = 0 and \sigma^2 = 1.

The two models describe two different penetration scores. In the Bernoulli model X_i = 1 records that the attacker breaches system i and X_i = 0 that it fails, so S_n counts the systems penetrated and p is the probability of breaching any one of them. The score can only grow, and it grows on average by p per system. In the Rademacher model each system moves the score one step up or down with equal probability: a gain on one system can be lost on the next, as when a foothold is detected and removed. Its increment has mean zero, so S_n is the symmetric simple random walk [4, Ch. III]: whatever growth it shows is fluctuation, not drift. This is why the assignment introduces it for the zero-mean case: there S_n/\sqrt{n} is already the standardised sum, centred and of unit variance, and the central limit theorem applies to it as it stands. The study takes p = {{fig.params.p}}, so that \mu = {{fig.theory.bernoulli.mean}} and \sigma^2 = {{fig.theory.bernoulli.variance}}: a success rate low enough for the increment to be visibly skewed, with a standardised third absolute moment \rho/\sigma^3 = {{fig.theory.bernoulli.rho}} against {{fig.theory.rademacher.rho}} for the symmetric increment, and high enough for every system count of the study to contain many successes.

2.2 The exact law of the total score

Summing (2) over the n systems gives the total score as an affine image of a binomial count, whose law is therefore known exactly [4, Ch. VI]:

\begin{gathered} S_n = n a + (b - a)\,K_n, \quad K_n \sim \mathrm{Bin}(n, q), \\ \Pr\{S_n = n a + (b - a)k\} = \binom{n}{k} q^k (1 - q)^{n - k}, \quad k = 0, \dots, n . \end{gathered}
(3)

In the Bernoulli model S_n = K_n itself; in the Rademacher model S_n = 2K_n - n takes the values -n, -n + 2, \dots, n, all of the parity of n. Every simulated distribution on this page is set against (3), so that the study separates what the simulation shows from what it can only estimate, and every exact probability is a finite sum of binomial terms rather than an approximation.

2.3 Moments of the three quantities

By linearity of the expectation and independence of the increments,

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[S_n] &= n\mu, & \mathrm{Var}(S_n) &= n\sigma^2, \\ \mathbb{E}[S_n/n] &= \mu, & \mathrm{Var}(S_n/n) &= \sigma^2/n, \\ \mathbb{E}[S_n/\sqrt{n}] &= \sqrt{n}\,\mu, & \mathrm{Var}(S_n/\sqrt{n}) &= \sigma^2 . \end{aligned}
(4)

The three quantities are S_n/n^{\alpha} with \alpha = 0, 1 and 1/2, and their variance is \sigma^2 n^{1 - 2\alpha}: it diverges for \alpha < 1/2, vanishes for \alpha > 1/2 and stays finite and positive only at \alpha = 1/2. Division by \sqrt{n} is therefore the only scaling under which the fluctuations of the score neither explode nor disappear. It fixes the spread but not the location: the mean \sqrt{n}\,\mu of S_n/\sqrt{n} still diverges unless \mu = 0, which is the second reason why the Rademacher model is the natural setting for the central limit theorem.

§4 Distributions as n grows

Figures 2 and 3 show the law of the three quantities after n = {{fig.params.distributionN}} systems, for the same {{fig.params.m}} attackers continuing on more systems. Within each column the horizontal axis is common to all rows, so that the spread of each quantity can be compared across n.

The three columns answer the comparison the assignment asks for. The total score moves and widens: in the Bernoulli model its law is centred at np and its variance grows from {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10.total.variance}} at n = 10 to {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10000.total.variance}} at n = 10,000, so that on a common axis the law at n = 10 is a spike at the origin and the law at n = 10,000 a wide bell; it has no limit. The average score does the opposite: its variance falls from {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10.average.variance}} to {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10000.average.variance}}, and its law shrinks onto the single point \mu, the degenerate limit of the law of large numbers. The normalised score keeps its variance, {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10.normalized.variance}} at n = 10 and {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10000.normalized.variance}} at n = 10,000 against \sigma^2 = {{fig.theory.bernoulli.variance}}, and only its shape changes: the eleven atoms of the skewed binomial law at n = 10 merge into a symmetric bell. In the Rademacher model the same happens around zero, and the bell of S_n/\sqrt{n} is the standard normal density itself; in the Bernoulli model it is the same bell, displaced by p\sqrt{n}, which §7.2 takes up.

Table 1 sets the sample moments against (4). Each estimate is given with its standard error, s/\sqrt{m} for the mean and \sqrt{(\hat\mu_4 - s^4)/m} for the variance, where \hat\mu_4 is the sample fourth central moment.

Every one of the {{fig.moments.count}} estimates of Table 1 lies within {{fig.moments.maxAbsZ}} standard errors of its theoretical value. The three quantities of a row are exact rescalings of one sample, so their agreement with (4) is a single check made three times: what changes from column to column is only the power of n by which the same variance is divided. The rows are not independent either, since the attackers at n = 10,000 are those at n = 1,000 continuing on more systems, which is why the deviations persist from one row to the next rather than scatter.

§5 Variance under the three normalisations

Dividing S_n by n^{\alpha} multiplies its variance by n^{-2\alpha}:

\begin{gathered} \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{S_n}{n^{\alpha}}\right) = \sigma^2\, n^{1 - 2\alpha}, \\ \log_{10} \mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{S_n}{n^{\alpha}}\right) = \log_{10}\sigma^2 + (1 - 2\alpha)\log_{10} n , \end{gathered}
(5)

a straight line of slope +1, -1 and 0 on log–log axes for \alpha = 0, 1 and 1/2, with the common intercept \log_{10}\sigma^2. Figure 4 plots the sample variances of the {{fig.params.m}} attackers at the {{fig.params.curveCount}} values n = {{fig.params.curveN}}; Table 2 gives the least-squares slopes and constants.

The fitted slopes are {{fig.variance.bernoulli.total.slope}}, {{fig.variance.bernoulli.average.slope}} and {{fig.variance.bernoulli.normalized.slope}} in the Bernoulli model and {{fig.variance.rademacher.total.slope}}, {{fig.variance.rademacher.average.slope}} and {{fig.variance.rademacher.normalized.slope}} in the Rademacher model, and the fitted constants {{fig.variance.bernoulli.total.constant}} and {{fig.variance.rademacher.total.constant}} recover \sigma^2 = {{fig.theory.bernoulli.variance}} and {{fig.theory.rademacher.variance}}. The three fits of a model share one intercept and their slopes differ by exactly one, because the three sample variances at each n are one variance divided by 1, n^2 and n; the only random element is the relative error of the sample variance, of order \sqrt{2/m}, and it is shared by the three lines.

The three regimes are the three answers to the question the assignment asks about the variance. The variance of the total diverges, so S_n has no limit law and the uncertainty about an attacker's total grows without bound. The variance of the average vanishes, so S_n/n converges to the constant \mu: this is the law of large numbers of §6, and \sigma^2/n is the rate behind it. The variance of the normalised score stays at \sigma^2, so S_n/\sqrt{n}, once centred, can converge to a non-degenerate law: this is the scale of the central limit theorem of §7.

§6 Law of large numbers

A sequence of random variables Y_n converges in probability to a constant c if \Pr\{|Y_n - c| \ge \varepsilon\} \to 0 as n \to \infty for every \varepsilon > 0. The weak law of large numbers states that the average score converges in probability to \mu [4, Ch. X; 2, Ch. 2]. For increments of finite variance the proof takes one line: since \mathbb{E}[S_n/n] = \mu and \mathrm{Var}(S_n/n) = \sigma^2/n by (4), Chebyshev's inequality gives

\Pr\left\{\left|\frac{S_n}{n} - \mu\right| \ge \varepsilon\right\} \le \frac{\mathrm{Var}(S_n/n)}{\varepsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} 0 .
(6)

Chebyshev's bound uses only the variance and decays as 1/n. For increments confined to [a, b], Hoeffding's inequality [5, Theorem 2] uses the bounded range and decays exponentially:

\Pr\left\{\left|\frac{S_n}{n} - \mu\right| \ge \varepsilon\right\} \le 2\exp\!\left(-\frac{2n\varepsilon^2}{(b - a)^2}\right) .
(7)

Neither bound is needed here to know the probability, which (3) gives exactly as a sum over the counts k with |a + (b - a)k/n - \mu| \ge \varepsilon. Figure 5 sets the exact probability against the two bounds and against the proportion of the {{fig.params.m}} attackers whose average deviates by at least \varepsilon, for \varepsilon = {{fig.params.eps1}} and {{fig.params.eps2}}; each proportion carries its Wilson 95% interval [9].

The exact curve falls far faster than Chebyshev's line. A natural way to read the convergence is the number of systems after which a deviation of \varepsilon has probability at most {{fig.params.llnLevel}}. In the Bernoulli model with \varepsilon = {{fig.params.eps1}} the exact probability stays below that level from n = {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.nExact}}; Hoeffding's bound guarantees it only from n = {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.nHoeffding}} and Chebyshev's from n = {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.nChebyshev}}. With \varepsilon = {{fig.params.eps2}} the three numbers become {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps5.nExact}}, {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps5.nHoeffding}} and {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps5.nChebyshev}}: halving the tolerance multiplies the number of systems by about four, as the \varepsilon^2 in both bounds predicts. At n = 1,000 the exact probability is {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.n1000.exact}}, Hoeffding's bound {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.n1000.hoeffding}} and Chebyshev's {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.n1000.chebyshev}}: Hoeffding captures the exponential decay but with a smaller exponent, and Chebyshev's bound, valid for any increment of the same variance, is useless at this range. The Rademacher model converges more slowly at the same tolerance, from n = {{fig.lln.rademacher.eps10.nExact}} for \varepsilon = {{fig.params.eps1}}, because its increment has variance {{fig.theory.rademacher.variance}} against {{fig.theory.bernoulli.variance}} and a range of two units against one.

At small n the exact curve is not monotone. The average score lives on the lattice a + (b - a)k/n, and whether a lattice point falls inside the tolerance depends on n: in the Rademacher model with n odd, |S_n| \ge 1 forces |S_n/n| \ge 1/n, and the probability is one for every odd n \le 1/\varepsilon. The simulated proportions follow the exact curve as long as the probability is large compared with 1/m. Where it is not, the simulation runs out of resolution: at n = 1,000 with \varepsilon = {{fig.params.eps1}} none of the {{fig.params.m}} Bernoulli attackers deviates, and the data only say that the probability is below the upper Wilson limit {{fig.lln.bernoulli.eps10.n1000.high}}, far above the exact value. In the Rademacher model at the same n, {{fig.lln.rademacher.eps10.n1000.count}} attackers deviate and the interval {{fig.lln.rademacher.eps10.n1000.low}}–{{fig.lln.rademacher.eps10.n1000.high}} misses the exact {{fig.lln.rademacher.eps10.n1000.exact}}; with 95% intervals at {{fig.params.curveCount}} values of n in four panels a few misses are expected, and they are not independent, since the same attackers are followed from one n to the next. Below 1/m the exact law of (3), not the simulation, carries the evidence.

§7 Central limit theorem

7.1 The Rademacher case

The Lindeberg–Lévy central limit theorem states that if X_1, X_2, \dots are independent and identically distributed with mean \mu and finite variance \sigma^2 > 0, then for every x \Pr\{(S_n - n\mu)/(\sigma\sqrt{n}) \le x\} \to \Phi(x), the standard normal distribution function [2, Ch. 3]; for a binomial count it is the de Moivre–Laplace theorem [4, Ch. VII]. In the Rademacher model \mu = 0 and \sigma = 1, so the standardised sum is the normalised score itself and S_n/\sqrt{n} \to N(0, 1) in distribution. The rate is measured by the Kolmogorov distance, which the Berry–Esseen inequality bounds [1]:

D_n = \sup_{x}\left|\Pr\left\{\frac{S_n - n\mu}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \le x\right\} - \Phi(x)\right| \le \frac{C_0\,\rho}{\sigma^3\sqrt{n}}, \qquad \rho = \mathbb{E}|X_1 - \mu|^3 ,
(8)

where the best known value of the absolute constant for identically distributed summands is C_0 < {{fig.params.berryEsseenConstant}} [8]; the bound drawn on this page uses that value. A simulation estimates D_n only up to its own sampling error: by the Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz inequality with Massart's constant [3; 7], the empirical distribution function \hat F_m of m independent attackers satisfies

\Pr\left\{\sup_x |\hat F_m(x) - F(x)| > t\right\} \le 2e^{-2mt^2}, \qquad t_\alpha = \sqrt{\frac{\ln(2/\alpha)}{2m}} ,
(9)

so that with probability at least 1 - \alpha it lies within t_\alpha of the true F: {{fig.clt.dkw}} for m = {{fig.params.m}} at the {{fig.params.dkwLevel}} level. Below this floor the simulation cannot resolve D_n, and the exact law (3) takes over.

Figure 6 shows the convergence on the paper of this site, where a normal sample falls on a straight line. At n = 10 the normalised score takes eleven values, 2/\sqrt{n} apart, and its quantiles form a staircase around the line, with a probability-plot correlation of {{fig.clt.plot.n10.r}}; at n = 100 the steps have narrowed to a ripple ({{fig.clt.plot.n100.r}}), and at n = 1,000 the points lie on the line ({{fig.clt.plot.n1000.r}}), with sample mean {{fig.clt.plot.n1000.mean}} and standard deviation {{fig.clt.plot.n1000.sd}}.

The exact distance of the Rademacher S_n/\sqrt{n} falls from {{fig.clt.rademacher.n1.exact}} at n = 1 to {{fig.clt.rademacher.n100.exact}} at n = 100 and {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.exact}} at n = 10,000, with a fitted log–log slope of {{fig.clt.rademacher.slopeExact}}: the rate n^{-1/2} of (8), always below the bound, which is {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.berryEsseen}} at n = 10,000. The bound is nearly attained, and the reason is the lattice. For even n the walk returns to zero with probability \Pr\{S_n = 0\} = \binom{n}{n/2}2^{-n} \sim \sqrt{2/(\pi n)} [4, Ch. III], and by symmetry its distribution function jumps at zero from (1 - \Pr\{S_n = 0\})/2 to (1 + \Pr\{S_n = 0\})/2, across \Phi(0) = 1/2. No continuous law can be closer than half the jump, so D_n \ge \Pr\{S_n = 0\}/2 \sim 1/\sqrt{2\pi n}. Table 4 shows that the whole distance is this central atom: \sqrt{n}\,D_n is {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10.scaled}} at n = 10, {{fig.clt.rademacher.n100.scaled}} at n = 100 and {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.scaled}} at n = 10,000, against 1/\sqrt{2\pi} = {{fig.theory.latticeConstant}}. For a lattice increment the rate n^{-1/2} cannot be improved, and the Berry–Esseen constant {{fig.params.berryEsseenConstant}} exceeds the attained {{fig.theory.latticeConstant}} by less than a fifth.

The simulated distance follows the exact one only down to the Monte Carlo floor. The exact distance falls below the DKW radius {{fig.clt.dkw}} of {{fig.params.m}} attackers from about n = {{fig.clt.rademacher.floorN}}; beyond, the simulated distance stops decreasing and measures the sampling error of the empirical distribution function rather than the error of the normal approximation: {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.empirical}} at n = 10,000 against an exact {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.exact}}. Even between n = 10 and n = 100 the floor flattens the fitted slope of the simulated distances to {{fig.clt.rademacher.slopeEmpirical}}. This is the first role of m: it does not change the law that is being approximated, only how small a distance the simulation can see, and since the floor shrinks as 1/\sqrt{m}, halving it takes four times as many attackers.

7.2 The Bernoulli case

In the Bernoulli model S_n/\sqrt{n} has mean p\sqrt{n}, which grows without bound, so it has no limit law; it is the standardised score (S_n - np)/\sqrt{np(1 - p)} that converges to N(0, 1), more slowly the more skewed the increment. Figure 8 shows both.

The contrast is complete. The mean of S_n/\sqrt{n} is {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n100.drift}} at n = 100 and {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n10000.drift}} at n = 10,000, and its distance to N(0, 1) rises from {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n1.raw}} at n = 1 to {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n100.raw}} at n = 100 and {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n10000.raw}} at n = 10,000: the law escapes to infinity while keeping its shape. The standardised score converges instead, from {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n10.exact}} at n = 10 to {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n10000.exact}} at n = 10,000, with an exact slope of {{fig.clt.bernoulli.slopeExact}}, and its simulated distances agree with the exact ones down to the same floor. Skewness slows the convergence. The Berry–Esseen ratio \rho/\sigma^3 is {{fig.theory.rademacher.rho}} for the Rademacher increment, {{fig.theory.bernoulli.rho}} for p = {{fig.params.p}} and {{fig.theory.bernoulliSmall.rho}} for p = {{fig.params.pSmall}}, and the exact distances follow the same order: at n = 100, {{fig.clt.rademacher.n100.exact}}, {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n100.exact}} and {{fig.clt.bernoulliSmall.n100.exact}}, and at n = 10,000, {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.exact}}, {{fig.clt.bernoulli.n10000.exact}} and {{fig.clt.bernoulliSmall.n10000.exact}}. All three fall as n^{-1/2}, with fitted slopes {{fig.clt.rademacher.slopeExact}}, {{fig.clt.bernoulli.slopeExact}} and {{fig.clt.bernoulliSmall.slopeExact}}; the skewed increment pays a larger constant, not a slower rate. A rare event, such as the breach of a system with p = {{fig.params.pSmall}}, needs many more systems before the normal approximation of its count can be trusted.

§8 Systems and attackers

8.1 The number of attackers as the size of the sample

Under the reading of §1 the number of attackers m does not change the law of any attacker's score; it sets how precisely the simulation estimates that law. To show this at n = {{fig.params.precisionN}}, the attackers are split into disjoint groups of m = {{fig.params.groupSizes}}, and each group estimates the mean average score and the distribution function of S_n.

The root-mean-square error of the group mean matches the standard error \sigma/\sqrt{nm}: their ratio is {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m10.ratio}} for groups of ten, averaged over {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m10.groups}} groups, and between {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m1000.ratio}} and {{fig.precision.rademacher.m1000.ratio}} for groups of a thousand, where only {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m1000.groups}} groups are available and the error is itself estimated from ten numbers. The distribution function behaves in the same way: the median distance falls from {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m10.medianD}} for ten attackers to {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m1000.medianD}} for a thousand, always below the DKW radius, which only {{fig.precision.bernoulli.m10.shareAbove}} of the groups of ten exceed, within the 5% the inequality allows; for a lattice law the band is conservative. Both errors fall as 1/\sqrt{m}. With all {{fig.params.m}} attackers at n = 10,000 the standard error of the mean average score is {{fig.moments.bernoulli.n10000.average.seMean}}, the precision with which §4 confirms \mu.

8.2 The aggregate penetration score

The aggregate score of m attackers over n systems, T = \sum_{j=1}^{m} S_n^{(j)}, is a sum of mn independent increments, so its law depends on m and n only through their product:

\begin{gathered} \mathbb{E}[T] = mn\mu, \qquad \mathrm{Var}(T) = mn\sigma^2, \\ T = mna + (b - a)\,K, \quad K \sim \mathrm{Bin}(mn, q) . \end{gathered}
(10)

Table 6 spends the same budget of {{fig.aggregate.budget}} attacks in four ways, from a thousand attackers on ten systems each to a single attacker on {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1.n}} systems. In the Bernoulli model the group means are {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1000.mean}}, {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m100.mean}}, {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m10.mean}} and {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1.mean}} against {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1.theoryMean}}: for the aggregate it makes no difference whether many attackers try few systems or few attackers try many. The sample variances scatter more, from {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1000.variance}} to {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1.variance}} against {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1.theoryVariance}}, because the first rows rest on only {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m1000.groups}} and {{fig.aggregate.bernoulli.m100.groups}} groups. Over all {{fig.params.m}} attackers and {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.n}} systems the Bernoulli aggregate is {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.value}} against an expected {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.mean}} with standard deviation {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.sd}} (z = {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.z}}). Per attacker and system this is {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.perSystem}}, with a standard deviation of {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.perSystemSd}}: the relative spread \sigma/(\mu\sqrt{mn}) of the aggregate is {{fig.aggregate.total.bernoulli.relSd}}, and it falls with both the system size and the number of attackers. 8.3 The best attacker

The aggregate hides the extremes, and for a defender the extremes matter. In the Bernoulli model S_n counts the systems an attacker penetrates, and the probability that at least one of m independent attackers penetrates at least a share p + \delta of them is

\Pr\left\{\max_{1 \le j \le m} S_n^{(j)} \ge \lceil (p + \delta)\,n \rceil\right\} = 1 - \Pr\{S_n < \lceil (p + \delta)\,n \rceil\}^{m} ,
(11)

with \delta = {{fig.best.excess}}, a share of {{fig.best.share}}. Figure 10 plots (11) against m and Table 7 gives the number of attackers for an even chance.

One attacker penetrates {{fig.best.share}} of the systems with probability {{fig.best.n10.p1}} when there are ten of them, {{fig.best.n100.p1}} when there are a hundred and {{fig.best.n1000.p1}} when there are a thousand. The number of attackers that gives an even chance that one of them succeeds grows accordingly: {{fig.best.n10.m50}} for ten systems, {{fig.best.n100.m50}} for a hundred, {{fig.best.n500.m50}} for five hundred and {{fig.best.n1000.m50}} for a thousand. The growth is exponential, at the large-deviation rate of the average score [2, Ch. 2]:

\begin{gathered} -\frac{1}{n}\ln \Pr\left\{\frac{S_n}{n} \ge p + \delta\right\} \longrightarrow D(p + \delta \,\|\, p), \\ D(p + \delta \,\|\, p) = (p + \delta)\ln\frac{p + \delta}{p} + (1 - p - \delta)\ln\frac{1 - p - \delta}{1 - p} , \end{gathered}
(12)

the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the Bernoulli law of parameter p + \delta from that of parameter p, here {{fig.best.klRate}}. The finite-n rates of Table 7 decrease towards it, {{fig.best.n100.rate}} at n = 100 and {{fig.best.n1000.rate}} at n = 1,000, and stay above Hoeffding's one-sided exponent 2\delta^2 = {{fig.best.hoeffdingRate}}, which is a lower bound for it by (7). Since m_{1/2} \approx \ln 2/\Pr\{S_n \ge \lceil (p + \delta)n\rceil\} for a small probability, m_{1/2} grows like e^{nD} up to a factor polynomial in n: every hundred further systems multiply the number of attackers needed by a factor that approaches e^{100D} = {{fig.best.mGrowth}}. The simulation confirms (11) wherever it can resolve it: of {{fig.best.empirical.n100.m10.groups}} groups of ten attackers on a hundred systems, a proportion {{fig.best.empirical.n100.m10.rate}} had a member reaching the share, against an exact {{fig.best.empirical.n100.m10.exact}}.

§9 Discussion

The three quantities answer three different questions about the same attackers. The total score measures how much an attacker has achieved, the average score its rate of success per system, and the normalised score the size of its fluctuation relative to the typical one. As n grows the first becomes less predictable in absolute terms, the second becomes certain, and the third keeps the same uncertainty and, once centred, the same normal shape (§4–§7).

The system size n sets the scale of the score. The expected total grows as n\mu and its fluctuations as \sigma\sqrt{n}, so the relative spread of a single attacker's total falls as 1/\sqrt{n}: on many systems the share an attacker penetrates becomes predictable, and a deviation of a fixed share becomes exponentially unlikely (§6). In the symmetric model there is no drift to dominate the fluctuations: the total wanders on the scale \sqrt{n}, crosses zero again and again, and its size reflects how long the attacker has been playing rather than how good it is.

The number of attackers m leaves the law of every attacker unchanged and acts in two other ways. It sets what can be seen: the standard error of every estimate falls as 1/\sqrt{m}, and the DKW radius sets a floor below which no simulated distribution can be resolved, which is why the simulated Kolmogorov distance of §7 stops falling near n = {{fig.clt.rademacher.floorN}}. And it drives the aggregates over attackers. Their sum has mean mn\mu and standard deviation \sigma\sqrt{mn}, so the aggregate penetration score is the most predictable quantity of all and depends on m and n only through their product (§8.2). Their maximum behaves in the opposite way: the probability that some attacker penetrates an unusually large share of the systems rises with m, but the number of attackers needed for it grows exponentially with n, at the rate {{fig.best.klRate}} per system (§8.3). In this model the exceptional attacker becomes exponentially rarer as the systems multiply, and only an exponentially growing number of attackers restores its chance, while the expected total damage grows linearly in both.

These conclusions rest on the assumptions of the assignment. The increments are independent across systems and attackers and identically distributed, so every system is equally vulnerable, every attacker equally capable, and nobody learns: an attacker who exploits a vulnerability shared by many systems, or who improves with experience as observed in intrusion experiments [6], produces dependent increments, and with them a variance that can grow faster than n and a law of large numbers that fails.

App. Reproducibility

Every figure, table and statistic on this page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds of Table A1, and can be recomputed by anyone from the repository: npm test runs the test suite, which covers the exact laws, the bounds, the simulation, the study and the interactive panels, and npm run build rebuilds the page. The study draws {{fig.params.draws}} pseudo-random numbers and takes about {{fig.params.computeSeconds}} seconds (last computed {{fig.params.computedAt}}); its results are cached under node_modules/.cache/hw04/ with the key {{fig.params.cacheKey}}, a hash of the parameters, of the source of every module involved and of the figure provider itself, so that a change to any of them recomputes everything.

Each run has its own stream of a seeded Mulberry32 generator, whose seed is the master seed {{fig.params.seed}} combined by exclusive or with the 32-bit FNV-1a hash of a label: {{fig.params.seeds.bernoulli}} (label scores:bernoulli) and {{fig.params.seeds.rademacher}} (scores:rademacher) for the two runs of {{fig.params.m}} attackers, and {{fig.params.seeds.trajectoriesBernoulli}} (trajectories:bernoulli) and {{fig.params.seeds.trajectoriesRademacher}} (trajectories:rademacher) for the paths of Figure 1. Within a run the attackers draw from the stream one after another, attacker j taking draws jN + 1, \dots, (j + 1)N for the largest n = N, so the scores at every smaller n are those of the same attackers and no two attackers share a draw. The generator is statistical, not cryptographic. The interactive panels start from the master seed and draw their own runs with the same generator and the same functions. The standard normal distribution function is computed through the regularised incomplete gamma function, accurate to rounding, because the smallest distances of §7 are of the order of {{fig.clt.rademacher.n10000.exact}}.
