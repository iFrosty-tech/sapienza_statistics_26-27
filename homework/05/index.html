§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Ex. 1 – Welford’s Recurrence Formulas

Derive Welford’s recurrence formulas for the sum of squares (SS) and the variance, following the same approach as Knuth’s recurrence formula for the mean.

Write the corresponding code. The core logic should require only about 3 lines.

Throughout, x_1, x_2, \dots is a sequence of observations read one at a time, and after k of them

\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i, \qquad SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2
(1)

are the sample mean and the corrected sum of squares. Evaluated directly, each definition stores every observation, and SS_k must be recomputed from scratch at every k because each new observation moves the mean around which all the earlier deviations are measured. A recurrence replaces this with a rule that turns the summary of k - 1 observations and one new value into the summary of k observations, and forgets the data.

§2 derives the recurrence for the mean. §3 applies the same approach to the sum of squares, with every algebraic step shown, and §4 deduces the forms for the variance. §5 gives the code, whose core is three lines, and traces it on a small data set. §6 reads the increment of the sum of squares as the area of a rectangle. §7 measures what the recurrence is worth numerically: against the exact value of the sum of squares, computed in rational arithmetic, the textbook formula loses every digit at a condition number of about {{fig.textbook.lostKappa}}, while the error of the recurrence grows only in proportion to that number, with a fitted log–log slope of {{fig.slope.welford}} against {{fig.slope.textbook}}. §8 discusses cost, memory and streaming, and links to the second exercise, which uses these recurrences. Every number on the page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds listed in the appendix.

§2 Knuth's recurrence for the mean

The approach has three moves: write the quantity after k observations as a sum, separate the last term, and recognise in what remains the same quantity after k - 1 observations. For the mean, multiplying (1) by k and separating the last term gives

k\,\bar{x}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i + x_k = (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k .
(2)

Dividing by k and writing (k - 1)/k = 1 - 1/k yields the recurrence presented by Knuth [4, §4.2.2]:

\bar{x}_k = \frac{k - 1}{k}\,\bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k}{k} = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}, \qquad k \ge 1 .
(3)

The initial condition is \bar{x}_1 = x_1, which is what (3) returns at k = 1 from any starting value, because the old estimate then has weight zero; taking \bar{x}_0 = 0 merely lets the same line of code handle the first observation.

The second form of (3) reads as old estimate + innovation/k. The innovation x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} is the part of the new observation that the current estimate did not predict, and 1/k is the share of it that the estimate absorbs. The role of k is exactly this gain: the first observation is taken whole, the second moves the estimate halfway towards it, and the hundredth by one hundredth. The gain falls as 1/k because every observation must end with the same weight in the mean; a constant gain would give an exponentially weighted average instead, a different estimator.

§3 Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

3.1 Derivation

The same three moves apply, once SS_k is written as a sum over which the last term can be separated. The definition (1) itself is not such a sum, because every one of its terms contains \bar{x}_k. Expanding the square and using \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i = k\,\bar{x}_k removes the difficulty:

SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i^2 - 2\bar{x}_k \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i + k\,\bar{x}_k^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(4)

Separating the last term of the sum in (4) gives

SS_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i^2 + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(5)

The identity (4) at k - 1 reads \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i^2 = SS_{k-1} + (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1}^2, which brings the previous sum of squares into (5). The increment we are after is therefore

SS_k - SS_{k-1} = (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1}^2 + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(6)

Now the recurrence for the mean enters. By (2), (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1} = k\,\bar{x}_k - x_k. Replacing one of the two factors \bar{x}_{k-1} of the first term of (6) with this expression gives

SS_k - SS_{k-1} = \bar{x}_{k-1}\,(k\,\bar{x}_k - x_k) + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(7)

Multiplying out and collecting the two terms in x_k and the two terms in k\,\bar{x}_k gives

\begin{aligned} SS_k - SS_{k-1} &= x_k^2 - x_k\,\bar{x}_{k-1} - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 + k\,\bar{x}_k\,\bar{x}_{k-1} \\ &= x_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - \bar{x}_k \cdot k\,(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) . \end{aligned}
(8)

The last bracket is the recurrence (3) again, in the form k\,(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}. Substituting it leaves the common factor x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} in both terms:

\begin{aligned} SS_k - SS_{k-1} &= x_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - \bar{x}_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) \\ &= (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) . \end{aligned}
(9)

This is the recurrence for the sum of squares:

SS_k = SS_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k), \qquad SS_1 = 0 .
(10)

Both factors are deviations of the new observation: one from the mean before it arrived and one from the mean after. Starting from SS_0 = 0 and \bar{x}_0 = 0 also gives SS_1 = 0, because at k = 1 the second factor vanishes.

3.2 An equivalent form, and a consequence

The two factors of (10) are proportional. From (3),

x_k - \bar{x}_k = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} - \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k} = \frac{k - 1}{k}\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) ,
(11)

and substituting (11) into (10) gives a form that uses the old mean alone:

SS_k = SS_{k-1} + \frac{k - 1}{k}\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2 .
(12)

The increment in (12) is a square times a non-negative factor, so the sum of squares never decreases: a new observation can only add to the total spread, and it leaves it unchanged exactly when x_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} or k = 1. The definition (1) does not show this, since each new observation changes every deviation in the sum. By (11) the same holds for the product in (10), whose two factors always share their sign. The property also matters in floating point, as §7 shows: the sum is built from non-negative terms.

3.3 Who gave which form

The recurrence is known by Welford's name, from a note of 1962 [6] on computing corrected sums of squares and products. Chan, Golub and LeVeque state that, for an unweighted sum of squares, the algorithms of Welford, of West [7] and of Hanson are virtually identical, and that those of Youngs and Cramer [8], which update the running sum rather than the running mean, are similar [2, pp. 242–243]. The product form (10) is the one usually quoted from Knuth, who credits the method to Welford [4, §4.2.2]; Ling compares several such algorithms [5]. The full texts of the note of 1962 and of Knuth's section were not available to the author, so this paragraph reports them through the sources just named and does not claim which algebraic form Welford's note prints. The derivation above is independent of those texts, and each of its results is checked numerically in the test suite of the repository.

§4 The variance

The sample variance of k \ge 2 observations, the estimator that is unbiased for the variance of the population, is

s_k^2 = \frac{SS_k}{k - 1}, \qquad k \ge 2 .
(13)

Dividing (12) by k - 1 and writing SS_{k-1} = (k - 2)\,s_{k-1}^2 gives a recurrence in the variance alone:

s_k^2 = \frac{k - 2}{k - 1}\,s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2}{k}, \qquad k \ge 2 .
(14)

At k = 2 the coefficient of s_1^2 is zero, so the undefined s_1^2 is never needed and (14) gives s_2^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2/2. The form that mirrors Knuth's recurrence starts from (10) instead. Dividing SS_k = (k - 2)\,s_{k-1}^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) by k - 1 and writing (k - 2)/(k - 1) = 1 - 1/(k - 1) gives

s_k^2 = s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - s_{k-1}^2}{k - 1}, \qquad k \ge 2,
(15)

with the convention s_1^2 = 0 at k = 2. This is again old estimate + innovation/step: the product of the two deviations plays the part of one new squared deviation, the innovation is its distance from the current variance, and the gain is 1/(k - 1) because the variance has k - 1 degrees of freedom. The estimate rises when the new observation is more surprising than the variance predicts and falls when it is less so, even though SS_k itself never falls. The same steps with the divisor k give the variance of the data themselves, \hat{\sigma}_k^2 = SS_k/k:

\hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2}{k}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_0^2 = 0 .
(16)

§5 The code

Listing 1 is cut from the file that runs, src/lib/welford.js, when the page is built, so the code printed here cannot drift from the code that the second exercise calls. The state is three numbers: the count k, the mean and the sum of squares. The marked lines are the core logic: the innovation, the recurrence (3) and the recurrence (10). The variance (13) is one division away.

The order of the lines is part of the algorithm. The innovation is saved before the mean moves, because it is the deviation from the old mean; the last line then reads the updated mean, so that x - state.mean is the deviation from the new one. Exchanging the last two lines would multiply the innovation by itself and overstate every increment by the factor k/(k - 1).

§6 A geometric reading

The increment in (10) is a product of two lengths, so it can be drawn as an area. Take a square plane with the data on both axes, at the same scale, and mark on its diagonal the point (x_k, x_k). The rectangle with opposite corners (\bar{x}_{k-1}, \bar{x}_k) and (x_k, x_k) has sides x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} and x_k - \bar{x}_k, and its area is what the observation adds to the sum of squares.

Three results of §3 can be read from this drawing. The two sides have the same sign, so the rectangle always lies on one side of the diagonal and its area is never negative: the sum of squares never decreases. By (11) the height is (k - 1)/k of the width, so the rectangle approaches a square as k grows, and (12) states its area through the width alone. And the mean moves towards the observation by 1/k of the width, which is the difference between the two sides.

Panel 1 is interactive and needs scripting. It steps through a sequence of up to sixteen values, editable or drawn from a seed, and for each observation shows the previous mean, the new observation, the new mean, the rectangle whose area is added and the running sum of squares as accumulated area. On the data of Table 1, step 2 moves the mean from {{fig.trace.step2.old}} to {{fig.trace.step2.mean}} and adds an area of {{fig.trace.step2.area}}, and step 8 moves it from {{fig.trace.step8.old}} to {{fig.trace.step8.mean}} and brings the sum of squares to {{fig.trace.step8.ss}}; Figure 1 prints these steps.

§7 Numerical behaviour

7.1 Three algorithms for one quantity

In exact arithmetic the recurrence and the definition are the same number. In floating point they are not. Three algorithms are compared here, in the terminology of Chan, Golub and LeVeque [2]: the textbook one-pass formula (4) evaluated as \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2/n, the two-pass algorithm, which computes the mean and then sums the squared deviations of (1), and Welford's updating recurrence (10), which is the code of Listing 1.

The textbook formula subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers. When the spread of the data is small compared with their size, most of the digits of the two terms agree and cancel, and what is left is dominated by the rounding errors made while accumulating them. The sensitivity of the problem is measured by the condition number [2, §2]

\kappa = \frac{\lVert x \rVert_2}{\sqrt{SS}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{n\,\bar{x}^2}{SS}} ,
(17)

the second equality being (4) divided by SS. It satisfies \kappa \ge 1, equals one when the mean is zero, and for a small spread is close to the absolute mean divided by the standard deviation.

7.2 The exact value

A measurement of rounding error needs a reference free of rounding error, and a higher-precision run only moves the problem. Here the reference is exact. Every finite double is a dyadic rational m\,2^{e}, so a sample can be scaled to a common power of two and written as integers M_i, with x_i = M_i\,2^{e}. Then

SS = \frac{n \sum_i M_i^2 - \bigl(\sum_i M_i\bigr)^2}{n}\; 2^{2e}
(18)

is evaluated in arbitrary-precision integers, with no rounding at all. The relative error of a computed value is itself a difference of rationals, evaluated exactly and rounded once at the end, to the nearest double, so that nothing in the measurement is limited by the arithmetic being measured.

7.3 The experiment

Each sample holds n = {{fig.params.n}} values x_i = c + z_i, the z_i being seeded standard normal draws. The shift c leaves the spread alone and raises the mean, so sweeping it sweeps the condition number: \kappa \approx c for c \gg 1. The same {{fig.params.replicates}} samples of z are used at each of the {{fig.params.shiftCount}} shifts, and the rounding of c + z_i belongs to the data, since the double that is stored is the observation. Figure 2 shows the median and the worst relative error of each algorithm.

On unshifted data the three algorithms are indistinguishable, near {{fig.at.k0.textbookMedian}}. With the shift they separate at once. At \kappa = {{fig.at.k4.kappa}} the median error of the textbook formula is {{fig.at.k4.textbookMedian}}, about {{fig.at.k4.digits}} correct digits, against {{fig.at.k4.welfordMedian}} for the recurrence and {{fig.at.k4.twoPassMedian}} for the two-pass algorithm. At {{fig.at.k7.kappa}} the textbook median is {{fig.at.k7.textbookMedian}}, and from {{fig.textbook.lostKappa}} it exceeds one, so that no digit is correct. The formula also returns an impossible value, a negative sum of squares, first at {{fig.textbook.firstNegativeKappa}}: {{fig.at.k8.negative}} of the samples at {{fig.at.k8.kappa}} and {{fig.textbook.negative}} results in the whole sweep. 7.4 Measured slopes and published bounds

Chan, Golub and LeVeque tabulate bounds on the relative error, keeping only the dominant terms and suppressing small constant factors [2, Table 1]: n\kappa^2 u for the textbook algorithm, n\kappa u for updating, and nu + n^2\kappa^2 u^2 for the two-pass algorithm, whose second term, derived in their earlier report [1], is the reason the algorithm is not simply independent of \kappa. Table 4 sets these exponents against the slopes of the medians on the logarithmic axes of Figure 2.

The measured slopes agree with the exponents: {{fig.slope.textbook}} against two for the textbook formula, and {{fig.slope.welford}} against one for the recurrence. The two-pass error is flat, with slope {{fig.slope.twoPassFlat}}, until the second-order term takes over near the end of the sweep, where the slope is {{fig.slope.twoPassTail}} over the last few points, too few to count as a measurement of the exponent. The bounds are generous, as bounds should be: over the informative part of the sweep the worst error reached at most {{fig.ratio.textbook}} of the textbook bound, {{fig.ratio.welford}} of the updating bound and {{fig.ratio.twoPass}} of the two-pass bound. The same comparison explains the ranking in Figure 2 and adds a qualification. The two-pass algorithm is the more accurate of the two stable ones, as its bound and Higham's discussion of these formulae say [3, Ch. 1], but it needs the data twice. The error of the recurrence grows with \kappa where the first-order error of the two-pass algorithm does not, and that is the price of a single pass.

7.5 The limit of the argument

Stability is not the reason to use the recurrence in the second exercise. Its scores X = B\,Y have mean \mu = {{fig.scores.mean}} and variance \sigma^2 = {{fig.scores.variance}}, so by (17) their condition number is close to \sqrt{1 + \mu^2/\sigma^2} = {{fig.scores.kappaTheory}}, and the median over {{fig.params.scoreReplicates}} simulated samples of {{fig.params.scoreN}} scores is {{fig.scores.kappa}}. On such data every algorithm is accurate: the worst relative errors are {{fig.scores.textbook}} for the textbook formula, {{fig.scores.twoPass}} for the two-pass algorithm and {{fig.scores.welford}} for the recurrence. The measurable gap of §7.3 needs data whose spread is small compared with their mean, which scores between 0 and 10 with a mean near their standard deviation are not. There, the reasons to prefer the recurrence are that it makes one pass, needs constant memory, and delivers the estimates at every step.

§8 Discussion

Cost. One call of push performs a division, a multiplication and four additions or subtractions, plus the increment of the counter, so n observations cost a time linear in n, as the two other algorithms do. The textbook formula performs fewer operations per observation, with one multiplication and two additions and no division in its loop, and the recurrence pays for its stability with the division, as Chan, Golub and LeVeque note when they compare operation counts [2, p. 247]. A shift of the data by a rough estimate of the mean, which they also analyse, reduces \kappa and is the classical alternative when the mean can be guessed in advance.

Memory. The state is three numbers, whatever the length of the sequence. The two-pass algorithm stores the data or reads them twice, which is impossible on a stream and costly on a file too large for main memory. The textbook formula also needs only constant memory, which is what makes it tempting and why its numerical failure matters.

Streaming. The recurrence holds a valid mean and variance after every observation, not only at the end. A monitor can read them at any moment, an experiment can stop as soon as an estimate is precise enough, and a whole trajectory of estimates comes at no extra cost, since each is an intermediate state of the same computation. The price is that no observation can be revised or removed once absorbed without further machinery.

The second exercise relies on exactly this property: each hacker's trajectory of scores passes through these recurrences once, and the code of Listing 1 yields the sampling mean and variance of the trajectory, and every intermediate estimate along it, with three numbers of state per hacker.

App. Reproducibility

Every figure, table and statistic on this page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds of Table A1, and can be recomputed by anyone from the repository: npm test runs the test suite, which checks each form of the recurrence against the definition, the exact sum of squares against hand cases, the three algorithms, the stepper and the study, and npm run build rebuilds the page. The study takes about {{fig.params.computeSeconds}} seconds (last computed {{fig.params.computedAt}}); its results are cached under node_modules/.cache/hw05/ with the key {{fig.params.cacheKey}}, a hash of the parameters, of the source of every module involved and of the figure provider itself, so that a change to any of them recomputes everything.

Each part of the study has its own stream of a seeded Mulberry32 generator, whose seed is the master seed {{fig.params.seed}} combined by exclusive or with the 32-bit FNV-1a hash of a label: {{fig.params.seedNoise}} (label stability:noise) for the {{fig.params.draws}} standard normal draws of the sweep, obtained by the Box–Muller transform, and {{fig.params.seedScores}} (stability:scores) for the scores of §7.5. The generator is statistical, not cryptographic. Panel 1 starts from the data of Table 1 and draws further sequences with the same generator, from a seed that can be set. Its first draw uses the master seed.
