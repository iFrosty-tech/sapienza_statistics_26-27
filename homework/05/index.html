Exercise 1 Welford's recurrences

§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Ex. 1 – Welford’s Recurrence Formulas

Derive Welford’s recurrence formulas for the sum of squares (SS) and the variance, following the same approach as Knuth’s recurrence formula for the mean.

Write the corresponding code. The core logic should require only about 3 lines.

Throughout, x_1, x_2, \dots is a sequence of observations read one at a time, and after k of them

\bar{x}_k = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i, \qquad \mathrm{SS}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (x_i - \bar{x}_k)^2
(1)

are the sample mean and the corrected sum of squares. Evaluated directly, each definition stores every observation, and \mathrm{SS}_k must be recomputed from scratch at every k because each new observation moves the mean around which all the earlier deviations are measured. A recurrence replaces this with a rule that turns the summary of k - 1 observations and one new value into the summary of k observations, and forgets the data.

§2 derives the recurrence for the mean. §3 applies the same approach to the sum of squares, with every algebraic step shown, and §4 deduces the forms for the variance. §5 gives the code, whose core is three lines, and traces it on a small data set. §6 reads the increment of the sum of squares as the area of a rectangle. §7 measures what the recurrence is worth numerically: against the exact value of the sum of squares, computed in rational arithmetic, the textbook formula loses every digit at a condition number of about {{fig.textbook.lostKappa}}, while the error of the recurrence grows only in proportion to that number, with a fitted log–log slope of {{fig.slope.welford}} against {{fig.slope.textbook}}. §8 discusses cost, memory and streaming, and links to the second exercise, which uses these recurrences. Every number on the page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds listed in the appendix.

§2 Knuth's recurrence for the mean

The approach has three moves: write the quantity after k observations as a sum, separate the last term, and recognise in what remains the same quantity after k - 1 observations. For the mean, multiplying (1) by k and separating the last term gives

k\,\bar{x}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i + x_k = (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1} + x_k .
(2)

Dividing by k and writing (k - 1)/k = 1 - 1/k yields the recurrence presented by Knuth [10, §4.2.2]:

\bar{x}_k = \frac{k - 1}{k}\,\bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k}{k} = \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}, \qquad k \ge 1 .
(3)

The initial condition is \bar{x}_1 = x_1, which is what (3) returns at k = 1 from any starting value, because the old estimate then has weight zero; taking \bar{x}_0 = 0 merely lets the same line of code handle the first observation.

The second form of (3) reads as old estimate + innovation/k. The innovation x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} is the part of the new observation that the current estimate did not predict, and 1/k is the share of it that the estimate absorbs. The role of k is exactly this gain: the first observation is taken whole, the second moves the estimate halfway towards it, and the hundredth by one hundredth. The gain falls as 1/k because every observation must end with the same weight in the mean; a constant gain would give an exponentially weighted average instead, a different estimator.

§3 Welford's recurrence for the sum of squares

3.1 Derivation

The same three moves apply, once \mathrm{SS}_k is written as a sum over which the last term can be separated. The definition (1) itself is not such a sum, because every one of its terms contains \bar{x}_k. Expanding the square and using \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i = k\,\bar{x}_k removes the difficulty:

\mathrm{SS}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i^2 - 2\bar{x}_k \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i + k\,\bar{x}_k^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{k} x_i^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(4)

Separating the last term of the sum in (4) gives

\mathrm{SS}_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i^2 + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(5)

The identity (4) at k - 1 reads \sum_{i=1}^{k-1} x_i^2 = \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} + (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1}^2, which brings the previous sum of squares into (5). The increment we are after is therefore

\mathrm{SS}_k - \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} = (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1}^2 + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(6)

Now the recurrence for the mean enters. By (2), (k - 1)\,\bar{x}_{k-1} = k\,\bar{x}_k - x_k. Replacing one of the two factors \bar{x}_{k-1} of the first term of (6) with this expression gives

\mathrm{SS}_k - \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} = \bar{x}_{k-1}\,(k\,\bar{x}_k - x_k) + x_k^2 - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 .
(7)

Multiplying out and collecting the two terms in x_k and the two terms in k\,\bar{x}_k gives

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{SS}_k - \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} &= x_k^2 - x_k\,\bar{x}_{k-1} - k\,\bar{x}_k^2 + k\,\bar{x}_k\,\bar{x}_{k-1} \\ &= x_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - \bar{x}_k \cdot k\,(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) . \end{aligned}
(8)

The last bracket is the recurrence (3) again, in the form k\,(\bar{x}_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}. Substituting it leaves the common factor x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} in both terms:

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{SS}_k - \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} &= x_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) - \bar{x}_k\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) \\ &= (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) . \end{aligned}
(9)

This is the recurrence for the sum of squares:

\mathrm{SS}_k = \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k), \qquad \mathrm{SS}_1 = 0 .
(10)

Both factors are deviations of the new observation: one from the mean before it arrived and one from the mean after. Starting from \mathrm{SS}_0 = 0 and \bar{x}_0 = 0 also gives \mathrm{SS}_1 = 0, because at k = 1 the second factor vanishes.

3.2 An equivalent form, and a consequence

The two factors of (10) are proportional. From (3),

x_k - \bar{x}_k = x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} - \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k} = \frac{k - 1}{k}\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}) ,
(11)

and substituting (11) into (10) gives a form that uses the old mean alone:

\mathrm{SS}_k = \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} + \frac{k - 1}{k}\,(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2 .
(12)

The increment in (12) is a square times a non-negative factor, so the sum of squares never decreases: a new observation can only add to the total spread, and it leaves it unchanged exactly when x_k = \bar{x}_{k-1} or k = 1. The definition (1) does not show this, since each new observation changes every deviation in the sum. By (11) the same holds for the product in (10), whose two factors always share their sign. The property also matters in floating point, as §7 shows: the sum is built from non-negative terms.

3.3 Who gave which form

The recurrence is known by Welford's name, from a note of 1962 [14] on computing corrected sums of squares and products. Chan, Golub and LeVeque state that, for an unweighted sum of squares, the algorithms of Welford, of West [15] and of Hanson are virtually identical, and that those of Youngs and Cramer [17], which update the running sum rather than the running mean, are similar [4, pp. 242–243]. The product form (10) is the one usually quoted from Knuth, who credits the method to Welford [10, §4.2.2]; Ling compares several such algorithms [11]. The full texts of the note of 1962 and of Knuth's section were not available to the author, so this paragraph reports them through the sources just named and does not claim which algebraic form Welford's note prints. The derivation above is independent of those texts, and each of its results is checked numerically in the test suite of the repository.

§4 The variance

The sample variance of k \ge 2 observations, the estimator that is unbiased for the variance of the population, is

s_k^2 = \frac{\mathrm{SS}_k}{k - 1}, \qquad k \ge 2 .
(13)

Dividing (12) by k - 1 and writing \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} = (k - 2)\,s_{k-1}^2 gives a recurrence in the variance alone:

s_k^2 = \frac{k - 2}{k - 1}\,s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})^2}{k}, \qquad k \ge 2 .
(14)

At k = 2 the coefficient of s_1^2 is zero, so the undefined s_1^2 is never needed and (14) gives s_2^2 = (x_2 - x_1)^2/2. The form that mirrors Knuth's recurrence starts from (10) instead. Dividing \mathrm{SS}_k = (k - 2)\,s_{k-1}^2 + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) by k - 1 and writing (k - 2)/(k - 1) = 1 - 1/(k - 1) gives

s_k^2 = s_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - s_{k-1}^2}{k - 1}, \qquad k \ge 2,
(15)

with the convention s_1^2 = 0 at k = 2. This is again old estimate + innovation/step: the product of the two deviations plays the part of one new squared deviation, the innovation is its distance from the current variance, and the gain is 1/(k - 1) because the variance has k - 1 degrees of freedom. The estimate rises when the new observation is more surprising than the variance predicts and falls when it is less so, even though \mathrm{SS}_k itself never falls. The same steps with the divisor k give the variance of the data themselves, \hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \mathrm{SS}_k/k:

\hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2 + \frac{(x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) - \hat{\sigma}_{k-1}^2}{k}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_0^2 = 0 .
(16)

§6 A geometric reading

The increment in (10) is a product of two lengths, so it can be drawn as an area. Take a square plane with the data on both axes, at the same scale, and mark on its diagonal the point (x_k, x_k). The rectangle with opposite corners (\bar{x}_{k-1}, \bar{x}_k) and (x_k, x_k) has sides x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1} and x_k - \bar{x}_k, and its area is what the observation adds to the sum of squares.

Three results of §3 can be read from this drawing. The two sides have the same sign, because the new mean lies between the old mean and the observation, so the area is never negative: the sum of squares never decreases. By (11) the height is (k - 1)/k of the width, so the rectangle approaches a square as k grows, and (12) states its area through the width alone. And the mean moves towards the observation by 1/k of the width, which is the difference between the two sides.

Panel 1 is interactive and needs scripting. It steps through a sequence of up to sixteen values, editable or drawn from a seed, and for each observation shows the previous mean, the new observation, the new mean, the rectangle whose area is added and the running sum of squares as accumulated area. On the data of Table 2, step 2 moves the mean from {{fig.trace.step2.old}} to {{fig.trace.step2.mean}} and adds an area of {{fig.trace.step2.area}}, and step 8 moves it from {{fig.trace.step8.old}} to {{fig.trace.step8.mean}} and brings the sum of squares to {{fig.trace.step8.ss}}; Figure 1 prints these steps.

§7 Numerical behaviour

7.1 Three algorithms for one quantity

In exact arithmetic the recurrence and the definition are the same number. In floating point they are not. Three algorithms are compared here, in the terminology of Chan, Golub and LeVeque [4]: the textbook one-pass formula (4) evaluated as \sum x_i^2 - (\sum x_i)^2/n, the two-pass algorithm, which computes the mean and then sums the squared deviations of (1), and Welford's updating recurrence (10), which is the code of Listing 1.

The textbook formula subtracts two large, nearly equal numbers. When the spread of the data is small compared with their size, most of the digits of the two terms agree and cancel, and what is left is dominated by the rounding errors made while accumulating them. The sensitivity of the problem is measured by the condition number [4, §2]

\kappa = \frac{\lVert x \rVert_2}{\sqrt{\mathrm{SS}}} = \sqrt{1 + \frac{n\,\bar{x}^2}{\mathrm{SS}}} ,
(17)

the second equality being (4) divided by \mathrm{SS}. It satisfies \kappa \ge 1, equals one when the mean is zero, and for a small spread is close to the absolute mean divided by the standard deviation.

7.2 The exact value

A measurement of rounding error needs a reference free of rounding error, and a higher-precision run only moves the problem. Here the reference is exact. Every finite double is a dyadic rational m\,2^{e}, so a sample can be scaled to a common power of two and written as integers M_i, with x_i = M_i\,2^{e}. Then

\mathrm{SS} = \frac{n \sum_i M_i^2 - \bigl(\sum_i M_i\bigr)^2}{n}\; 2^{2e}
(18)

is evaluated in arbitrary-precision integers, with no rounding at all. The relative error of a computed value is itself a difference of rationals, evaluated exactly and rounded once at the end, to the nearest double, so that nothing in the measurement is limited by the arithmetic being measured.

Chan, Golub and LeVeque tabulate bounds on the relative error, keeping only the dominant terms and suppressing small constant factors [4, Table 1]: n\kappa^2 u for the textbook algorithm, n\kappa u for updating, and nu + n^2\kappa^2 u^2 for the two-pass algorithm, whose second term, derived in their earlier report [3], is the reason the algorithm is not simply independent of \kappa. The measured slopes agree with the exponents: {{fig.slope.textbook}} against two for the textbook formula, and {{fig.slope.welford}} against one for the recurrence. The two-pass error is flat, with slope {{fig.slope.twoPassFlat}}, until the second-order term takes over near the end of the sweep, where the slope is {{fig.slope.twoPassTail}} over the last few points, too few to count as a measurement of the exponent. The bounds are generous, as bounds should be: over the informative part of the sweep the worst error reached at most {{fig.ratio.textbook}} of the textbook bound, {{fig.ratio.welford}} of the updating bound and {{fig.ratio.twoPass}} of the two-pass bound. The same comparison explains the ranking in Figure 2 and adds a qualification. The two-pass algorithm is the more accurate of the two stable ones, as its bound and Higham's discussion of these formulae say [8, Ch. 1], but it needs the data twice. The error of the recurrence grows with \kappa where the first-order error of the two-pass algorithm does not, and that is the price of a single pass.

7.5 The limit of the argument

Stability is not the reason to use the recurrence in the second exercise. Its scores X = B\,Y have mean \mu = {{fig.scores.mean}} and variance \sigma^2 = {{fig.scores.variance}}, so by (17) their condition number is close to \sqrt{1 + \mu^2/\sigma^2} = {{fig.scores.kappaTheory}}, and the median over {{fig.params.scoreReplicates}} simulated samples of {{fig.params.scoreN}} scores is {{fig.scores.kappa}}. On such data every algorithm is accurate: the worst relative errors are {{fig.scores.textbook}} for the textbook formula, {{fig.scores.twoPass}} for the two-pass algorithm and {{fig.scores.welford}} for the recurrence. The two-pass figure is the largest because about 70% of the scores are exactly 0, and their identical terms round in the same direction, so that the errors add up instead of cancelling; thirteen digits remain correct all the same. The measurable gap of §7.3 needs data whose spread is small compared with their mean, which scores between 0 and 10 with a mean near their standard deviation are not. There, the reasons to prefer the recurrence are that it makes one pass, needs constant memory, and delivers the estimates at every step.

§8 Discussion

Cost. One call of push performs a division, a multiplication and four additions or subtractions, plus the increment of the counter, so n observations cost a time linear in n, as the two other algorithms do. The textbook formula performs fewer operations per observation, with one multiplication and two additions and no division in its loop. The recurrence pays for its stability with the extra work, and Chan, Golub and LeVeque compare the two operation counts [4]. A shift of the data by a rough estimate of the mean, which they also analyse, reduces \kappa and is the classical alternative when the mean can be guessed in advance.

Memory. The state is three numbers, whatever the length of the sequence. The two-pass algorithm stores the data or reads them twice, which is impossible on a stream and costly on a file too large for main memory. The textbook formula also needs only constant memory, which is what makes it tempting and why its numerical failure matters.

Streaming. The recurrence holds a valid mean and variance after every observation, not only at the end. A monitor can read them at any moment, an experiment can stop as soon as an estimate is precise enough, and a whole trajectory of estimates comes at no extra cost, since each is an intermediate state of the same computation. The price is that no observation can be revised or removed once absorbed without further machinery.

The second exercise relies on exactly this property: each hacker's trajectory of scores passes through these recurrences once, and the code of Listing 1 yields the sampling mean and variance of the trajectory, and every intermediate estimate along it, with three numbers of state per hacker.

Exercise 2 Sampling mean and variance

§9 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Ex. 2 – Sampling Mean and Variance

Extend the previous homework by assigning each penetration attempt a score between 0 and 10, drawn from a fixed probability distribution of your choice. Let the theoretical mean and variance of this distribution be μ and σ², respectively.

Assume there are m hackers, each attempting to penetrate n systems. Each hacker receives one score for each system, resulting in a trajectory of n scores.

For each hacker, use Welford’s recurrence formulas to compute the sampling mean and sampling variance of their n scores.

Across the m hackers, compute the average of the sampling means and the average of the sampling variances. Compare these two averages with the theoretical mean μ and variance σ², respectively.

What conclusions can you draw from these comparisons? Why are these results useful in inferential statistics? Repeat the experiment for different values of n. How does increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker affect the variability of the sampling means and sampling variances?

Throughout this exercise, the sampling mean and the sampling variance of the assignment are the sample mean \bar{x}_n and the sample variance s_n^2 = \mathrm{SS}_n/(n - 1), where \mathrm{SS}_n is the corrected sum of squares of the n scores of one hacker; the variance therefore needs n \ge 2.

The law of the scores is the free choice of the exercise, and §10 makes it so that nothing in this exercise can owe its result to normality. §11 runs the experiment, with every estimate obtained through Welford's recurrences, and §12 compares the two averages with \mu and \sigma^2. §13 proves why they agree, §14 repeats the experiment for {{fig.params.nCount}} values of n from 2 to {{fig.params.nMax}}, §15 turns the results into inference about a single hacker, and §16 gathers the answers. In short: the average of the sampling means stays on \mu and the average of the sampling variances on \sigma^2 at every n, even n = 2, because both estimators are unbiased; the spread of both across hackers shrinks as 1/\sqrt{n}; and the 95% interval for \mu reaches its nominal level as n grows, whereas the usual interval for \sigma^2 never does, stalling near {{fig.cover.chiLimit}} however many systems are attempted.

§10 The score law

10.1 From a penetration attempt to a score

Homework 3 modelled one penetration attempt by a Bernoulli increment B, equal to 1 if the attempt succeeds, with p = 0.3. The extension asked for here replaces the bare outcome by a score between 0 and 10. A failed attempt scores 0; a successful one scores the depth Y it reached, which is uniform on the interval from 0 to 10 and independent of the outcome:

\begin{gathered} X = B\,Y, \\ B \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(0.3), \quad Y \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0, 10], \quad B \perp Y . \end{gathered}
(19)

This law is chosen for three reasons. It extends the previous homework literally: X > 0 exactly when the attempt of homework 3 succeeds, with the same p. All its moments are available in closed form, so every comparison below is made against an exact value and not against another simulation. And it is far from normal, with a spike at 0 and a long flat tail, so that nothing below can be an artefact of normality. The range from 0 to 10 echoes the base score of the Common Vulnerability Scoring System [7], which rates the severity of a vulnerability from 0.0 to 10.0; the score of (19) is not a CVSS score, and it borrows only the scale.

10.2 The distribution

The law of (19) is neither discrete nor continuous. It has an atom of 1 - p at 0 and a constant density p/10 on (0, 10], so that

F(x) = \Pr\{X \le x\} = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0, \\ 0.7 + 0.03\,x, & 0 \le x < 10, \\ 1, & x \ge 10. \end{cases}
(20)

Scores are simulated by inversion, with exactly one uniform U per score: X = 0 when U \le 0.7, and X = (U - 0.7)/0.03 otherwise. Figure 3 sets (20) against a sample of {{fig.sample.size}} scores, of which {{fig.sample.zeros}} are 0.

10.3 Exact moments

Since B and Y are independent and B^k = B for k \ge 1, the raw moments of (19) are

\mathbb{E}[X^k] = \mathbb{E}[B]\,\mathbb{E}[Y^k] = \frac{p\,10^k}{k + 1}, \qquad k \ge 1 ,
(21)

which gives 3/2, 10, 75 and 600 for k = 1, \dots, 4. Expanding the central moments \mu_k = \mathbb{E}[(X - \mu)^k] in these,

\begin{aligned} \mu &= \tfrac{3}{2}, \\ \sigma^2 &= 10 - \mu^2 = \tfrac{31}{4}, \\ \mu_3 &= 75 - 3\mu \cdot 10 + 2\mu^3 = \tfrac{147}{4}, \\ \mu_4 &= 600 - 4\mu \cdot 75 + 6\mu^2 \cdot 10 - 3\mu^4 = \tfrac{4317}{16} . \end{aligned}
(22)

Thus \mu = {{fig.law.mu}}, \sigma^2 = {{fig.law.sigma2}}, \mu_3 = {{fig.law.mu3}} and \mu_4 = {{fig.law.mu4}}. The skewness \mu_3/\sigma^3 is {{fig.law.skewness}} and the kurtosis \kappa = \mu_4/\sigma^4 is {{fig.law.kurtosis}}, against 0 and 3 for a normal law: the score is markedly right-skewed and heavy in the fourth moment, and both facts matter in §15. From here on \kappa denotes this kurtosis, not the condition number of §7.

§11 The experiment

11.1 m hackers, n systems

Hacker j attempts n systems in turn and receives the score X_i^{(j)} on system i; the scores are independent across systems and hackers and follow (19). Each hacker's trajectory of n scores goes through the recurrences derived in Exercise 1 [14], which from \bar{x}_0 = 0 and \mathrm{SS}_0 = 0 read

\begin{aligned} \bar{x}_k &= \bar{x}_{k-1} + \frac{x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1}}{k}, \\ \mathrm{SS}_k &= \mathrm{SS}_{k-1} + (x_k - \bar{x}_{k-1})(x_k - \bar{x}_k) , \end{aligned}
(23)

and leave \bar{x}_n^{(j)} and s_n^{2\,(j)} = \mathrm{SS}_n^{(j)}/(n - 1). No hacker's scores are stored: three numbers of state are enough, and the same function performs every update in this exercise, in the study as in Panel 2. For each n of §14 this is an independent experiment of {{fig.params.m}} hackers.

11.2 The whole path of an estimate

Because (23) holds at every step, a trajectory of n scores yields not one estimate but a path of them: what the hacker's sample would report after k systems, for every k \le n. §12 The averages against the theory

12.1 The comparison

Across the m hackers the assignment asks for the two averages

A_n = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} \bar{x}_n^{(j)}, \qquad B_n = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{j=1}^{m} s_n^{2\,(j)} .
(24)

An average of m independent estimates has a standard error of \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\cdot)/m}, and both variances are known exactly from §13, so each comparison can be read as a z-score, (A_n - \mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2/(mn)} and (B_n - \sigma^2)/\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2)/m}. Table 5 lists them for every n, and Figure 5 plots them.

The averages stay on their theoretical values at every n, from a hacker with two scores to one with {{fig.params.nMax}}. Of the {{fig.avg.zCount}} z-scores, {{fig.avg.zAboveTwo}} exceed 2 in absolute value: {{fig.avg.zList}}. If the averages were exactly unbiased, and the z-scores close to normal, about {{fig.avg.zExpected}} would be expected to do so, so this count is what an honest experiment yields and not a discrepancy. The seed was fixed before the first run and has not been changed since. The two exceedances are of opposite sign and sit at unrelated values of n; the z-score of B_n at the same n moves with that of A_n because the two estimators are correlated (§15.4).

12.2 Why the divisor is n − 1

The recurrences give \mathrm{SS}_n, and the obvious estimator would divide it by n. The last two columns of Table 5 show what that estimator does: its average does not settle on \sigma^2 but on \sigma^2(n - 1)/n. At n = 2 it reports {{fig.avg.n2.ssOverN}} where the variance is {{fig.law.sigma2}}, {{fig.avg.n2.shortfall}} too low; at n = 10 the shortfall is still {{fig.avg.n10.shortfall}}. Figure 6 shows this against n: averaging more hackers only pins the wrong value more precisely, because the error is a bias, not noise.

§13 Why the averages agree

The results of §12 hold for any law with enough moments, and the proofs below use nothing about (19) beyond independence and identical distribution. They are standard [2, Ch. 5; 5], and they are given here because they explain every column of Table 5.

13.1 The sampling mean

By linearity of the expectation, and by independence for the variance,

\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\bar{x}_n] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu, \\ \mathrm{Var}(\bar{x}_n) &= \frac{1}{n^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathrm{Var}(X_i) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} . \end{aligned}
(25)

13.2 The sampling variance

Adding and subtracting \mu inside the sum of squares, and using \sum_i (X_i - \bar{x}_n) = 0,

\mathrm{SS}_n = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \bar{x}_n)^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} (X_i - \mu)^2 - n(\bar{x}_n - \mu)^2 .
(26)

The first term has expectation n\sigma^2 and the second, by (25), n \cdot \sigma^2/n = \sigma^2. Hence

\begin{gathered} \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SS}_n] = (n - 1)\,\sigma^2, \qquad \mathbb{E}[s_n^2] = \sigma^2, \\ \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{SS}_n/n] = \frac{n - 1}{n}\,\sigma^2 . \end{gathered}
(27)

Deviations are measured from \bar{x}_n and not from the unknown \mu, and \bar{x}_n is the point that makes them smallest. That is the whole of the bias in §12.2, and it costs exactly one \sigma^2 out of n\sigma^2.

13.3 The variance of the sampling variance

The spreads of §14 need the second moment of s_n^2. The shortest route writes the sample variance as an average over pairs,

s_n^2 = \binom{n}{2}^{\!-1} \sum_{i < j} \frac{(X_i - X_j)^2}{2} ,
(28)

which is a U-statistic with kernel h(x, y) = (x - y)^2/2. Hoeffding's formula for such a statistic [9] gives

\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2) = \frac{4(n - 2)\,\zeta_1 + 2\,\zeta_2}{n(n - 1)} ,

with \zeta_1 = \mathrm{Var}(\mathbb{E}[h(X_1, X_2) \mid X_1]) and \zeta_2 = \mathrm{Var}(h(X_1, X_2)). Here \mathbb{E}[h \mid X_1] = ((X_1 - \mu)^2 + \sigma^2)/2, so \zeta_1 = (\mu_4 - \sigma^4)/4, and expanding (X_1 - X_2)^4 gives \zeta_2 = (\mu_4 + \sigma^4)/2. Substituting and simplifying,

\mathrm{Var}(s_n^2) = \frac{1}{n} \left( \mu_4 - \frac{n - 3}{n - 1}\,\sigma^4 \right) ,
(29)

the classical result [5]. It depends on the law only through \sigma^2 and \mu_4. For normal scores \mu_4 = 3\sigma^4 and (29) reduces to the familiar 2\sigma^4/(n - 1).

13.4 The two estimators together

Taking \mu = 0 without loss of generality, (28) expresses s_n^2 through pairs, and only a pair that contains system i can be correlated with X_i. For such a pair \mathrm{Cov}(X_1, (X_1 - X_2)^2) = \mu_3, and counting the n - 1 pairs that contain each index gives

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Cov}(\bar{x}_n, s_n^2) &= \frac{\mu_3}{n}, \\ \mathrm{Corr}(\bar{x}_n, s_n^2) &= \frac{\mu_3}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 \bigl(\mu_4 - \frac{n - 3}{n - 1}\sigma^4\bigr)}} . \end{aligned}
(30)

The covariance vanishes for a symmetric law. For the law (19) it does not, and the correlation tends to \mu_3/\sqrt{\sigma^2(\mu_4 - \sigma^4)} = {{fig.scatter.limit}} as n grows.

13.5 What the comparisons show

The first conclusion is that \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are unbiased, and this is what the averages of Table 5 display: an estimator is unbiased when the mean of its sampling distribution equals the quantity it estimates, and an average over many hackers is a direct estimate of that mean. The second is that unbiasedness holds at every n, not only in the limit, and for this strongly non-normal law as much as for any other: neither the central limit theorem nor normality is used in (25) or (27). The third is that it is a property of the divisor: \mathrm{SS}_n/n, computed from the same recurrences, is biased, and no number of hackers repairs it. The fourth is a caution: unbiasedness says nothing about a single hacker. At n = 2 the averages are right while the individual estimates are almost useless; how far one hacker's estimate can stray is the subject of §14.

§14 Repeating for different n

14.1 The spreads across hackers

Each n of {{fig.params.nGrid}} is a fresh experiment of {{fig.params.m}} hackers with its own random stream, so no estimate at one n is a continuation of another. The averages did not move with n (§12); what moves is the spread of the estimates across hackers, measured here by their standard deviation.

Both spreads fall as 1/\sqrt{n}. For the mean, (25) gives \mathrm{SD}(\bar{x}_n) = \sigma/\sqrt{n} exactly, a slope of precisely -1/2 on the log–log plot; the fitted slope is {{fig.spread.slopeMean}}. For the variance, (29) gives \mathrm{SD}(s_n^2) \approx \sqrt{(\mu_4 - \sigma^4)/n} for large n, the same rate with a constant of {{fig.law.sdConstant}}. The fitted slope over the whole grid is {{fig.spread.slopeVariance}}, and the exact curve of (29) has the same {{fig.spread.slopeExactVariance}}: the small excess over -1/2 comes from the factor (n - 3)/(n - 1) at the smallest values of n, not from a different rate. Multiplying the number of systems by 100 therefore divides both spreads by about 10: from n = 10 to n = 1,000, the spread of the means falls from {{fig.spread.n10.sdMean}} to {{fig.spread.n1000.sdMean}} and that of the variances from {{fig.spread.n10.sdVariance}} to {{fig.spread.n1000.sdVariance}}.

The two estimators obey the same rate but not the same constant, and they answer to different properties of the law. The spread of \bar{x}_n is governed by the variance \sigma^2, the spread of s_n^2 by the fourth moment, that is, by the kurtosis. Writing (29) in terms of \kappa, the right panel of Figure 7 shows what this costs: the true variance of s_n^2 exceeds its normal-theory value at every n, and the ratio climbs to (\kappa - 1)/2 = {{fig.spread.ratioLimit}}. It does not fall back to 1 however many systems are attempted, and §15.3 draws the consequence.

14.2 The sampling distributions

Figure 8 shows the whole law of the two estimators at n = {{fig.params.distributionN}}, against the normal approximations N(\mu, \sigma^2/n) and N(\sigma^2, \mathrm{Var}(s_n^2)).

At n = 5 neither law resembles a normal one. A share {{fig.dist.n5.atomShare}} of the hackers scored 0 on every system (the exact value is 0.7^5, or {{fig.dist.n5.atomExact}}) and they pile up at \bar{x}_n = 0 and s_n^2 = 0, the atom of the score law seen through the estimators. As n grows the central limit theorem takes over, and by n = 500 both histograms sit close to their normal curves. The skewness of \bar{x}_n is the skewness of the score divided by \sqrt{n}, {{fig.dist.n5.skewMean}} at n = 5 and {{fig.dist.n500.skewMean}} at n = 500. The sampling variance turns out to be less skewed still, with {{fig.dist.n5.skewVariance}} and {{fig.dist.n500.skewVariance}} measured across the hackers at the same n, the second within sampling error of 0. That is a coincidence of this law, whose moments nearly cancel the leading term of the third cumulant of s_n^2, and not a general rule: for normal scores \bar{x}_n has no skewness at all and s_n^2 has some.

14.3 Hackers against systems

The two indices of the experiment are easily confused, and they do different things. The number of systems n sets how much each hacker knows. The number of hackers m sets how well the experiment measures what a hacker would know. Figure 9 fixes n = {{fig.precision.n}} and cuts a long run of {{fig.params.precisionHackers}} hackers into groups of m to show the difference.

The error of the two averages falls as 1/\sqrt{m}, from {{fig.precision.m10.rmsMean}} at m = 10 to {{fig.precision.m1000.rmsMean}} at m = 1,000 for the sampling mean, with fitted slopes of {{fig.precision.slopeMean}} and {{fig.precision.slopeVariance}}. The fit runs a little steeper than -1/2 because the largest groups are few ({{fig.precision.m10000.groups}} groups at m = 10,000), and the last points are themselves noisy. The spread of one hacker's estimate stays where it is: {{fig.precision.m10.spreadMean}} at m = 10, {{fig.precision.m1000.spreadMean}} at m = 1,000, within 1% of its exact value of {{fig.precision.exactSdMean}}. More hackers tell us more exactly what a hacker with {{fig.precision.n}} scores can expect; they do not make that hacker any better informed. Only n does that.

The direct answer to the last question of the assignment follows. Increasing the number of systems attempted by each hacker reduces the variability of both the sampling means and the sampling variances, each in proportion to 1/\sqrt{n}, leaves their centres where they are, and brings their laws closer to the normal law. The spread of the sampling means depends on the variance of the scores and the spread of the sampling variances on their kurtosis. In absolute terms the sampling variance is much the noisier estimate; relative to what each one estimates, the two happen to be about equally precise for this law, since at n = 1,000 the spreads are {{fig.spread.n1000.sdVariance}} out of {{fig.law.sigma2}} and {{fig.spread.n1000.sdMean}} out of {{fig.law.mu}}, or {{fig.spread.n1000.relVariance}} and {{fig.spread.n1000.relMean}}. Both are slow: a tenfold reduction takes a hundredfold effort.

§15 Inference

15.1 One hacker, and the thought experiment behind it

The experiment above has a privileged position: it can repeat the sample {{fig.params.m}} times. Nobody who studies real attackers can. In practice there is one sample, and inferential statistics is the discipline of saying something about \mu and \sigma^2 from it. The m hackers are the frequentist thought experiment that gives such statements their meaning: the sampling distribution of an estimator is what we would see across many repetitions, and a claim about one sample is a claim about the procedure that produced it. The results of §12–§14 are useful because they describe that sampling distribution, and because they can be used from one sample alone.

A single hacker's trajectory supplies a point estimate \bar{x}_n of \mu, unbiased by (25), and also an estimate of its own precision. The standard error \sigma/\sqrt{n} involves the unknown \sigma, but s_n^2, unbiased by (27) and consistent by (29), can stand in for \sigma^2. The sample therefore reports both a value and how far to trust it, \bar{x}_n \pm s_n/\sqrt{n}, and the two numbers come out of the same three-line recurrence. What licenses the substitution is the consistency of s_n^2, not its unbiasedness: s_n^2/n is unbiased for \mathrm{Var}(\bar{x}_n), yet its square root underestimates \sigma/\sqrt{n} on average, by Jensen's inequality, and the biased \mathrm{SS}_n/n would serve as well for large n.

15.2 The t-interval for the mean

The central limit theorem gives \sqrt{n}(\bar{x}_n - \mu)/\sigma \to N(0, 1) in distribution, and s_n \to \sigma in probability, by (29) here and by the law of large numbers for any law of finite variance, so Slutsky's theorem [6] gives the same limit with the estimated standard deviation:

T_n = \frac{\sqrt{n}\,(\bar{x}_n - \mu)}{s_n} \xrightarrow{\;d\;} N(0, 1) .
(31)

Any finite-variance law qualifies, and normality of the scores is not needed. The 95% interval \bar{x}_n \pm t_{n-1;\,0.975}\, s_n/\sqrt{n}, with the quantile of Student's distribution [13], is exact for normal scores and, by (31), asymptotically correct for these. Figure 10 and Table 7 measure how soon: the share of hackers whose interval contains \mu is {{fig.cover.n5.tRate}} at n = 5, {{fig.cover.n20.tRate}} at n = 20, {{fig.cover.n100.tRate}} at n = 100 and {{fig.cover.n5000.tRate}} at n = 5,000.

At the smallest n the hackers with no success dominate: at n = 2 they make up {{fig.cover.n2.degenerate}} of the {{fig.params.m}} hackers, and the coverage of {{fig.cover.n2.tRate}} sits just under the ceiling of {{fig.cover.n2.ceiling}} they impose. Their probability falls fast, to {{fig.cover.n20.zeroProbability}} at n = 20, and from then on what remains of the shortfall has a subtler cause (§15.4).

15.3 The χ² interval for the variance

For normal scores (n - 1)s_n^2/\sigma^2 has a \chi^2_{n-1} law, which gives the textbook interval

\left[ \frac{(n - 1)\,s_n^2}{\chi^2_{n-1;\,0.975}}, \; \frac{(n - 1)\,s_n^2}{\chi^2_{n-1;\,0.025}} \right] .
(32)

No analogue of (31) comes to its rescue here. By the central limit theorem s_n^2 is asymptotically normal, but with the variance of (29), of order \sigma^4(\kappa - 1)/n, while the half-width of (32) is calibrated for the normal value \sigma^4 \cdot 2/n. The interval therefore stays too short by the fixed factor \sqrt{2/(\kappa - 1)}, and its coverage tends not to 95% but to

2\,\Phi\!\left(1.96\,\sqrt{\frac{2}{\kappa - 1}}\right) - 1 .
(33)

For \kappa = {{fig.law.kurtosis}} this limit is {{fig.cover.chiLimit}}. The simulation confirms it: at n = {{fig.cover.largestN}} the coverage is {{fig.cover.n5000.chiRate}}, with a Wilson interval from {{fig.cover.n5000.chiLow}} to {{fig.cover.n5000.chiHigh}}, which differs from (33) by {{fig.cover.chiZ}} standard errors, and the average over all n from {{fig.cover.tailFrom}} upwards is {{fig.cover.chiLargeMean}}. More data do not help: this is the sensitivity to non-normality that Box [1] set out, and the reason why the two intervals of this section behave so differently. A central limit theorem protects the mean because the interval uses it directly; the interval for the variance leans on the exact \chi^2 law, which is a property of normal samples and which no amount of averaging supplies.

15.4 Why the t-interval is slow: the two estimators are tied

For normal scores \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are independent, and independence characterises the normal law [12]. For the law (19) they are far from independent: by (30) their correlation tends to {{fig.scatter.limit}}. A hacker with few successes has both a low mean and a low variance, since nearly all of the spread of the scores comes from the successes themselves; Figure 11 shows the resulting cloud, and at n = 10 the hackers with few successes draw visible arcs in it.

This dependence explains how the t-interval fails at moderate n. When \bar{x}_n happens to be low, s_n tends to be low with it, so the interval is short exactly when it needs to be long, and it stops short of \mu from below. When \bar{x}_n is high the interval is long and easily reaches back. The misses are therefore lopsided: at n = 20, {{fig.cover.n20.tBelow}} intervals lie wholly below \mu against {{fig.cover.n20.tAbove}} above it, so that {{fig.cover.n20.tBelowShare}} of the misses are on one side, although the nominal 5% is meant to be shared equally. At n = 5,000 the split is {{fig.cover.n5000.tBelow}} to {{fig.cover.n5000.tAbove}}, and the coverage has reached its nominal level. The practical lesson is that (31) is a statement about large n, and that for a skewed score, "large" is a matter of how skewed: the same 95% label covers {{fig.cover.n10.tRate}} of the hackers at n = 10.

§16 Discussion

What the comparisons show. Across the hackers, the average of the sampling means agrees with \mu and the average of the sampling variances with \sigma^2, within the sampling error of the experiment, at every n and for a law that is nowhere near normal: \bar{x}_n and s_n^2 are unbiased (§12, §13). The divisor n - 1 is what makes the second true, and \mathrm{SS}_n/n would fall short by the factor (n - 1)/n.

Why it matters for inference. Unbiasedness and the exact variances describe the sampling distribution of an estimator, which is the object that gives a statement drawn from one sample its meaning. The consistency of s_n^2 lets one hacker report an estimate and its own standard error, and through the central limit theorem and Slutsky's theorem it yields an interval for \mu with the stated coverage for large n, for any law of finite variance. The argument also has its limits: the coverage of the usual interval for \sigma^2 settles near {{fig.cover.chiLimit}}, not 95%, because it rests on normality itself and not on a limit theorem (§15).

What n does. Increasing the number of systems shrinks the variability of both estimators as 1/\sqrt{n}, keeps their centres fixed and makes their laws more nearly normal. The number of hackers does none of this: m sharpens our picture of the sampling distribution, not the estimates of any hacker (§14).

What the model leaves out. The conclusions rest on the assumptions of the assignment. The scores are independent across systems, whereas a hacker who breaches one system of a network learns something that helps on the next, and a vulnerability is often shared by many systems; positive dependence inflates the variance of \bar{x}_n beyond \sigma^2/n, while s_n^2/n goes on reporting the smaller value. All hackers also share one p, so the study has no skilled and unskilled attackers: with hackers of different ability the spread of \bar{x}_n across hackers would not shrink to 0 with n but to the spread of their abilities, and the average over hackers would estimate the mean ability, not the ability of any one of them. The depth of a success is uniform by choice, and a heavier tail would slow every convergence measured here.
