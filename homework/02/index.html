§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

HMWK 2 — From Wallet Seed to Ethereum Address: Hashing, Key Derivation and Cryptographic Primitives / Statistical properties / TASK Check the practical cryptographic pipeline used in Ethereum wallets, from a mnemonic phrase to an Ethereum address and briefly describe the role and purpose of the following standards and cryptographic primitives: BIP-39 — mnemonic sentence and seed generation; BIP-32 — hierarchical deterministic (HD) key derivation; BIP-44 — standard derivation paths for hierarchical deterministic wallets; secp256k1 — elliptic-curve public-key cryptography; Keccak-256 — hash function used in Ethereum address derivation; EIP-55 — mixed-case checksum encoding for Ethereum addresses.

In case try experiencing with software such as metamask, rabby, exodus, rainbow, ... or similar to understand the role of each iece of technology ... A simplified wallet pipeline can be represented as: Mnemonic ↓ BIP-39 ↓ Seed BIP-32 / BIP-44 ↓ Private Key ↓ secp256k1 ↓ Public Key ↓ Keccak-256 ↓ Ethereum Address ↓ EIP-55 ↓ Checksummed Address.

For the purpose of this homework, distinguish clearly between: key generation and key derivation; public-key cryptography; hashing; checksums; encoding. Also briefly discuss the role of each primitive and how the different steps fit together and the statistical properties.

Hints: TEMPORAL AND HISTORICAL CONTEXT Briefly place these technologies in chronological context. Discuss the approximate historical order of: RSA — public-key cryptography, introduced in 1977; SHA-2 / SHA-512 — standardized in 2001; BIP-32 — hierarchical deterministic wallets, 2012; BIP-39 — mnemonic code for generating deterministic wallets, 2013; BIP-44 — multi-account hierarchical deterministic wallets, 2014; SHA-3 — standardized by NIST in 2015, based on Keccak; EIP-55 — Ethereum mixed-case checksum addresses, 2016. Also explain the distinction between: SHA-2 / SHA-512 and Keccak / SHA-3 They are related historically and both belong to the family of cryptographic hash functions, but Keccak is the underlying design selected for SHA-3, while Ethereum uses Keccak-256, which is not identical to the standardized SHA3-256 function (because of the padding/domain-separation convention.)

Why does the Ethereum wallet pipeline use several different cryptographic techniques instead of using one single algorithm for everything? Explain briefly why: BIP-39/BIP-32/BIP-44 → key management and derivation; secp256k1 → public/private key relationship and digital signatures; Keccak-256 → hashing; EIP-55 → checksum/error detection serve different purposes in the overall system.

To check the pipeline is given here an operational meaning with three parts. First, every step is implemented independently from the text of its standard, in plain JavaScript and without a cryptographic library: SHA-512, HMAC and PBKDF2, the Keccak-f[1600] permutation and its sponge, the arithmetic of secp256k1, BIP-39, BIP-32, the BIP-44 paths, the address rule and EIP-55. The same code runs when the page is built and in the reader's browser. Second, the implementation is validated against published test vectors, among them the reference vectors of BIP-39 [28], the test vectors of BIP-32 [30, "Test vectors"], the test cases of EIP-55 [5] and the published digests of the empty input under Keccak-256 [9] and SHA3-256 [20]; where Node's built-in crypto module offers the same primitive, the tests also compare the two implementations. Third, one worked example is recomputed every time the page is built: the development mnemonic of the Hardhat toolkit, whose first account is published with its address and its private key [21]. The build stops if either value, or either of the two digests of the empty input, differs from its published value; the published addresses of the second and third accounts are checked by the test suite. The intermediate values printed in §3 are not each compared with a published value: they lie on the computation that produced these verified endpoints.

The work answers five questions.

  1. Does an independent implementation reproduce the published values at every stage of the pipeline (§3)?
  2. To which class of operation does each step belong: key generation, key derivation, public-key cryptography, hashing, checksum or encoding (§2)?
  3. Which statistical properties should each stage have if its hash functions behave as random functions, and do simulated samples agree with them (§4)?
  4. In what order did the technologies appear, and how do SHA-2, Keccak and SHA-3 differ (§5)?
  5. Why does the pipeline combine several primitives instead of one (§6), and what is left of its security when the randomness at its start is weak (§7)?

The scope is deliberately narrow. Only Ethereum accounts are considered (coin type 60 of BIP-44), with the English word list of BIP-39; the worked example has an empty passphrase, which the explorer of Figure 2 lets the reader change. Signatures are described but not implemented. No wallet application was run by the author: §3.8 gives a procedure by which a reader can repeat the comparison in a wallet, and the evidence offered here is the agreement with published vectors checked at build time. The statistical tests of §4 can reveal certain departures from the behaviour of an ideal random function; as in Homework 1, passing them is a necessary condition for a cryptographic function, not a proof of its security. Timings are measured on one machine with one thread and are meant as orders of magnitude only.

§2 A taxonomy of operations

The assignment asks for five notions to be kept apart. They are distinguished here by what the operation computes and by three formal properties: whether it takes a secret key as a parameter, whether it can be inverted, and whether its input is secret. Table 1 collects the result.

Key generation is the sampling of a secret: a value drawn by a random process, ideally uniformly from a set so large that guessing it is infeasible. It is not a function of earlier data, and it is the only step of the pipeline that creates entropy. In a BIP-39 wallet it is the draw of the initial entropy, a string of ENT bits with ENT a multiple of 32 between 128 and 256 [23, "Generating the mnemonic"]; every later secret is computed from it. Its quality is a property of the random generator, not of any standard of the pipeline, and §7 shows what follows when the generator is weak.

Key derivation computes key material from secrets that already exist by a deterministic function. A deterministic function cannot add entropy (§4.1), so derivation serves other ends. PBKDF2 performs key stretching: it maps a secret that a person can transcribe, together with a salt, to a key of fixed length by iterating a pseudorandom function, so that every guess costs an attacker a fixed amount of work [15, §5.2]. BIP-32 performs hierarchical derivation: from a parent key and a 256-bit chain code it computes a tree of child keys with HMAC-SHA512, so that a single backup determines any number of accounts [30]. Both are keyed, because HMAC takes the password or the chain code as its key [11, §2], and both are one-way.

Public-key cryptography rests on a pair (k, K) linked by a map that is easy to evaluate and believed infeasible to invert. On secp256k1, K = kG is the k-fold sum of a fixed base point G in a group of prime order n [6, §2.4.1]. What separates this relation from a hash is its algebraic structure: it lets the holder of k produce signatures that anyone who knows only K can verify. Ethereum transactions are signed with recoverable ECDSA signatures on this curve [29, Appendix F].

Hashing is an unkeyed, deterministic map from inputs of any length to outputs of fixed length, designed so that it is infeasible to find an input with a given digest (preimage resistance) or two inputs with the same digest (collision resistance). In the pipeline, Keccak-256 maps the public key to the address; SHA-256 computes the checksum of BIP-39; SHA-512 works inside HMAC, and therefore inside PBKDF2 and BIP-32. A hash function has no key pair and no group structure: on its own it is not a signature scheme.

A checksum is redundancy appended to data so that accidental errors can be detected. The data stay in clear and anyone can compute a valid checksum for any data, so a checksum gives neither secrecy nor authentication. The pipeline has three: the CS = ENT/32 bits of BIP-39, the letter case of EIP-55, and the 32 bits derived from a double SHA-256 that are appended to a serialised extended key before its Base58 encoding [30, "Serialization format"].

An encoding is a reversible change of representation, with no key and no secrecy: bits into 11-bit word indices and words, bytes into hexadecimal digits, extended keys into Base58, letters into upper or lower case. An encoding preserves information exactly; it neither adds nor removes entropy.

Some steps belong to two classes. The mnemonic is an encoding of the entropy followed by a checksum. EIP-55 is a checksum carried by an encoding: its check bits are stored in the case of letters that already encode the address. The address is the Keccak-256 digest of the public key truncated to its last 160 bits; a truncated hash is again a hash with a shorter output, which is why Figure 1 tags that stage Hashing · truncation. Conversely, the checksum of BIP-39 and the case pattern of EIP-55 are computed with hash functions without being hashes in purpose: the class of a step is set by what it is for, not by the primitive inside it.

3.1 From entropy to the BIP-39 mnemonic

BIP-39 turns the entropy into a sentence of common words that a person can write down. The entropy must be a multiple of 32 bits, between 128 and 256; a checksum made of the first ENT/32 bits of its SHA-256 digest is appended, and the concatenation is cut into groups of 11 bits, "each encoding a number from 0-2047, serving as an index into a wordlist" [23, "Generating the mnemonic"]:

\mathrm{CS} = \frac{\mathrm{ENT}}{32}, \qquad \mathrm{MS} = \frac{\mathrm{ENT} + \mathrm{CS}}{11},
(1)

where MS is the number of words. The five admissible sizes give (\mathrm{ENT}, \mathrm{CS}, \mathrm{MS}) = (128, 4, 12), (160, 5, 15), (192, 6, 18), (224, 7, 21) and (256, 8, 24). The step is an encoding with a checksum (§2): the words are a bijective image of the \mathrm{ENT} + \mathrm{CS} bits, and the checksum is a function of the entropy, so a mnemonic carries exactly ENT bits of information. The word list has 2048 entries; the standard describes an ideal list as one in which "it's enough to type the first four letters to unambiguously identify the word", similar words are avoided and the list is sorted, and it requires words to be encoded in UTF-8 under Normalization Form Compatibility Decomposition (NFKD) [23, "Wordlist"].

In the worked example the {{fig.example.entropyBits}} entropy bits are followed by the {{fig.example.checksumLength}} checksum bits {{fig.example.checksumBits}} and cut into {{fig.example.words}} word indices (Figure 3). The first eleven groups are pure entropy; the last index, {{fig.example.lastIndex}} ("{{fig.example.lastWord}}"), holds the last seven entropy bits followed by the checksum. This mixed structure of the last word is what §4.2 and §4.3 test.

3.2 From the mnemonic to the seed

The seed is not the entropy but a key stretched from the sentence. BIP-39 uses "the PBKDF2 function with a mnemonic sentence (in UTF-8 NFKD) used as the password and the string "mnemonic" + passphrase (again in UTF-8 NFKD) used as the salt", with 2048 iterations of HMAC-SHA512 and a derived key of 512 bits; an absent passphrase is the empty string [23, "From mnemonic to seed"]. In the notation of RFC 8018 [15, §5.2], with P the normalised sentence and S the salt,

\mathrm{seed} = U_1 \oplus U_2 \oplus \cdots \oplus U_{2048}, \qquad U_1 = \mathrm{HMAC}_P\bigl(S \,\|\, \mathrm{INT}(1)\bigr), \quad U_j = \mathrm{HMAC}_P(U_{j-1}),
(2)

where \mathrm{HMAC}_K(x) = H\bigl((K \oplus \mathrm{opad}) \,\|\, H((K \oplus \mathrm{ipad}) \,\|\, x)\bigr) with H = SHA-512 and K the key zero-padded to the 128-byte block of SHA-512, or first hashed if longer [11, §§2–3; 17, §1]. Since SHA-512 already yields 512 bits, a single output block suffices. The sentence is the HMAC key, so the step is keyed key derivation; its purpose is that every candidate sentence or passphrase costs an attacker 2048 evaluations of HMAC instead of one. Against 128 bits of uniform entropy this factor of 2^{11} is immaterial, since the search is already infeasible (§4.9); it matters when the secret is weak, as a passphrase chosen by a person may be.

Two consequences of the definition deserve emphasis. First, the passphrase is a second factor that nothing checks: "every passphrase generates a valid seed (and thus a deterministic wallet) but only the correct one will make the desired wallet available" [23, "From mnemonic to seed"], so a mistyped passphrase silently opens a different, empty wallet. Second, the checksum of §3.1 does not enter the seed. "The conversion of the mnemonic sentence to a binary seed is completely independent from generating the sentence", and software "must compute a checksum for the mnemonic sentence using a wordlist and issue a warning if it is invalid" [23, "From mnemonic to seed"]: an invalid checksum calls for a warning, not a rejection. The explorer of Figure 2 follows the standard in this respect: it warns about an invalid checksum and derives the seed all the same. The conversion is also one-way: the sentence cannot be recovered from the seed [23, "Shortcomings"].

3.3 BIP-32: master key and child keys

BIP-32 turns one seed into a tree of keys. Every node is an extended key: a key on secp256k1 together with a 256-bit chain code [30, "Extended keys"]. The root is computed from the seed S as

I = \mathrm{HMAC\text{-}SHA512}(\text{key} = \texttt{"Bitcoin seed"},\ S), \qquad k_m = \mathrm{parse}_{256}(I_L), \quad c_m = I_R,
(3)

where I_L and I_R are the left and right 32 bytes of I; the master key is invalid if k_m = 0 or k_m \ge n [30, "Master key generation"]. A child of index i is derived from its parent (k_{\mathrm{par}}, c_{\mathrm{par}}) by the function CKDpriv:

\begin{gathered} I = \begin{cases} \mathrm{HMAC}\bigl(c_{\mathrm{par}},\ \texttt{0x00} \,\|\, \mathrm{ser}_{256}(k_{\mathrm{par}}) \,\|\, \mathrm{ser}_{32}(i)\bigr), & i \ge 2^{31},\\ \mathrm{HMAC}\bigl(c_{\mathrm{par}},\ \mathrm{ser}_P(k_{\mathrm{par}} G) \,\|\, \mathrm{ser}_{32}(i)\bigr), & i < 2^{31}, \end{cases}\\ k_i = \mathrm{parse}_{256}(I_L) + k_{\mathrm{par}} \bmod n, \qquad c_i = I_R, \end{gathered}
(4)

where HMAC is HMAC-SHA512, the first case is a hardened child, the second a normal one, and \mathrm{ser}_P is the 33-byte compressed form of a point [30, "Conventions"; "Private parent key → private child key"]. Indices from 0 to 2^{31} - 1 are normal; those from 2^{31} to 2^{32} - 1 are hardened and are written with an apostrophe. The difference lies only in the data hashed: a hardened child hashes the parent's private key, a normal child its public key. A normal child can therefore also be derived without any private key, by the function CKDpub,

K_i = \mathrm{parse}_{256}(I_L)\, G + K_{\mathrm{par}}, \qquad i < 2^{31},
(5)

which BIP-32 defines for normal children only; deriving a private child from a public parent "is not possible" [30]. CKDpub is what makes watch-only wallets possible: the extended public key of the example at {{fig.example.changePath}}, printed below, lets anyone compute every receiving address of the account without being able to spend from it.

The same algebra has a price. Since I_L of a normal child is computable from the parent's extended public key, a single leaked child private key gives

k_{\mathrm{par}} = k_i - \mathrm{parse}_{256}(I_L) \bmod n .
(6)

BIP-32 states it plainly: "knowledge of a parent extended public key plus any non-hardened private key descending from it is equivalent to knowing the parent extended private key (and thus every private and public key descending from it)" [30, "Security"]. The button "Recover the parent private key" of Figure 4 performs equation (6) on the example and compares the result with the key derived from the seed. Hardened derivation blocks the leak, because its I_L depends on k_{\mathrm{par}}, which the extended public key does not contain. This is why BIP-44 hardens its first three levels (§3.4): a leak below the account level reaches that account's chains, but neither the other accounts, nor the other coins, nor the master key.

A derived key is invalid when \mathrm{parse}_{256}(I_L) \ge n or k_i = 0, in which case "one should proceed with the next value for i", an event that "has probability lower than 1 in 2127" [30]. Its dominant term is computed exactly in §4.5. The implementation used here reports such an event as an error instead of moving to the next index; at that probability the difference has no practical consequence. Serialised extended keys are 78-byte structures (version, depth, parent fingerprint, child number, chain code and key) followed by a 32-bit checksum and encoded in Base58, "a Base58-encoded string of exactly 111 characters" beginning with xprv or xpub [30, "Serialization format"].

3.4 BIP-44 paths and wallet conventions

BIP-32 allows any tree; BIP-44 fixes one shape for it, the path m / purpose' / coin_type' / account' / change / address_index, in which the apostrophe marks hardened derivation [22, "Path levels"]. The purpose is the constant 44' (0x8000002C), following BIP-43. The coin type separates currencies; its registry is delegated to SatoshiLabs' SLIP-0044, where Ether has the number 60, and the hardened component for coin type n is \texttt{0x80000000} + n [22, 26]. The account level separates independent sets of funds. The change level is 0 for the external chain of receiving addresses and 1 for the internal chain of change addresses, and the last level numbers the addresses; these two levels use normal derivation, so that an account's extended public key can generate all its addresses [22]. When a wallet is restored, the standard sets an address gap limit of 20: "If the software hits 20 unused addresses in a row, it expects there are no used addresses beyond this point and stops searching the address chain" [22, "Address gap limit"].

The standard leaves room for different readings, and Ethereum wallets have adopted at least three conventions for numbering the accounts that a user sees. MetaMask derives account i at m/44'/60'/0'/0/i: its HD keyring fixes the parent path m/44'/60'/0'/0 and derives the child i, and its documentation states that it supports only this default path when a seed phrase is imported [12]. Rainbow uses the same default path [24]. The Ledger Live convention places the index at the account level, m/44'/60'/i'/0/0, and the legacy convention of MyEtherWallet and MyCrypto at a fourth level, m/44'/60'/0'/i; both appear among the hardware-wallet paths of the MetaMask extension and the first in Rainbow's Ledger code [12, 24].

The consequence for a user who imports a phrase into another wallet follows directly. For i = 0 the MetaMask and Ledger Live conventions name the same key, m/44'/60'/0'/0/0, but they part from the second account on: m/44'/60'/0'/0/1 against m/44'/60'/1'/0/0. The legacy convention differs from the first account, since its m/44'/60'/0'/0 is, under BIP-44, the node of the external chain rather than an address. The same twelve words can thus show different addresses, or empty accounts, in different wallets while the funds remain intact at another path; the remedy is to select the matching path, not to doubt the phrase. 3.5 secp256k1: the public key

The private key at the end of the path is a scalar of the curve secp256k1, defined in SEC 2 over the prime field \mathbb{F}_p [6, §2.4.1]:

y^2 = x^3 + 7, \qquad p = 2^{256} - 2^{32} - 2^9 - 2^8 - 2^7 - 2^6 - 2^4 - 1, \qquad K = kG,
(7)

with the base point G of prime order n and cofactor h = 1; SEC 2 calls it "a Koblitz curve". The Ethereum yellow paper takes the private key to be "a randomly selected positive integer … in the range [1, secp256k1n − 1]", where secp256k1n is the order n, and the public key to be "a byte array of size 64", the concatenation of the two coordinates of K [29, Appendix F]. BIP-32 uses instead the 33-byte compressed form, a prefix 02 or 03 for the parity of y followed by x [30, "Conventions"]. Both forms of the example are given below.

The step from k to K is public-key cryptography in the sense of §2: a scalar multiplication, computable in a few hundred group operations by double-and-add, whose inversion is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. BIP-32 assumes that solving it requires 2^{128} group operations [30, "Security"], and the SafeCurves project puts the cost of Pollard's rho method on this curve at 2^{127.8} [2] (§4.9). Transactions are signed with recoverable ECDSA signatures on the curve, and the yellow paper requires the component s of a valid signature to lie in the lower half of its range, 0 < s < n/2 + 1 [29, Appendix F]. A signature is produced with the private key and a fresh secret nonce and is verified with the public key alone, which a hash function on its own does not offer. Homework 1 built a toy hash from the arithmetic of this same curve and examined its group law and statistical behaviour in detail.

3.6 Keccak-256 and the address

The yellow paper defines the address as "the rightmost 160-bits of the Keccak-256 hash of the corresponding ECDSA public key" [29, Appendix F, eq. (316)]:

A(k) = \mathcal{B}_{96..255}\bigl(\mathrm{KEC}(x \,\|\, y)\bigr),
(8)

the bytes 12 to 31 of the 32-byte digest of the 64-byte key, without the prefix 04 of the uncompressed SEC encoding. In the example below, the last 40 hex digits of the digest are the address.

Keccak-256 is a sponge. A permutation f of a state of b = 1600 bits is split into a rate of r bits and a capacity of c = b - r bits; the padded message is absorbed r bits at a time, each block being added to the rate part of the state before f is applied, and the digest is then read from the rate part. FIPS 202 defines the family KECCAK[c] as the sponge built on the permutation KECCAK-p[1600, 24] with the padding rule pad10*1 and the rate 1600 - c, and the 256-bit functions use c = 512, hence r = 1088 [18, §5.2; 3]. The capacity is never output, and "the capacity is double the digest length" [18, §6.1]. A 64-byte public key, plus its padding, fits in one block of 136 bytes, so the address costs exactly one application of the permutation.

The permutation Keccak-f[1600] acts on "a 5-by-5-by-w array of bits" with w = 64 [18, §3.1]: twenty-five lanes of 64 bits, which Figure 5 draws as a lattice. Each of its 24 rounds (12 + 2\ell with w = 2^{\ell}, \ell = 6 [3]) composes five step mappings, \mathrm{Rnd}(A, i_r) = \iota(\chi(\pi(\rho(\theta(A)))), i_r) [18, §3.2–3.3]. The step θ adds to every bit the parities of two neighbouring columns; ρ rotates every lane by its own offset; π rearranges the lanes; χ, the only non-linear step, combines each bit with two others of its row; and ι adds a round constant to one lane. Figure 5 follows a single-bit difference between two states through these steps, and §4.6 measures how fast it spreads.

3.7 EIP-55: the mixed-case checksum

An address written in hexadecimal has no redundancy: every string of 40 hex digits is a valid address, so a typing error sends funds to another valid address. EIP-55, by Buterin and Van de Sande, adds a checksum without changing the alphabet or the length, by giving a meaning to the case of the letters a–f [5]. The rule is to "convert the address to hex, but if the ith digit is a letter (ie. it's one of abcdef) print it in uppercase if the 4ith bit of the hash of the lowercase hexadecimal address is 1 otherwise print it in lowercase", where the hash is Keccak-256 of the 40 lower-case digits encoded as ASCII [5, "Specification"; "Implementation"]. With h_i the i-th hex digit of that digest, the rule reads

a_i \text{ is printed in upper case} \iff a_i \in \{\mathtt{a}, \dots, \mathtt{f}\} \ \text{and}\ h_i \ge 8,
(9)

which is the test of the reference implementations (a nibble of at least 8 is one whose leading bit is 1). Each letter thus carries one check bit and each decimal digit none, so the number of check bits of an address equals its number of letters: the address of the example has {{fig.example.letters}} letters, hence as many check bits. EIP-55 is a checksum carried by an encoding (§2): it adds no secrecy, a wallet that ignores case still reads the same address, and its whole purpose is to make a random typing error unlikely to pass. The EIP estimates that "on average there will be 15 check bits per address, and the net probability that a randomly generated address if mistyped will accidentally pass a check is 0.0247%" [5, "Rationale"]; §4.8 derives both numbers.

§4 Statistical properties

The statistical model of this section is the usual idealisation of the hash functions in the pipeline as random functions: on distinct inputs their outputs are independent and uniformly distributed bit strings. Every stage then has an exact law, and every experiment tests one consequence of it. The general null hypothesis, for the bits X_1, \dots, X_m of the output of a stage, is

H_0:\ X_1, \dots, X_m \ \text{i.i.d.} \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}\bigl(\tfrac12\bigr),
(10)

specialised below to the statistic at hand. Some quantities are exact, whatever the hash functions do; they are proved, not simulated, and the simulation then checks the implementation. Entropy is drawn in the simulations from a seeded statistical generator so that every number is reproducible from the seed printed beside it; the consequences of that choice are discussed in §7. Proportions are compared with their exact values by the two-sided exact binomial test (the rule of R's binom.test: the total probability of all outcomes no more likely than the one observed) and given with 95% Wilson intervals; distributions are compared by Pearson's chi-square test, with the tails merged until every expected count is at least 5 and, since the null laws have no estimated parameter, one degree of freedom fewer than the number of cells.

Four rules of interpretation apply throughout. A p-value is the probability, computed under H_0, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one observed; it is not the probability that H_0 is true. A large p-value means that the sample is consistent with H_0, not that H_0 has been proved. Since this section reports 31 p-values, some of them on overlapping data, an occasional value below 0.05 would be expected even if every hypothesis held; the reading follows the whole set rather than any single value. Finally, as Homework 1 found for its toy hash, statistical indistinguishability from a random function is necessary for a cryptographic primitive but not sufficient: a function can pass every test of this section and still be easy to invert, as §7 shows.

4.1 The entropy ledger

For any function f and discrete random variable X,

H(f(X)) \le H(X), \qquad \text{with equality if and only if } f \text{ is injective on the support of } X .
(11)

A deterministic step can preserve or destroy entropy; it can never create it. Applied along the pipeline, inequality (11) bounds the entropy of every stage by that of the generation step, and a second bound is the length of the representation itself. With M the mnemonic and W a passphrase chosen independently of it, and uniform entropy, H(\mathrm{seed}) \le H(M, W) = \mathrm{ENT} + H(W): the seed of a 12-word mnemonic with an empty passphrase is {{fig.entropyLedger.w12.seedBits}} bits long but carries at most {{fig.entropyLedger.w12.entropy}} bits of entropy, and its length is no measure of its strength. The address carries at most the smaller of the entropy and its 160 bits: {{fig.entropyLedger.w12.addressEntropy}} bits for 12 words and {{fig.entropyLedger.w24.addressEntropy}} for 24, in which case on average about 2^{256}/2^{160} = 2^{96} private keys share each address. Figure 7 sets the two bounds side by side for every stage.

4.2 The BIP-39 checksum

Two error models are considered. In the first, a sequence of 12 or 24 words is drawn uniformly from the list. Whatever the first ENT bits are, exactly one of the 2^{\mathrm{CS}} values of the checksum bits is valid, so the acceptance rate is exactly 2^{-\mathrm{CS}}, that is 1/16 for 12 words and 1/256 for 24, and H_0: X \sim \mathrm{Bin}(N, 2^{-\mathrm{CS}}) for the number X of valid sequences among N. This is an exact statement, so the test checks the implementation rather than SHA-256. Of {{fig.checksum.w12.n}} random 12-word sequences, {{fig.checksum.w12.count}} were valid (proportion {{fig.checksum.w12.rate}}, exact value {{fig.checksum.w12.expected}}, p = {{fig.checksum.w12.p}}); of {{fig.checksum.w24.n}} 24-word sequences, {{fig.checksum.w24.count}} (p = {{fig.checksum.w24.p}}). The figure of BIP-39 itself, "1-in-256 errors will be missed" [23, "Shortcomings"], is the 24-word case; for the 12 words that MetaMask generates [12] the checksum has four bits and misses one random sequence in sixteen.

In the second model a valid mnemonic suffers one error: a uniformly chosen word is replaced by a uniformly chosen different word. At a position other than the last, the entropy changes while the checksum stays, and under the random-function model the new SHA-256 digest matches the old checksum with probability 2^{-\mathrm{CS}}. At the last position the probability is exact. Claim: exactly 2^{11-\mathrm{CS}} - 1 of the 2047 other words keep a valid mnemonic valid when they replace its last word, that is 127 for 12 words and 7 for 24. Proof: the last word consists of the final 11 - \mathrm{CS} entropy bits followed by the CS checksum bits, and the other words fix the remaining entropy. Each of the 2^{11-\mathrm{CS}} values of the free bits determines the whole entropy, hence its checksum, so exactly one of the 2^{\mathrm{CS}} completions is valid; of the 2^{11-\mathrm{CS}} valid last words one is the original, which leaves 2^{11-\mathrm{CS}} - 1, whatever values SHA-256 takes. The overall probability that a substitution goes undetected is therefore

P(\text{undetected}) = \frac{\mathrm{MS} - 1}{\mathrm{MS}}\, 2^{-\mathrm{CS}} + \frac{1}{\mathrm{MS}} \cdot \frac{2^{11 - \mathrm{CS}} - 1}{2047},
(12)

which is {{fig.substitution.w12.overall.expected}} for 12 words and {{fig.substitution.w24.overall.expected}} for 24. 4.4 Uniformity and avalanche of the seed, the keys and the address

For {{fig.params.pipeline.n}} simulated wallets with uniform 128-bit entropy and an empty passphrase, the full pipeline was run to m/44'/60'/0'/0/0, together with two twins: wallet A, whose entropy differs from the original in one uniformly chosen bit, and wallet B, the sibling account at index 1 of the same mnemonic. For a stage of m bits (the seed, 512; the private key and the x-coordinate of the public key, 256; the address, 160) three statistics were recorded: the Hamming weight W of the original (uniformity), its Hamming distance D_A to twin A (avalanche) and, for the private key and the address, its distance D_B to twin B (independence of sibling keys). Under the random-function model

W,\ D_A,\ D_B \sim \mathrm{Bin}\bigl(m, \tfrac12\bigr), \qquad \mathbb{E} = \frac{m}{2}, \qquad \mathrm{sd} = \frac{\sqrt{m}}{2},
(13)

because the weight of a uniform string, and the distance between two independent uniform strings, are sums of m independent fair bits. The model is an approximation for the keys: a private key lies in [1, n-1] rather than in [0, 2^{256}), and x lies below p and is the abscissa of a point of the curve; the first departure concerns a set of relative size about 2^{-128}, far below what {{fig.params.pipeline.n}} samples can resolve, and the second a structure that a count of bits does not see.

Figure 8 shows each distribution against its binomial law and Table 3 lists the tests. One flipped bit of entropy changes on average {{fig.stages.seed.avalanche.mean}} bits of the seed (expected {{fig.stages.seed.avalanche.expectedMean}}), {{fig.stages.privateKey.avalanche.mean}} of the private key and {{fig.stages.address.avalanche.mean}} of the address (expected {{fig.stages.privateKey.avalanche.expectedMean}} and {{fig.stages.address.avalanche.expectedMean}}); the sibling keys differ in {{fig.stages.privateKey.sibling.mean}} bits on average, as two unrelated keys would. None of the stage tests of Table 3 rejects at the 5% level, the smallest p-value being {{fig.stages.address.sibling.p}} for the sibling distance of addresses. Figure 9 looks at the address bit by bit: the number of ones at each of the 160 positions is \mathrm{Bin}(N_w, \tfrac12) under H_0, with N_w the number of addresses in the sample, and the sum of the squared standardised counts, \chi^2(160) = {{fig.addressBits.all.statistic}}, gives p = {{fig.addressBits.all.p}}. {{fig.addressBits.all.beyondTwoSigma}} positions lie beyond ±1.96, where 8 are expected, and the largest |z|, {{fig.addressBits.all.maxAbsZ}}, is below the Bonferroni threshold for 160 two-sided tests at the 5% level, about 3.6.

These results say that, within the power of {{fig.params.pipeline.n}} samples, the outputs of every stage are, in these statistics, consistent with independent fair coin flips and that a one-bit change at the start reaches about half of the bits of every later stage. They do not say that the stages are secure. The permutation Keccak-f[1600] of §3.6 is itself invertible by design and passes the diffusion test of §4.6; one-wayness is a property that no count of bits can establish.

4.5 The range of the private key

BIP-32 rejects a derivation whose I_L, read as an integer, is at least the group order n (§3.3). If I_L is uniform on [0, 2^{256}), the probability of this event is exact:

P(I_L \ge n) = \frac{2^{256} - n}{2^{256}}, \qquad 2^{256} - n = \texttt{0x14551231950b75fc4402da1732fc9bebf} \approx 2^{128.35},
(14)

which evaluates to {{fig.ledger.invalidKeyProbability}}, that is 2 to the power {{fig.ledger.invalidKeyLog2}}, in agreement with the bound "lower than 1 in 2127" of BIP-32. This is one quantity that no simulation can estimate. A Monte Carlo estimate from N trials of an event of probability q has relative standard error \sqrt{(1-q)/(Nq)}, so a single expected occurrence already requires N \approx 1/q \approx 2^{128} derivations; the value has to be computed, and the build computes it from the integer n of SEC 2. Because such values are rejected rather than reduced, an accepted I_L is uniform on [0, n) under the random-function model, and the child key of equation (4), a shift of it modulo n, is then uniform on [1, n-1], the range the yellow paper requires.

4.6 Diffusion in Keccak-f[1600]

The hash of the address is only as good as the diffusion of its permutation. Pairs of uniformly random 1600-bit states that differ in one uniformly chosen bit were passed through Keccak-f[1600], and the number of differing bits was recorded after every step of the first rounds and after every round. The first steps are exact. Claim: after θ of round 1 the states differ in exactly 11 bits. Proof: θ adds to every bit the parities of column (x-1, z) and of column (x+1, z-1). A difference at (x, y, z) changes the parity of column (x, z) only, and that parity is added to the five bits of column (x+1, z) and to the five bits of column (x-1, z+1); together with the original bit, which θ leaves different, that makes 1 + 5 + 5 = 11. The steps ρ and π only move bits, so they preserve the count; ι adds the same constant to both states, so it leaves the difference unchanged. Only χ, being non-linear, makes the count depend on the states. The simulated mean after θ of round 1 is {{fig.diffusion.all.r1theta}}, as the proof requires.

After the full first round the mean is {{fig.diffusion.all.r1chi}}; after rounds 2, 3 and 4 it is {{fig.diffusion.all.r2}}, {{fig.diffusion.all.r3}} and {{fig.diffusion.all.r4}} (Figure 10). Under the random-function model two outputs are independent uniform states, so their distance is \mathrm{Bin}(1600, \tfrac12), with mean 800 and standard deviation 20. After the 24 rounds the {{fig.diffusion.all.n}} pairs give mean {{fig.diffusion.all.finalMean}} and standard deviation {{fig.diffusion.all.finalSd}}, and the binned chi-square test gives \chi^2({{fig.diffusion.all.df}}) = {{fig.diffusion.all.statistic}}, p = {{fig.diffusion.all.p}}. The distance reaches the level of the binomial law within three rounds out of 24. Whatever the remaining rounds contribute to security is invisible to this statistic: once two states are indistinguishable from independent ones by their distance, more rounds cannot make them more so.

4.7 Statistics of the address

A vanity address is one chosen for a recognisable pattern, such as leading zero digits. In the experiment of Figure 11 a search starts from a uniformly random private key d and tries d, d+1, d+2, \dots until the lower-case address begins with k zero hex digits. Under the random-function model each trial succeeds independently with probability 16^{-k}, so the number of trials T of a search is geometric:

H_0:\ P(T = t) = (1 - 16^{-k})^{t-1}\, 16^{-k}, \quad t = 1, 2, \dots, \qquad \mathbb{E}[T] = 16^{k}.
(15)

For k = 1, {{fig.vanity.k1.searches}} searches took {{fig.vanity.k1.mean}} trials on average, 95% interval {{fig.vanity.k1.low}}–{{fig.vanity.k1.high}}, against an expected {{fig.vanity.k1.expectedMean}}, and the binned chi-square test of the whole distribution gives p = {{fig.vanity.k1.p}}; for k = 2, {{fig.vanity.k2.searches}} searches averaged {{fig.vanity.k2.mean}} trials ({{fig.vanity.k2.low}}–{{fig.vanity.k2.high}}, expected {{fig.vanity.k2.expectedMean}}, p = {{fig.vanity.k2.p}}). Each additional digit multiplies the expected cost by 16, which is the whole economics of vanity addresses; §7 returns to a vanity-address generator whose keys came from a 32-bit seed.

The same searches record the number Z of leading zero digits of every address generated, whose law under H_0 is P(Z = j) = \tfrac{15}{16}\,(\tfrac{1}{16})^{j}. A naive test of this law on all generated addresses would be wrong, because the stopping rule fixes how many addresses reach Z \ge k: exactly one per search. The test used here conditions on the stopping rule. The addresses that did not end a search follow the law of Z given Z < k; those that ended one follow the law of Z given Z \ge k, which by the memorylessness of the geometric law is k plus a geometric variable on \{0, 1, \dots\} with success probability 15/16; the two chi-square statistics are added, as are their degrees of freedom. The p-values are {{fig.vanity.k1.lzP}} for k = 1 and {{fig.vanity.k2.lzP}} for k = 2.

4.8 Undetected errors: BIP-39 and EIP-55

Consider a uniform address whose letters are given a uniformly random case, the model of a mistyped address in EIP-55's rationale. Given its number of letters L, the pattern passes the check with probability 2^{-L}, since each letter must match one independent fair bit. Averaging over L \sim \mathrm{Bin}(40, 6/16) with the probability generating function of the binomial law, \mathbb{E}[s^L] = (1 - \pi + \pi s)^{40} with \pi = 6/16 and s = 1/2, gives

P(\text{pass}) = \mathbb{E}\bigl[2^{-L}\bigr] = \Bigl(1 - \tfrac{6}{16} + \tfrac{3}{16}\Bigr)^{40} = \Bigl(\tfrac{13}{16}\Bigr)^{40},
(16)

about {{fig.eip55.exactPercent}}%, the figure of the EIP. Its "15 check bits per address on average" is \mathbb{E}[L] = 40 \cdot 6/16 = 15. The two statements are consistent, but the second does not imply the first: 2^{-15} is about 0.0031%, eight times smaller. Since t \mapsto 2^{-t} is convex, Jensen's inequality gives \mathbb{E}[2^{-L}] \ge 2^{-\mathbb{E}[L]}: the average is dominated by the addresses with few letters, which are poorly protected. An address with no letter at all, of probability (10/16)^{40} = {{fig.eip55.noLetter}}, is not protected at all.

Two random error models were simulated with {{fig.params.errors.n}} trials each, and a third was enumerated (Figure 13). For random case patterns, {{fig.eip55.randomCase.accepted}} passed against {{fig.eip55.randomCase.expectedAccepted}} expected (p = {{fig.eip55.randomCase.p}}). §5 Historical context

5.1 A chronology

The pipeline assembles four decades of work, in an order that Figure 14 draws to scale. Public-key cryptography was described publicly by Diffie and Hellman in November 1976 [7]. RSA followed as an MIT technical memorandum in April 1977 and as a journal article in Communications of the ACM in February 1978 [25]; the assignment's "1977" is the date of the memorandum. Elliptic-curve cryptography, on which secp256k1 rests, was proposed independently by Miller, at CRYPTO '85 with proceedings published in 1986 [14], and by Koblitz in 1987 [10]. The curve secp256k1 was first published in version 1.0 of SEC 2, dated 20 September 2000 [6, version 1.0, §2.7.1]; the parameters are cited on this page from version 2.0, dated 27 January 2010 [6].

The hash functions came next. A draft of FIPS 180-2, adding SHA-256, SHA-384 and SHA-512 to SHA-1, was announced on 30 May 2001, and the final standard is dated 1 August 2002, effective from 1 February 2003 [16]. The assignment's "standardized in 2001" is therefore the date of the draft: SHA-2 was standardised in 2002. The edition cited here, FIPS 180-4, is dated August 2015 [17]. NIST announced the competition for SHA-3 on 2 November 2007, selected Keccak on 2 October 2012, published a draft of FIPS 202 in May 2014 and the final standard on 4 August 2015 [19]; the assignment's 2015 is correct.

The wallet standards are Bitcoin Improvement Proposals, whose current files carry the status "Deployed" and an "Assigned" date: 11 February 2012 for BIP-32 [30], 10 September 2013 for BIP-39 [23] and 24 April 2014 for BIP-44 [22], all three in the years given by the assignment. SLIP-0044, the registry that gives Ether its coin type, was created on 9 July 2014 [26]. On the Ethereum side, ethereum.org dates the whitepaper to 27 November 2013 and the launch of the main network, Frontier, to 30 July 2015 [8], five days before FIPS 202 was published. EIP-55 was created on 14 January 2016 and is now published, with status Final, as ERC-55 [5]. Table 5 sets the dates of the assignment beside the verified ones; Figure 14 adds the two failures of key generation discussed in §7, disclosed in 2022 and 2023.

5.2 SHA-2 and Keccak / SHA-3

SHA-2 and SHA-3 are both cryptographic hash functions standardised by NIST, but they are built in different ways. SHA-512, specified in FIPS 180-4 [17, §§5.1.2, 5.2.2, 6.4], pads the message with its length, splits it into 1024-bit blocks and feeds them one at a time to a compression function of 80 rounds that updates a chaining value of eight 64-bit words; the digest is the final chaining value. This iterated compression of fixed-size blocks is the construction known as Merkle–Damgård, and the whole internal state is the output. Keccak, designed by Bertoni, Daemen, Peeters and Van Assche and selected as SHA-3, is a sponge (§3.6): a fixed permutation of 1600 bits, a rate through which the message enters and the digest leaves, and a capacity that is never output [3, 18]. FIPS 202 defines four hash functions, SHA3-224 to SHA3-512, and two extendable-output functions, SHAKE128 and SHAKE256, all on the same permutation [18]. The two families are specified side by side, in FIPS 180-4 and FIPS 202, both dated August 2015. Within the pipeline, SHA-256 and SHA-512 belong to BIP-39 and BIP-32, and Keccak-256 to Ethereum.

5.3 Keccak-256 is not SHA3-256

"The four SHA-3 hash functions differ slightly from the instances of KECCAK that were proposed for the SHA-3 competition", FIPS 202 explains: "a two-bit suffix is appended to the messages" [18, §1]. In its notation,

\begin{aligned} \mathrm{SHA3\text{-}256}(M) &= \mathrm{KECCAK}[512](M \,\|\, 01,\ 256),\\ \mathrm{Keccak\text{-}256}(M) &= \mathrm{KECCAK}[512](M,\ 256), \end{aligned}
(17)

the first by [18, §6.1], the second being the competition instance without the suffix. Both use the same permutation, the same capacity c = 512 and the same rate r = 1088; they differ only in the bits that separate the message from the padding. The Keccak team encodes these trailing bits and their number in one "delimited suffix" byte, 0x06 for SHA3-256 [3]. For the competition instance the suffix is empty, and the same rule gives 0x01; this value is derived from the team's formula rather than quoted, and the implementation confirms it, since the sponge with 0x01 reproduces the published Keccak-256 of the empty input [9] and with 0x06 the published SHA3-256 [20]. The suffix is a form of domain separation: it lets FIPS 202 distinguish its hash functions from its extendable-output functions, which use other suffixes, on one permutation. Because the suffix is part of the padded input, the two digests of the same message are unrelated, as Figure 15 shows for any text the reader types.

Ethereum fixed its hash before the standard was final. The yellow paper specifies Keccak-256 "as per version 3 of the winning entry to the SHA-3 contest by Bertoni et al. [2011], rather than the final SHA-3 specification" [29], and the main network was launched on 30 July 2015, five days before FIPS 202 [8]. The name "sha3" nonetheless survives in places: ethereum.org warns that a function called "sha3_256" in Ethereum's code is "not standard sha3" but Keccak-256 [8]. The converse mistake is just as easy: an implementation of SHA3-256 in the sense of FIPS 202, used in place of Keccak-256, yields different digests and therefore different addresses.

§6 Why several primitives

A wallet must satisfy several requirements at once, and they are not compatible with a single algorithm. Taken one at a time, each requirement calls for a property, and each property is supplied by a different primitive.

  1. A backup that a person can transcribe, with error detection. The secret must survive being written on paper and typed back. BIP-39 supplies an encoding into common words and a short checksum; the property needed is a bijection plus redundancy, not secrecy.
  2. Slow, salted processing of possibly weak human input. A passphrase may carry little entropy, so each guess must be expensive. PBKDF2-HMAC-SHA512 supplies a deliberately costly deterministic map; a fast hash would be the wrong tool here, and a slow one the wrong tool everywhere else.
  3. Many keys from one backup, with watch-only derivation. BIP-32 supplies a tree of keys from one seed, and BIP-44 a shared layout of that tree. Its normal derivation lets an extended public key generate addresses without any private key, a property that rests on the group structure of the curve (equation (5)); its hardened derivation limits the damage of a leak.
  4. Proof of ownership that anyone can verify. Spending requires a signature that is produced with a secret and verified with public data. secp256k1 with ECDSA supplies this through the one-way relation K = kG and its group structure; a hash function on its own has no pair of a signing key and a verification key.
  5. Short, fixed-length identifiers. An account needs a name shorter than a 64-byte public key that commits to it. Keccak-256 compresses the key and truncation fixes the length at 160 bits; a signature scheme does not compress arbitrary data into identifiers, while a hash does so with no key and no structure to exploit.
  6. Protection against typing errors at no cost in length. EIP-55 supplies a cheap, public checksum in the letter case. It is meant to add no security, and it adds none: anyone can compute the checksum of any address.
  7. Interfaces for people and programs. Words, hexadecimal and Base58 are encodings that move the same information between representations without loss.

The requirements also pull in opposite directions: a key-stretching function must be slow and a checksum fast; an encoding must be invertible and a hash must not; a signature needs structure that a hash must avoid. A single algorithm would have to compromise on all of them. The design follows instead the principle of separation of concerns: each step does one job, and the interfaces between steps are fixed byte strings. Domain separation keeps the jobs apart even where the same primitive is reused: BIP-39 salts PBKDF2 with the string "mnemonic", BIP-32 keys its master HMAC with "Bitcoin seed" and, in hardened derivation, pads the private key with the byte 0x00 to 33 bytes [30, "Private parent key → private child key"], which also keeps that input apart from a compressed public key, whose first byte is 02 or 03 [30, "Conventions"]; FIPS 202 separates its functions by their suffixes, and EIP-55 hashes the ASCII text of the address, a different input from the public key whose digest produced the address.

The choice of primitives also reflects history rather than a single design. BIP-39 and BIP-32 come from the Bitcoin community and use SHA-256 and SHA-512 of the NIST SHA-2 family; the curve is defined by SEC 2 and shared by BIP-32 and Ethereum; Ethereum chose Keccak-256 before SHA-3 was final; and EIP-55 was added to Ethereum addresses after the network was running. The pipeline is a composition of standards from different bodies and different years, which is one more reason why it uses several primitives.

§7 When the statistical assumption fails

Every argument of §4 assumed that the entropy at the start is uniform on 2^{\mathrm{ENT}} values. Inequality (11) makes the consequence of a failure precise: if the generator can produce only N distinct entropies, every later stage, the seed, the keys and the address, has at most \log_2 N bits of entropy, however long its representation and however good the functions that follow. No derivation downstream can restore what generation did not supply.

The Milk Sad disclosure is a documented instance. The command bx seed of Libbitcoin Explorer, versions 3.0.0 to 3.6.0, drew wallet entropy from the Mersenne Twister generator mt19937, seeded with 32 bits derived from the system clock; in the words of the CVE record, "the use of an mt19937 Mersenne Twister PRNG restricts the internal entropy to 32 bits regardless of settings", which "allows remote attackers to recover any wallet private keys generated from "bx seed" entropy output", and the weakness "was exploited in the wild in June and July 2023" [13]. The researchers who disclosed it note that the generator "can at most have 2^32 starting positions", so that a wallet is found "after a maximum of about 4.29 billion attempts", and estimate that over 900,000 US dollars' worth of cryptocurrency, at August 2023 exchange rates, was moved [13]. Whatever mnemonic length a user selected, 24 words included, the key space was at most 2^{32} rather than up to 2^{256}. Table 4 shows the difference at the rate of the JavaScript implementation of this page, on one thread: an expected {{fig.brute.b32.expectedYears}} years and at most {{fig.brute.b32.exhaustYears}} years for the 32-bit space, against {{fig.brute.b256.expectedYears}} years for a 24-word mnemonic. The 32-bit search, moreover, divides exactly among parallel workers: w workers need 1/w of the time.

The Profanity vanity-address generator failed in the same way. According to the disclosure by 1inch, "Profanity used a random 32-bit vector to seed 256-bit private keys"; 1inch later built proof-of-concept code able to "recover private keys from any vanity address generated with Profanity" and advised users to move their assets [1]. Since the tool started from "1 of 4 bln seed private keys" rather than from a mnemonic [1], Table 4 applies to it through the size of the seed space, not through the cost per trial. Each seed, however, starts a long sequence of candidate keys, so that, by 1inch's estimate, recovering every vanity address of six or seven characters "would have required thousands of GPUs and months of time" [1]. According to The Block, the market maker Wintermute said in September 2022 that 160 million dollars had been stolen from its Ethereum vault, and experts quoted in the same report blamed a Profanity-generated address; the figure and the attribution rest on press reports, not on an official post-mortem [27].

Randomness is needed after generation as well. An ECDSA signature uses a fresh secret nonce (§3.5), and Breitner and Heninger recovered keys from signatures whose nonces were biased: "we compute hundreds of Bitcoin private keys and dozens of Ethereum, Ripple, SSH, and HTTPS private keys" [4]. Here too the defect lies in the distribution of a secret, not in the functions applied to it.

The statistical lesson is the one Homework 1 drew for its toy hash, and the experiments of this page illustrate it directly. Their wallets were generated, for reproducibility, from Mulberry32, a statistical generator whose whole state is one 32-bit integer: every wallet of Figure 8 is determined by the seed {{fig.params.pipeline.seed}}, and the whole sample belongs to a space of at most 2^{32} sequences. Every test of §4 was passed nonetheless. This is to be expected: with 2^{32} equally likely entropies, a sample of m independently seeded wallets contains a repeated one with probability about m^2/2^{33}, which is negligible for samples of a few thousand; and without repetitions, under the random-function model, distinct inputs give independent uniform outputs however few the possible inputs are. The tests of §4, on weights, distances and frequencies, are therefore blind to the size of the input space. Only a count of collisions could reveal a space of 2^{32} entropies, and by the same formula it would need samples of the order of 2^{16} wallets before a repetition became likely, far beyond the samples used here. A wallet is secure only if its entropy is generated by a cryptographically secure source, and that property must be established by inspecting the generator, not by testing what comes out of the pipeline.

§8 Conclusion

The pipeline from mnemonic to checksummed address was implemented from its standards and checked against published values. The worked example reproduces the address {{fig.example.address}} and the private key that Hardhat publishes for its development mnemonic, together with the addresses of its second and third accounts, and the primitives agree with the reference vectors of BIP-39, BIP-32, Keccak-256, SHA3-256 and EIP-55 (first question). The build checks the address and private key of the first account and the two digests of the empty input, and stops on any disagreement; the second and third accounts are checked by the test suite. The intermediate values printed in §3 are not each compared with a published value: they lie on the computation that produced these verified endpoints.

The steps fall into distinct classes (second question). Only the draw of the entropy is key generation; PBKDF2 and BIP-32 are keyed, deterministic key derivations, the first for stretching and the second for hierarchy; k \mapsto kG on secp256k1 is public-key cryptography; Keccak-256 is hashing, truncated for the address; the BIP-39 checksum and EIP-55 are checksums; and words, hexadecimal, Base58 and letter case are encodings. Several steps combine two classes, and the class of a step is set by its purpose rather than by the primitive inside it.

Under the model of hash functions as random functions, every stage has an exact law, and the samples are consistent with all of them (third question): the acceptance and substitution rates of the BIP-39 checksum match their exact values, including the 127/2047 and 7/2047 of the last word; seeds, keys and addresses have binomial weights and avalanche distances; Keccak-f[1600] reaches the binomial level of diffusion within three rounds; vanity searches are geometric; and EIP-55 lets a random error pass with probability (13/16)^{40}, about {{fig.eip55.exactPercent}}%, a value that also explains the EIP's own figure. None of the 31 tests rejects at the 5% level, which is consistent with the model and proves nothing more.

Historically (fourth question), the pipeline joins public-key ideas of 1976 to 1987, a hash standard of 2002 rather than 2001, wallet proposals of 2012 to 2014 and an address checksum of 2016. Ethereum adopted Keccak-256 before FIPS 202 was final, and the two functions, identical in permutation and rate, differ only in the suffix that separates the message from the padding, which is enough to make their digests unrelated.

The pipeline needs several primitives because its requirements conflict (fifth question): slowness against speed, invertibility against one-wayness, algebraic structure against its absence. Each primitive does one job, and domain separation keeps the jobs apart. Its security, however, depends first of all on a step that none of the others can repair: the generation of the entropy. The Milk Sad and Profanity cases show what follows when that step produces 32 bits instead of 128 or 256, and the experiments of this page show why the statistical tests of §4, at these sample sizes, could not have revealed it.
