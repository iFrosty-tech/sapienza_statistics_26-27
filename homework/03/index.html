§1 Problem statement

The assignment reads, verbatim:

Homework 2 — Using the toy cryptographic scheme based on the secp256k1 elliptic curve developed in Part 1 of the previous homework, encrypt and decrypt a simple message. Describe the main steps of the algorithm and the role of the keys.

The Caesar Cipher: Implement the Caesar cipher to encrypt and decrypt the same message. Discuss the similarities and differences between the two schemes, focusing on their encryption and decryption procedures, key spaces, and statistical properties.

Statistical Cryptanalysis of the Caesar Cipher: Given a sufficiently large corpus of text in a known language, design and implement a simple algorithm to recover the key of the Caesar cipher by exploiting the statistical distribution of letters. Test the algorithm on ciphertexts of different lengths and discuss its effectiveness and limitations.

Optional: Compare alternative approaches, such as exhaustive key search, letter-frequency analysis, and n-gram scoring.

The toy scheme developed in Part 1 uses the secp256k1 elliptic curve but restricts the private key to a small range to make experiments computationally feasible. Design and implement a simple attack to recover the private key, or otherwise decrypt the message, exploiting this restricted key space. Discuss the differences between this attack and the statistical attack against the Caesar cipher. Explain why the same approach would not necessarily be feasible against a properly configured ECC scheme using a sufficiently large private-key space.

Optional: Discuss alternative attacks and explain which weaknesses arise from the restricted key space and which, if any, could arise from the design of the toy scheme itself.

The assignment sets two ciphers side by side that fail for opposite reasons. The Caesar cipher has a key space of 26 shifts, {{fig.keySpace.caesar.log2}} bits, and its ciphertext carries the letter statistics of the plaintext almost intact; the toy elliptic-curve scheme produces ciphertexts that no simple statistic distinguishes from random bytes, but its private key is drawn from [1, 2^{20}], a space of {{fig.keySpace.toy.log2}} bits against the {{fig.keySpace.secp256k1.log2}} bits of a full secp256k1 key. The first is broken by statistical inference from the ciphertext, the second by an exact search of the key space that never looks at the ciphertext at all.

The page is organised as follows. §2 builds the toy public-key encryption scheme on the key generation Q = kG of Exercise 1 of Homework 1, encrypts and decrypts a message and describes the role of each key; §3 encrypts the same message with the Caesar cipher; §4 compares the two schemes in their procedures, key spaces and statistical properties. §5 designs and tests five estimators of the Caesar key on ciphertexts of 1 to 500 letters, which answers the optional comparison of approaches. §6 recovers the private key of the toy scheme by three searches of its restricted key space, contrasts this attack with the statistical one and explains why it does not scale to a full-size key. §7, the optional part, separates the weaknesses due to the restricted key space from those of the toy design and concludes. Every number on the page is computed when the page is built, from the parameters and seeds listed in the appendix.

§2 Toy ECC encryption and decryption

2.1 Key generation and the restricted key space

The curve is secp256k1 of the SEC 2 standard: the points (x, y) with y^2 = x^3 + 7 over \mathbb{F}_p, p = 2^{256} - 2^{32} - 977, together with the point at infinity O, and a base point G of prime order n \approx 2^{256} with cofactor 1, so that G generates the whole group [2, §2.4.1]. Part 1 of Exercise 1 implemented the group law and the map from a private key to a public key: a private key is an integer k and the public key is the point Q = kG, computed by double-and-add. On secp256k1 the program of that exercise accepts any key in [1, n - 1], a 256-bit integer, as SEC 1 prescribes for key-pair generation [1, §3.2.1].

The toy scheme keeps the curve, the base point and the map k \mapsto kG unchanged and restricts only the distribution of the private key:

k \sim \mathrm{Uniform}\{1, 2, \dots, 2^{B}\}, \qquad Q = kG, \qquad B = 20 .
(1)

The key is drawn by taking \lceil B/8 \rceil random bytes, keeping their B low-order bits and adding one, which gives an exactly uniform value with no modular bias. The size B = 20 is set by the cost of the simplest attack. The brute-force walk of §6 runs at {{fig.attack.brute.rate}} group operations per second on the build machine, so it recovers a {{fig.params.toyBits}}-bit key in {{fig.attack.brute.meanSecondsToy}} s on average and in {{fig.attack.brute.worstSecondsToy}} s in the worst case: short enough for the attacks to run live in a browser, long enough for their costs to be measured and to differ by orders of magnitude. Nothing else in the scheme depends on B; the restriction is the designed weakness of the exercise.

2.2 Encryption

A key pair of this kind does not encrypt by itself: the scalar multiplication is a one-way map, not a cipher. The scheme used here is hashed EC-ElGamal. ElGamal's public-key cryptosystem [3] masks a message with a Diffie–Hellman value shared between a fresh random exponent of the sender and the public key of the receiver; transposed to the group of an elliptic curve, and with the shared value passed through a hash function to produce a keystream, this is the structure of the Elliptic Curve Integrated Encryption Scheme (ECIES) [1, §5.1], [5, §4.5.1]. The hash is Keccak-256, the function of Ethereum examined in Exercise 1. To encrypt a message m, a string of \ell bytes in UTF-8, to the public key Q, the sender:

  1. draws an ephemeral scalar r uniformly from [1, n - 1], by rejection sampling of 32 random bytes, and computes the ephemeral point R = rG;
  2. computes the shared point S = rQ;
  3. derives a keystream from the compressed encoding of S (33 bytes: a prefix 02 or 03 for the parity of y, then x [1, §2.3.3]) by Keccak-256 in counter mode, with a 4-byte big-endian counter \langle i \rangle_4 and the label KS:
K = \Big( H\big(\langle 0 \rangle_4 \,\|\, \texttt{KS} \,\|\, \mathrm{comp}(S)\big) \,\|\, H\big(\langle 1 \rangle_4 \,\|\, \texttt{KS} \,\|\, \mathrm{comp}(S)\big) \,\|\, \cdots \Big)_{1 \dots \ell}, \qquad H = \text{Keccak-256};
(2)
  1. masks the message with the keystream, byte by byte,
c = m \oplus K ;
(3)
  1. and computes a key-confirmation tag over the shared point and the masked bytes,
t = H\big(\texttt{TAG} \,\|\, \mathrm{comp}(S) \,\|\, c\big) .
(4)

The ciphertext is the triple (R, c, t): the compressed ephemeral point, the masked bytes and the 32-byte tag. The ephemeral scalar r and the shared point S are discarded. 2.3 Decryption and correctness

The receiver, who holds k, decodes R and rejects it unless it is a point of the curve other than O; computes S' = kR; recomputes the tag H(\texttt{TAG} \,\|\, \mathrm{comp}(S') \,\|\, c) and rejects the ciphertext if it differs from t; and otherwise derives the keystream K' from S' by (2) and returns m = c \oplus K'. Decryption is correct because the receiver obtains the same point as the sender:

S' = kR = k(rG) = (kr)G = (rk)G = r(kG) = rQ = S ,
(5)

which uses only the fact, recalled in §2.2 of Exercise 1, that k \mapsto kG is a homomorphism from \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z} to the group of the curve, together with the commutativity of the product of integers. With S' = S the keystream is the same, (m \oplus K) \oplus K = m, and the tag matches. A wrong private key gives a different point, a different tag and a rejection rather than a garbled message; so does any change to c or to t.

2.4 The role of the keys

  • The private key k is the only long-term secret, held by the receiver. It is needed for decryption, through S = kR, and for nothing else. Recovering it from Q is the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP), and the whole security of the scheme against an attacker who sees only public values rests on that problem being hard.
  • The public key Q = kG is published. Anyone can encrypt to it, and it is the only information about k that leaves the receiver. Encryption needs no shared secret, which is what makes the scheme asymmetric.
  • The ephemeral scalar r is chosen by the sender for one message, kept secret and discarded after encryption; only its trace R = rG is transmitted. It randomises the encryption: two encryptions of the same message to the same key use different r, hence different S and unrelated keystreams. It must be secret, because anyone who knows r computes S = rQ without k, and fresh, because a repeated r repeats the keystream (§7).
  • The shared point S is never transmitted. The sender computes it as rQ and the receiver as kR; an eavesdropper who sees Q and R but neither scalar faces the elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman problem. The keystream and the tag are derived from it, with the labels KS and TAG keeping the two uses apart.

2.5 A teaching scheme, not ECIES

The scheme has the shape of ECIES but none of its guarantees, and it must not be used to protect anything. Standard ECIES derives its keying material with a specified key derivation function [1, §3.6] and splits it into an encryption key and a separate key for a standard MAC scheme [1, §3.7, §5.1.3]; the toy scheme uses an ad hoc counter-mode hash for the keystream and an ad hoc hash of the shared point and the ciphertext as tag, separated only by their labels. Its arithmetic is written for readability with JavaScript integers of arbitrary size, with no attempt at constant-time execution, so the time it takes depends on the bits of k and r, the starting point of the timing and power-analysis attacks on implementations [5, §5.3]. A real deployment would use a full-range private key, a vetted implementation of a standardised scheme, with a standard key derivation function, an authenticated encryption or MAC algorithm with independent keys, validation of every received point [1, §3.2.2] and a cryptographically secure source of randomness for k and r [1, §3.10]; §7 returns to these differences.

§3 Caesar cipher

The Caesar cipher is the simplest additive substitution: every letter of the plaintext is replaced by the letter a fixed number of places further along the alphabet, wrapping around from Z to A [9, Ch. 1]. Identifying the letters A, B, …, Z with the residues 0, 1, \dots, 25 of \mathbb{Z}/26\mathbb{Z}, the key is a residue k, and encryption and decryption are

E_k(x) = (x + k) \bmod 26, \qquad D_k(y) = (y - k) \bmod 26, \qquad D_k(E_k(x)) = x .
(6)

The implementation shifts upper-case and lower-case letters within their own case and leaves every other character unchanged: spaces, digits, punctuation and letters outside A–Z pass through as they are. A Caesar ciphertext therefore keeps the case pattern, the word lengths and the punctuation of the plaintext, which the n-gram and dictionary methods of §5 will exploit. The key k = 0 leaves the text unchanged, so of the 26 keys 25 actually encrypt. Encryption and decryption are the same operation with opposite shifts, and both use the same key: the cipher is symmetric.

§4 Comparison of the two schemes

4.1 Encryption and decryption procedures

The two schemes share one structural feature. Both add a key-dependent mask to the plaintext symbol by symbol and remove it by the inverse operation: the Caesar cipher adds the constant k modulo 26 to every letter, and the toy scheme adds the keystream K modulo 2 to every bit, by (3). In this sense the toy scheme is a stream cipher whose keystream is set up by a public-key key agreement, and the Caesar cipher is a stream cipher whose keystream is a single symbol repeated. Everything else differs.

  • Symmetric against asymmetric. The Caesar cipher encrypts and decrypts with the same key, which sender and receiver must share in advance. The toy scheme encrypts with the public key Q and decrypts with the private key k; the sender needs no secret except the ephemeral r, which it creates itself.
  • Deterministic against randomised. The Caesar cipher maps a message to the same ciphertext every time; the toy scheme draws a fresh r for each encryption. Table 2 encrypts the message of §2 twice under each scheme: the Caesar ciphertexts are identical, whereas the two toy ciphertexts differ in the ephemeral point, in the tag and in {{fig.repeat.differingBits}} of the {{fig.repeat.totalBits}} bits of c, close to the half expected of two independent keystreams. An observer of the Caesar channel learns when a message is repeated; an observer of the toy channel does not.
  • Alphabet and format. The Caesar cipher acts on the 26 letters and preserves everything else, including the length, the word boundaries and the case. The toy scheme acts on bytes, so it encrypts any data, and hides the word structure; it preserves only the length of the message in bytes and adds 65 bytes, the 33 of R and the 32 of t.
  • Integrity. A modified Caesar ciphertext decrypts to a modified plaintext without any warning. The toy scheme rejects any ciphertext whose tag does not match the shared point (§2.3).
  • Cost. Caesar encryption costs one addition per letter. The toy scheme costs two scalar multiplications to encrypt, rG and rQ, and one to decrypt, kR, each of a few hundred group operations, plus one Keccak-256 evaluation per 32 bytes of message.

4.2 Key spaces

Table 1 compares the number of keys. The Caesar cipher has 26 keys, about 2{{fig.keySpace.caesar.log2}}; the toy scheme has 2^{20} = 1\,048\,576, some forty thousand times more; a full secp256k1 key ranges over n - 1 \approx 2^{256} values. The last column gives the time a search of the whole space would take at the rates measured on this machine: the 26 Caesar keys, each tried with the dictionary test of §5, in a fraction of a millisecond; the toy key space by the brute-force walk of §6 in seconds; the full secp256k1 key space in a time that dwarfs the age of the universe.

The size of the key space is necessary for security but far from sufficient. A general monoalphabetic substitution, which replaces each letter by an arbitrary permutation of the alphabet, has 26! ≈ 2{{fig.keySpace.substitution.log2}} keys, more than a modern 128-bit cipher, and is still broken by hand from the letter frequencies [9, Ch. 2]. The Caesar cipher fails on both counts: its key space can be enumerated, and, as the next subsection shows, its ciphertext tells which key is right. The toy scheme fails on the first count only.

4.3 Statistical properties

Figure 1 takes a message of {{fig.compare.chars}} characters ({{fig.compare.letters}} letters) from the held-out text of §5 and shows the histogram of its letters, of the letters of its Caesar ciphertext under the key {{fig.compare.key}}, and of the bytes of its toy ciphertext. The Caesar histogram is the plaintext histogram rotated by {{fig.compare.key}} places, bar for bar: the counts are the same numbers attached to different letters. The toy histogram is flat, with the scatter expected of {{fig.compare.bytes}} draws from 256 equally likely values.

The index of coincidence summarises this contrast in one number. Introduced by Friedman [4], it is the probability that two symbols drawn at random without replacement from a text are equal:

\mathrm{IC} = \frac{\sum_{i} n_i (n_i - 1)}{N (N - 1)} ,
(7)

where n_i is the count of the i-th symbol and N = \sum_i n_i. For a long text whose symbols follow the probabilities p_i it tends to \sum_i p_i^2: {{fig.compare.ic.english}} for the English letter frequencies of the training corpus, against 1/26 \approx {{fig.compare.ic.uniform26}} for uniformly distributed letters and 1/256 \approx {{fig.compare.ic.uniform256}} for uniformly distributed bytes. The index is invariant under any permutation \pi of the symbols, because relabelling only reorders the terms of the sum: if the ciphertext counts are n'_{\pi(i)} = n_i, then \sum_i n'_i (n'_i - 1) = \sum_i n_i (n_i - 1). A Caesar shift is the permutation \pi(i) = (i + k) \bmod 26, so the Caesar ciphertext has exactly the index of its plaintext, {{fig.compare.ic.caesarLetters}}, whatever the key.

Table 3 adds Pearson's \chi^2 tests. Against the English frequencies the Caesar ciphertext gives \chi^2 = {{fig.compare.caesarVsEnglish.statistic}} on {{fig.compare.caesarVsEnglish.df}} degrees of freedom: its letters are nothing like English. Against the English frequencies shifted by the key it gives {{fig.compare.caesarVsShiftedEnglish.statistic}}, exactly the value of the plaintext against unshifted English, because the two comparisons involve the same pairs of numbers. That value still rejects the null hypothesis ({{fig.compare.plainVsEnglish.pEq}}): with {{fig.compare.letters}} letters the test detects the small but real difference between the letter frequencies of one novelist and the average of six others. This is a first warning, taken up in §5, that the \chi^2 statistic is useful as a distance between a text and a language rather than as a test of whether a text is English. The toy ciphertext, finally, is compatible with uniform bytes ({{fig.compare.eccBytesVsUniform256.pEq}}), with an index of {{fig.compare.ic.eccBytes}}, although the UTF-8 bytes of its plaintext are as far from uniform as text can be (\chi^2 = {{fig.compare.plainBytesVsUniform256.statistic}}, index {{fig.compare.ic.plainBytes}}).

The two outcomes have different explanations. The Caesar cipher is a fixed relabelling of the alphabet, so it preserves the distribution of the plaintext up to that relabelling, and every statistic that does not depend on the names of the letters, the index of coincidence first among them, survives encryption unchanged. Recovering the key then amounts to finding the relabelling that turns the ciphertext distribution back into the known distribution of the language, which is the subject of §5. The toy scheme destroys the distribution of the plaintext under a modelling assumption: if Keccak-256 in counter mode behaves as a random function of the shared point, the keystream is uniform and independent of the message, and for every message m and every byte string \gamma of the same length,

\Pr(c = \gamma \mid m) = \Pr(K = \gamma \oplus m) = 2^{-8\ell} ,
(8)

the argument of the one-time pad: the ciphertext has the same uniform distribution whatever the message. Two remarks qualify this result. First, the assumption is an idealisation of the hash, not a property proven of Keccak-256; the test of Table 3 is consistent with it but cannot prove it. Second, and more important here, statistical indistinguishability from random says nothing about the size of the key space. The ciphertext of Figure 1 was produced with a {{fig.params.toyBits}}-bit private key that §6 recovers in a fraction of a second; its bytes look uniform because the keystream is derived from a uniformly random 256-bit ephemeral scalar, not because the private key is strong.

§5 Statistical cryptanalysis of the Caesar cipher

5.1 The corpus

The known language is English, and its statistics are estimated from {{fig.corpus.trainingBooks}} public-domain novels of the nineteenth century from Project Gutenberg [7], by six different authors: Pride and Prejudice by Jane Austen, A Tale of Two Cities by Charles Dickens, Moby-Dick by Herman Melville, The Adventures of Sherlock Holmes by Arthur Conan Doyle, Frankenstein by Mary Shelley and The Adventures of Tom Sawyer by Mark Twain. Each text is stripped of the Project Gutenberg header, licence and footer, and normalised: diacritics are removed, apostrophes inside words are dropped, every other run of non-letters becomes one space and the letters are folded to upper case. The training corpus so obtained holds {{fig.corpus.trainingLetters}} letters in {{fig.corpus.trainingWords}} words. From it are estimated the 26 letter frequencies, the counts of the {{fig.corpus.bigrams}} distinct bigrams and {{fig.corpus.trigrams}} distinct trigrams found inside words, and a dictionary of the {{fig.corpus.dictionaryWords}} most frequent words, which covers {{fig.corpus.dictionaryCoverage}} of the word occurrences of the corpus.

The ciphertexts are drawn from a seventh book, never used for training, by a seventh author: {{fig.corpus.heldoutTitle}} ({{fig.corpus.heldoutYear}}) by {{fig.corpus.heldoutAuthor}}, of which the first {{fig.corpus.heldoutMaxChars}} normalised characters, {{fig.corpus.heldoutLetters}} letters, are kept. Training and test texts are thus disjoint by construction, so that the success rates below are not inflated by attacking the text on which the model was fitted; they measure the realistic situation of an analyst whose model of the language comes from other texts. The books, their roles and the hashes that identify them are listed in the appendix.

5.2 Five estimators of the key

Let y be a ciphertext with N letters and let o_j be the count of the letter j in it. Decrypting with the candidate key \kappa moves the count of the ciphertext letter i + \kappa to the plaintext letter i, so the candidate plaintext has the counts o^{(\kappa)}_i = o_{(i + \kappa) \bmod 26}. Let p_i be the English letter frequencies of the training corpus. Every method below evaluates all 26 candidates and returns the best; they differ only in the criterion by which a candidate plaintext is judged to be English [9, Ch. 1].

Letter frequencies and the \chi^2 distance. The first estimator chooses the key whose candidate plaintext is closest to the English frequencies in Pearson's sense:

\hat\kappa_{\chi^2} = \arg\min_{\kappa} X^2(\kappa), \qquad X^2(\kappa) = \sum_{i=0}^{25} \frac{\big(o^{(\kappa)}_i - N p_i\big)^2}{N p_i} .
(9)

Multinomial maximum likelihood. If the letters of the plaintext are modelled as independent draws from p, the probability of the ciphertext under the key \kappa is \prod_t p_{(y_t - \kappa) \bmod 26}, and the maximum-likelihood estimator maximises the log-likelihood

\hat\kappa_{\mathrm{ML}} = \arg\max_{\kappa} \ell(\kappa), \qquad \ell(\kappa) = \sum_{i=0}^{25} o^{(\kappa)}_i \ln p_i = -N \, H\big(q^{(\kappa)}, p\big) ,
(10)

where q^{(\kappa)} = o^{(\kappa)} / N is the empirical distribution of the candidate plaintext and H(q, p) = -\sum_i q_i \ln p_i the cross-entropy. Since H(q, p) = H(q) + D(q \,\|\, p), and the entropy H(q^{(\kappa)}), like the index of coincidence, does not depend on \kappa, maximising the likelihood is the same as minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence of the candidate plaintext from English. The two letter-frequency estimators are therefore close relatives: the likelihood-ratio statistic G^2 = 2N\,D(q \,\|\, p) and Pearson's X^2 agree to second order when q is close to p. They differ when the counts are small, because X^2 divides by the expected count N p_i: a single J, Q, X or Z, with p_i below one per cent, contributes a large term, whereas in \ell it contributes only a bounded \ln p_i.

N-gram scoring. The letter frequencies ignore the order of the letters. A bigram or trigram model scores each candidate plaintext by the log-probability of its overlapping n-grams:

\hat\kappa_{n} = \arg\max_{\kappa} \sum_{g \,\in\, \mathcal{G}_n(D_\kappa(y))} \ln \hat P(g), \qquad \hat P(g) = \frac{c(g)}{T_n} \text{ if } c(g) > 0, \quad \hat P(g) = \frac{\varepsilon}{T_n} \text{ otherwise},
(11)

where \mathcal{G}_n(x) is the multiset of n-grams of x that lie inside a word, c(g) the count of g in the training corpus, T_n the total number of training n-grams and \varepsilon = {{fig.params.smoothing}} a pseudo-count that gives an n-gram never seen in training a floor below every seen one, instead of probability zero. The score is a pseudo-likelihood: it treats the overlapping n-grams as if they were independent, which they are not, but it ranks the candidates well, which is all an estimator of the key needs. N-grams never span a space, because the Caesar ciphertext keeps the word boundaries of the plaintext.

Exhaustive search with a dictionary. The last method is the plain search of the key space: decrypt with each of the 26 keys and keep the candidate that looks most like English, judged by the fraction of its words that belong to the dictionary of {{fig.corpus.dictionaryWords}} common words. Seen this way, every method of this section is an exhaustive search: with 26 keys, enumeration is free, and the whole difficulty lies in the criterion that recognises the plaintext. The four statistical criteria give every candidate a graded score; the dictionary gives a coarse one, and many candidates of a short text tie at zero.

5.3 Experimental design

For each length L of a grid of 21 values from 1 to 500 letters, {{fig.params.windows}} windows of exactly L letters are drawn from the held-out text, each starting at a letter chosen uniformly at random and keeping the spaces between its words. Each window is encrypted under a key drawn uniformly from \{0, \dots, 25\}, and all five methods attack the same ciphertexts, so the comparison between methods is paired. A trial succeeds when the method's best key is the true key. When several keys tie for the best score, as happens when a ciphertext has no letter, no n-gram or no dictionary word, one of them is chosen uniformly at random, so that a method without information scores the chance level 1/26 \approx {{fig.caesar.baseline}} instead of the bias of a fixed tie-breaking order; the rank of the true key is its mid-rank among tied scores, 1 being the best. The success rate \hat\pi = s/N_w of s successes in N_w = {{fig.params.windows}} trials carries the Wilson score interval [10]

\frac{\hat\pi + \dfrac{z^2}{2N_w} \pm z \sqrt{\dfrac{\hat\pi(1 - \hat\pi)}{N_w} + \dfrac{z^2}{4N_w^2}}}{1 + \dfrac{z^2}{N_w}}, \qquad z = 1.96 ,
(12)

which, unlike the normal approximation \hat\pi \pm z\sqrt{\hat\pi(1-\hat\pi)/N_w}, stays inside [0, 1] and does not shrink to zero width when the observed rate is 0 or 100%, the rates that dominate this study at its two ends. The shortest length for a success rate of 95% (or 99%) is the smallest L of the grid from which the rate stays at or above that level for every longer length. Every window, key and tie-break comes from streams derived from the seed {{fig.params.seed}}.

5.4 Results

Figure 2 and Table 4 show the success rate of each method against the length of the ciphertext. All five methods break the cipher with near certainty from a few dozen letters: from 50 letters every rate is at least {{fig.caesar.chiSquare.L50.rate}}. They differ markedly on short texts. The bigram score reaches 95% from {{fig.caesar.bigram.l95}} letters and 99% from {{fig.caesar.bigram.l99}}, the trigram score from {{fig.caesar.trigram.l95}} and {{fig.caesar.trigram.l99}}; the two letter-frequency estimators need {{fig.caesar.logLikelihood.l95}} letters for 95%, and for 99% the likelihood needs {{fig.caesar.logLikelihood.l99}} and the \chi^2 distance {{fig.caesar.chiSquare.l99}}; the dictionary search reaches 95% from {{fig.caesar.exhaustive.l95}} letters and 99% from {{fig.caesar.exhaustive.l99}}. At five letters, the length of a single word, the bigram score is right {{fig.caesar.bigram.L5.rate}} of the time, the likelihood {{fig.caesar.logLikelihood.L5.rate}}, the \chi^2 distance {{fig.caesar.chiSquare.L5.rate}} and the dictionary {{fig.caesar.exhaustive.L5.rate}}.

The shortest ciphertexts show what each criterion can see. A single letter carries no n-gram and almost never a dictionary word, so the bigram and trigram scores tie on every key and fall to the chance level ({{fig.caesar.bigram.L1.rate}} and {{fig.caesar.trigram.L1.rate}}). The letter-frequency estimators, given one letter, choose the key that decrypts it to E, the most frequent letter of English, and succeed exactly when the plaintext letter was E: {{fig.caesar.chiSquare.L1.rate}}, close to the frequency of E in English text. A two-letter window contains a bigram only if it is a two-letter word, and a trigram never, so the trigram score stays at chance up to L = 2 ({{fig.caesar.trigram.L2.rate}}) and catches up with the bigram score from about ten letters. Figure 3 shows the same experiment through the mean rank of the true key: even where a method fails, the true key is usually near the top, with a mean rank of {{fig.caesar.trigram.L5.rank}} for the trigram score and {{fig.caesar.bigram.L5.rank}} for the bigram score at five letters, so that a short list of candidates would almost always contain it.

5.5 Effectiveness and limitations

The attack is effective because the cipher leaves the statistics of the plaintext intact and the key has very little to hide. A back-of-the-envelope count makes this precise. Under the unigram model of the training corpus a letter of English carries an entropy of {{fig.corpus.letterEntropy}} bits, against the \log_2 26 \approx 4.70 bits of a uniformly random letter; each letter of ciphertext therefore supplies about {{fig.corpus.redundancy}} bits of evidence about the shift, and the \log_2 26 bits of the key are exhausted after about {{fig.corpus.unicity}} letters. The letter-frequency estimators indeed become reliable from one to two dozen letters, while the n-gram scores, which also exploit the dependence between neighbouring letters, a much larger source of redundancy, need fewer. The limitations follow from the same count.

  • Very short texts. Below about ten letters no method is reliable, because the ciphertext does not contain enough information to single out one key; several keys then give plausible plaintexts and the estimator can only rank them (Figure 3). Short English words that are themselves shifts of other words make the ambiguity concrete: ADD and BEE differ by a shift of 1, CHEER and JOLLY by a shift of 7.
  • Unusual texts. The estimators assume that the plaintext follows the frequencies of the corpus. A lipogram written without the letter E, a list of proper names, a text full of abbreviations or a message in another language violates that assumption, and the letter-frequency methods, which rely on a handful of dominant letters, are the most exposed; the n-gram scores degrade more gracefully, because they rest on many weak constraints rather than a few strong ones. Digits and punctuation are not encrypted at all and leak directly.
  • Mismatch between corpus and target. The held-out novel differs significantly from the training average (\chi^2 = {{fig.compare.plainVsEnglish.statistic}}, {{fig.compare.plainVsEnglish.pEq}}, §4.3), yet the key is recovered with near certainty from a few dozen letters. Recovery does not require the plaintext to fit the model, only that the true decryption fit it better than the 25 wrong ones, a much weaker condition: the wrong decryptions are shifted profiles, far from any language. A model trained on modern technical prose or on another language would degrade the short-text rates, not the long-text ones.
  • Validity of the \chi^2 approximation. The asymptotic \chi^2_{25} law of Pearson's statistic requires expected counts of the order of five or more in every cell, which for the rarest letters means thousands of letters. In this attack the statistic is used only as a distance to be minimised, so its distribution is irrelevant to the choice of the key; but its p-values would be meaningless at the lengths that matter, and its division by tiny expected counts makes it oversensitive to a single rare letter, which is why it trails the likelihood below 50 letters.
  • Ties. A criterion that cannot separate the keys returns a tie, not an answer. The dictionary search suffers most: at short lengths most candidates contain no dictionary word, and its rate is the lowest of the five from 2 to 15 letters.
  • Cost. Cost is no limitation. One complete search of the 26 keys with the dictionary test takes about {{fig.caesar.exhaustiveMicroseconds}} µs on a 100-letter ciphertext, and the statistical criteria are cheaper still. With 26 keys the obstacle to exhaustive search is never the enumeration; it is recognising the right plaintext when it appears.

5.6 Comparison of the approaches

The optional comparison of exhaustive key search, letter-frequency analysis and n-gram scoring has a clear outcome on this corpus. N-gram scoring is the most efficient criterion, because it uses the dependence between adjacent letters, which the letter frequencies discard: there are {{fig.corpus.bigrams}} distinct bigrams in the training corpus against 26 letters, and a wrong shift produces improbable pairs almost at once. The bigram score is the best from two letters on, and the trigram score comes within a percentage point of it from about ten letters; the trigram model, with {{fig.corpus.trigrams}} distinct trigrams estimated from the same corpus, is sparser, needs words of at least three letters and leans more on the smoothing floor. Among the letter-frequency methods the likelihood, the optimal estimator under its own model, matches the \chi^2 distance on the shortest texts and is clearly better from about eight letters. The exhaustive search with a dictionary is the weakest at short lengths and as good as the others from about 50 letters: its criterion is binary at the level of a word, and it fails on texts whose words are rare. The comparison also shows that the three approaches are not alternatives so much as components: the key space is always searched exhaustively, and letter frequencies, n-grams and dictionaries are three ways of recognising the plaintext, of increasing precision per letter and decreasing robustness to unusual text. One caveat applies to every result of this section: the ciphertexts keep the spaces between words. A ciphertext stripped of spaces, as classical practice wrote it in groups of five letters, would remove the word boundaries on which the n-gram and dictionary criteria rely, a case this study does not measure.

§6 Attack on the restricted key space

6.1 Three searches of the key space

The attacker knows the curve, the base point G, the public key Q and the fact that k \in [1, 2^{b}], with b = B = 20 for the scheme of §2. The problem is the discrete logarithm of Q restricted to an interval of width w = 2^{b}. Its solution is unique: k \mapsto kG is injective on [1, n - 1] and 2^{b} < n. Three generic algorithms, which use nothing but the group operation and the comparison of points, were implemented.

Brute force. The walk P_1 = G, P_{j+1} = P_j + G visits G, 2G, 3G, \dots and stops when P_j = Q, which happens at j = k after k - 1 additions. For a uniform key the expected cost is (2^{b} - 1)/2 \approx 2^{b-1} additions, with constant memory. The implementation keeps P_j in Jacobian coordinates and compares it with Q projectively, which avoids a field inversion per step.

Baby-step giant-step. Shanks' method trades memory for time. With m = \lceil 2^{b/2} \rceil, every key in the interval can be written k = im + j with 0 \le i < m and 1 \le j \le m. The baby steps store the points G, 2G, \dots, mG in a hash table; the giant steps compute Q, Q - mG, Q - 2mG, \dots until one of them is found in the table, since

Q - i(mG) = jG \iff Q = (im + j)G \iff k = im + j .
(13)

The table costs m - 1 additions and the giant steps m/2 on average, about 1.5 \cdot 2^{b/2} operations in all, but the table holds m points: 2^{10} = 1024 for a 20-bit key.

Pollard's kangaroo. Pollard's lambda method [6] keeps the square-root time of baby-step giant-step with constant memory. A tame kangaroo starts at the known point 2^{b} G and makes jumps P \mapsto P + s_{h(P)} G, where the class h(P) of the current point, computed from its abscissa, selects one of a small set of jump sizes whose mean is about \sqrt{w}/2; after a fixed number of jumps it stops and sets a trap at its final point, of known logarithm 2^{b} + d_{\mathrm{tame}}, where d_{\mathrm{tame}} is the distance it has travelled. A wild kangaroo starts at Q and jumps by the same rule. Because each jump depends only on the current point, once the wild kangaroo lands on any point of the tame path it follows that path to the trap, and then

Q + d_{\mathrm{wild}}\,G = \big(2^{b} + d_{\mathrm{tame}}\big)\,G \quad \Longrightarrow \quad k = 2^{b} + d_{\mathrm{tame}} - d_{\mathrm{wild}} .
(14)

The expected cost is of the order of \sqrt{w} jumps, with memory for two points. The implementation runs the tame kangaroo for 4\sqrt{w} jumps and restarts with a new assignment of the jumps when the wild kangaroo overshoots the trap, which makes it reliable at the price of a larger constant.

Figure 4 measures the three attacks on {{fig.params.keysPerBits}} random keys of each size, up to {{fig.params.bruteMaxBits}} bits for brute force, {{fig.params.kangarooMaxBits}} for the kangaroo and {{fig.params.bsgsMaxBits}} for baby-step giant-step. The fitted slopes of \log_2 of the mean cost on b are {{fig.attack.brute.slope}} for brute force, {{fig.attack.bsgs.slope}} for baby-step giant-step and {{fig.attack.kangaroo.slope}} for the kangaroo, with correlations of {{fig.attack.brute.r}}, {{fig.attack.bsgs.r}} and {{fig.attack.kangaroo.r}}: the predicted exponents 1 and 1/2. The brute-force slope falls short of 1 by more than the others fall from 1/2 because its cost, k - 1, is itself uniform, and the mean of {{fig.params.keysPerBits}} uniform values has a relative standard deviation of about 20%; the square-root methods vary much less from key to key. The intercepts reflect the constants: baby-step giant-step is close to its 1.5 \cdot 2^{b/2}, the kangaroo, with its long tame walk and its restarts, spends several times more. In time the ranking is tempered by the cost per operation: the brute-force walk runs at {{fig.attack.brute.rate}} operations per second, baby-step giant-step, whose affine additions each need an inversion and a hash lookup, at {{fig.attack.bsgs.rate}}, and the kangaroo at {{fig.attack.kangaroo.rate}}.

6.2 Recovering the key of §2 and decrypting the message

Applied to the public key of §2, baby-step giant-step returns k = {{fig.demo.k}} after {{fig.demo.bsgsOperations}} group operations; brute force finds it after {{fig.demo.bruteOperations}} additions, one fewer than the key itself, and the kangaroo after {{fig.demo.kangarooOperations}} operations. The result is certain: the attacker checks that kG = Q, which holds for exactly one key. The message then follows by the receiver's own decryption of §2.3: S = kR, the tag is verified, and the keystream removed from c gives {{fig.demo.message}}. Once k is known, every message ever encrypted to Q, past or future, is readable in the same way: the scheme has no forward secrecy. 6.3 Decrypting without the private key

The assignment also allows an attack that decrypts the message otherwise. Recovering k is one way to obtain the shared point; recovering the ephemeral scalar is another, since S = rQ. With the full-range r of §2.2 this is as hard as the full ECDLP, but if the scheme were implemented with an ephemeral scalar restricted like the private key, the same searches would find r from R whatever the size of k. The study demonstrates this with a private key of 254 bits, far beyond any search, and an ephemeral scalar restricted to {{fig.demo.ephemeralBits}} bits: baby-step giant-step on R returns r = {{fig.demo.ephemeral}} after {{fig.demo.ephemeralOperations}} operations, and S = rQ decrypts the ciphertext to {{fig.demo.ephemeralMessage}}. This attack exploits a design error, not the restricted private key, and §7 classifies it accordingly.

6.4 Differences from the statistical attack on the Caesar cipher

  • What the attack uses. The Caesar attack uses the ciphertext and a statistical model of the language, and nothing else. The attack on the toy scheme uses neither: it needs only the public key Q and the base point G, both public before any message is sent, and the ciphertext is consulted only after the key has been found, to read the message. It works on messages in any language, on random bytes, and before the first message exists.
  • What is exploited. The Caesar attack exploits a leak: the cipher preserves the distribution of the plaintext (§4.3). The attack on the toy scheme exploits no leak at all; the ciphertext is statistically indistinguishable from random bytes, and the public key is a point of the curve like any other. It exploits only the smallness of the space in which the key was chosen.
  • The nature of the answer. The Caesar attack is statistical inference: it returns the most plausible key, which is wrong with a probability that depends on the length of the ciphertext and on how well the language model fits it (§5.4), and its answer can only be judged by reading the decryption. The attack on the toy scheme is a search verified by an exact algebraic check, kG = Q: it either finds the key, with certainty, or has not finished. Its running time is random, its answer is not.
  • How the cost scales. The cost of the Caesar attack does not grow with anything: the key space is fixed at 26. The cost of the attack on the toy scheme grows exponentially with the key size, as 2^{b/2} for the best of the three methods, and this growth is what a properly configured scheme relies on.

6.5 Why the attack does not scale to a full-size key

Table 5 extrapolates the fitted lines of Figure 4 to keys of 128 and 256 bits. A full secp256k1 key is uniform on [1, n - 1], so the interval is the whole group. Brute force would need about 2^{255} additions; baby-step giant-step about 2^{128} operations and a table of 2^{128} points, a memory requirement that alone rules it out; the kangaroo, by its theoretical cost, about 2^{129} operations. On the full group the best known method is Pollard's rho [6], which needs about \sqrt{\pi n/2} group operations with negligible memory, and \sqrt{\pi n/4} ≈ 2{{fig.attack.rho.log2}} when the negation map is used to identify each point with its negative [5, §4.1.2].

These numbers are not an artefact of slow code. At the rate of the brute-force walk measured here, Pollard's rho on secp256k1 would take {{fig.attack.rho.yearsMeasured}}; at {{fig.attack.fastRate}} operations per second, a rate far beyond any single machine, it would take {{fig.attack.rho.yearsFast}}, about {{fig.attack.rho.universeAges}} times the age of the universe of roughly 13.8 billion years. Parallelisation divides the time by the number of processors and no more [5, §4.1.2], so even a billion such machines leave the attack out of reach. Nor can a cleverer generic algorithm be expected: Shoup proved that any generic algorithm for the discrete logarithm in a group whose order has a largest prime factor p needs of the order of \sqrt{p} group operations [8], and the order of secp256k1 is itself a prime close to 2^{256}. Faster, non-generic attacks are known only for special classes of curves, which secp256k1 avoids [5, §4.1.4]. The gap between the toy scheme and the real one is therefore the gap between 2^{10} and 2^{128} operations, a factor of 2^{118}.

The assignment's wording, that the approach would not necessarily be feasible, is apt. A full-size key space removes the generic search, and nothing else: a properly sized key that is generated from a weak random source, reused across protocols or leaked through the implementation can still be recovered, by attacks that §7 separates from the ones above.

§7 Discussion

7.1 Weaknesses that arise from the restricted key space

The attacks of §6 depend on the restricted key space and on nothing else; they would apply unchanged to a flawless implementation of standard ECIES with a {{fig.params.toyBits}}-bit private key. Three further consequences of the restriction are worth stating.

  • Precomputation reusable across users. The baby-step table depends only on G and b, not on the victim. An attacker who stores all 2^{20} multiples jG, about {{fig.keySpace.toy.tableMB}} MB of compressed points, recovers the key of any user by one table lookup, and every user of the scheme falls at the cost of the first. A table of intermediate size trades storage for the giant steps that remain per user.
  • Collisions between users. With 2^{20} possible keys, the birthday bound gives an even chance that two of about {{fig.keySpace.toy.birthday}} independent users share a private key, and so can read each other's messages without any computation, as soon as they notice that their public keys are equal.
  • No forward secrecy. Every message sent to Q is protected by the same k; a key found by a search, today or years later, opens the whole history.

7.2 Weaknesses that arise from the design of the toy scheme

Other weaknesses would survive a full-size private key, because they belong to the construction of §2 rather than to the distribution of k.

  • A restricted or predictable ephemeral scalar. The security of each message rests as much on r as on k. §6.3 recovered a {{fig.demo.ephemeralBits}}-bit ephemeral from R in {{fig.demo.ephemeralOperations}} operations although the private key had 254 bits; an ephemeral drawn from a weak or seeded generator is just as fatal. The library draws r from the full range with the cryptographic generator of the host, but nothing in the ciphertext format protects against an implementation that does not.
  • A reused ephemeral scalar. Two messages encrypted with the same r share R, S and the keystream, so c_1 \oplus c_2 = m_1 \oplus m_2: the keystream cancels, and the two plaintexts can be separated with the language statistics of §5. Ephemeral reuse turns the toy scheme back into a problem of statistical cryptanalysis, the kind of attack its random keystream was meant to rule out.
  • An ad hoc integrity check. Without the tag, the scheme would be malleable: flipping a bit of c flips the same bit of the decrypted message, undetected. The tag of (4) detects such changes, but it is a hash of the secret and the data rather than a standard MAC with an independent key [1, §3.7], and its security is argued only informally. It confirms that the sender knew S, not who the sender was: anyone can encrypt to Q, so anyone can produce a ciphertext with a valid tag. Public-key encryption alone gives confidentiality, not authentication of the origin.
  • Key derivation without a standard function. The keystream and the tag are derived from the same input, the compressed shared point, separated only by the labels KS and TAG, by a construction specified on this page alone; standard ECIES uses a specified key derivation function and independent keys for encryption and authentication [1, §3.6, §5.1.3].
  • Point validation. A scheme that combined its private key with an unchecked received point would be exposed to invalid-curve and small-subgroup attacks, in which a crafted point reveals k modulo small numbers. The implementation is not exposed here: every point it receives, Q or R, is either decoded from its compressed form, which yields a point of secp256k1 or an error, or given by its coordinates; in both cases it is checked to lie on the curve, and the point at infinity is rejected. Because the cofactor of secp256k1 is 1, every other point of the curve has the prime order n, and these checks amount to the full public-key validation of SEC 1 [1, §3.2.2].
  • Side channels. The arithmetic is not constant-time: the double-and-add multiplication by k and r takes a time that depends on their bits, and the implementation makes no attempt to resist timing or power analysis [5, §5.3].
  • Length. The ciphertext has exactly the length of the message, which is therefore revealed; the Caesar cipher shares this property.

The distinction matters for the conclusion of §6. Enlarging the private key to its full 256 bits removes the weaknesses of §7.1 and none of those of §7.2; a real system needs both a full key space and a sound construction and implementation.

7.3 Conclusion

The Caesar cipher and the toy elliptic-curve scheme are both insecure, for complementary reasons. The Caesar cipher is a relabelling of the alphabet: it preserves the distribution of the plaintext, its index of coincidence exactly, and its 26 keys are recovered by statistical inference from the ciphertext alone. On ciphertexts from a held-out novel the bigram score identifies the key in 95% of cases from {{fig.caesar.bigram.l95}} letters, and every method studied does so from {{fig.caesar.chiSquare.l95}} letters; the limits of the attack are those of the information in short or unusual texts, not of computation. The toy scheme, hashed EC-ElGamal on secp256k1, produces ciphertexts indistinguishable from random bytes and is randomised, but its private key lies in a space of 2^{20} values; generic searches recover it from the public key alone, exactly and with a cost growing as 2^{b/2}, in {{fig.demo.bsgsOperations}} group operations by baby-step giant-step. The same searches on a full 256-bit key would need some 2^{128} operations, which no foreseeable computation can perform. Statistical properties of the ciphertext, the subject of the comparison, thus decide the fate of the classical cipher and say nothing about the modern one, whose security rests on the size of its key space and on the soundness of its construction.
